## Introduction

The lower leg, extending from the knee to the ankle, contains the tibia and fibula and the four muscular compartments that house the muscles of dorsiflexion, plantar flexion, inversion, and eversion of the foot. The surgical anatomy is dominated by the four compartments (anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, deep posterior), the relatively superficial position of the anteromedial tibial cortex with its limited soft-tissue coverage, and the regional vascular and neural structures (anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve anteriorly, posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve posteriorly, superficial peroneal nerve in the lateral compartment). The principal surgical approaches — anteromedial for the tibial shaft, posterolateral for the fibula, and the fasciotomy approaches for compartment syndrome — together provide access to the bony and neurovascular structures of the leg. This chapter, drawing on Orthopaedic Surgical Approaches, Netter's Concise Orthopaedic Anatomy, Gray's Anatomy, and the classical Bulgarian Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology of Boycho Boychev — the namesake of the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya — is supplemented with the classical procedures for the leg after Küntscher, Ilizarov, Wagner, Bogoraz, Chaklin, Brandt, Kirschner, Springer, Hadzhistamov, Ruy, Phemister, Papineau, Masquelet, Hahn–Huntington–Codivilla, Putti, Lexer, Hallock, Moore, Kocher, Boyd, Eyre-Brook, Sofield–Millar, Zhukhovitsky, Karchinov, Holevich, Boychev, Imamaliev, Zhelev, Zucman, Mubarak–Hargens, Harmon, Farabeuf, Burgess, Pirogoff, Yusevich, Frumin, and the Russo-Soviet school (Bogoraz, Ilizarov, Dzhanelidze, Vreden, Spizharni), which remain part of the Bulgarian orthopedic curriculum.

The tibia is the principal weight-bearing bone of the lower leg, with the anatomical features detailed in [Topic Trauma-27](/en/trauma/27/) (tibial shaft) and [Topic Trauma-28](/en/trauma/28/) (ankle): triangular cross-section with anteromedial subcutaneous surface, isthmus in mid-shaft, and characteristic anterior bow. The fibula is the slender lateral bone, articulating with the tibia at the proximal tibiofibular joint and at the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis, forming the lateral wall of the ankle mortise distally.

The leg compartments are four: Anterior compartment: Contains the tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus (EHL), extensor digitorum longus (EDL), and peroneus tertius. Functions: ankle dorsiflexion and toe extension. Innervated by the deep peroneal nerve. Vascular supply: anterior tibial artery. Lateral compartment: Contains the peroneus longus and peroneus brevis. Functions: foot eversion and assistance with plantar flexion. Innervated by the superficial peroneal nerve. Vascular supply: branches of the peroneal artery. Superficial posterior compartment: Contains the gastrocnemius, soleus, and plantaris. Functions: ankle plantar flexion. Innervated by the tibial nerve. Vascular supply: branches of the posterior tibial artery. Deep posterior compartment: Contains the tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus (FDL), and flexor hallucis longus (FHL). Functions: foot inversion, toe flexion, ankle plantar flexion. Innervated by the tibial nerve. Vascular supply: posterior tibial artery.

The compartments are separated by intermuscular septa attached to the bones and the interosseous membrane.

## Topography After the Classical Bulgarian School

The classical Bulgarian topographic school (Boychev) defines the regio cruris with the proximal border running anteriorly through the base of the tuberositas tibiae and posteriorly 7–8 cm distal to the inferior transverse fold of the popliteal fossa. The distal border is a circular line 7–8 cm proximal to the tip of the medial malleolus. The region divides into anterior (regio cruris anterior) and posterior (regio cruris posterior) parts; the dividing line runs medially along the margo medialis tibiae and laterally along the line connecting the posterior aspect of the caput fibulae with the posterior aspect of the malleolus lateralis.

In the anterior region the skin is relatively thin and mobile, thinning distally and medially; the subcutaneous fat layer is single-layered and poorly developed. The great saphenous vein (v. saphena magna) with n. saphenus, branches of n. cutaneus surae lateralis, and n. peroneus superficialis run in the subcutaneous tissue. The saphenous vein runs along the medial border of the tibia in its own fascial envelope; distally it lies anterior to the medial malleolus, while proximally it lies 2 cm posterior to the margo medialis. N. saphenus usually passes anterior to the vein and gives off sensory branches to the medial leg (rr. cutanei cruris medialis). N. peroneus superficialis pierces the deep fascia 10–12 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus, between m. peroneus brevis and m. extensor hallucis longus, and divides into two cutaneous branches.

The fascia cruris is thin and translucent; medially it fuses with the periosteum of the tibia. Two longitudinal intermuscular septa (anterior and posterior) divide the anterior region into anterior and lateral fascial compartments. The interosseous membrane is a dense connective-tissue partition with two constant openings: a proximal one at the level of the base of caput fibulae through which a. tibialis anterior and its venae comitantes pass to the anterior compartment, and a distal one 6–8 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus through which the perforating branch of a. peronea passes. The anterior intermuscular septum (septum intermusculare cruris anterius) attaches to the anterior border of the fibula and has a proximal opening for n. peroneus profundus and its muscle branches.

In the posterior region the fascia cruris has superficial and deep laminae, between which in the proximal half lies the fascial canal of Pirogov containing v. saphena parva with n. suralis; distally the vein passes deeper. At the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the leg, a narrow fascial cleft between the two laminae of the fascia cruris forms the distal exit of canalis cruropopliteus through which the deep posterior neurovascular bundle emerges into the subcutaneous tissue of the posteromedial surface.

## Canalis Cruropopliteus and Canalis Musculoperoneus

Canalis cruropopliteus is the canal containing the posterior neurovascular bundle of the leg (a. tibialis posterior with two venae comitantes, and n. tibialis). It lies in the medial region between superficial and deep muscles. Its anterior wall is formed proximally by m. tibialis posterior and m. flexor digitorum longus, distally by the latter and m. flexor hallucis longus; the posterior wall is m. soleus. The canal has three openings: one entrance and two exits. The entrance is a cleft between arcus tendineus m. solei and m. popliteus, through which the popliteal vessels and n. tibialis enter. The proximal exit is a triangular gap between the fibular neck (laterally), m. popliteus (above), and m. tibialis posterior (medially and below); through it a. tibialis anterior with its veins passes to the anterior compartment. The distal exit is a narrow fascial cleft between the two laminae of the fascia cruris at the junction of the middle and distal thirds.

Canalis musculoperoneus superior lies on the lateral surface of the fibula with an entrance along the lateral border of caput fibulae (3 cm distal to its tip), through which n. peroneus communis enters. The proximal exit is along the medial border of the fibula, 6–7 cm from the head, through which n. peroneus profundus emerges to enter the anterior compartment. The distal exit is on the anterolateral surface of the leg 10–12 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus, through which n. peroneus superficialis becomes subcutaneous. Canalis musculoperoneus inferior begins 5–7 cm from the origin of a. peronea and descends parallel and close to the fibula; the artery gives off its perforating branch 6–8 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus and a communicating branch at the level of the ankle.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The three canals of the leg by the classical Bulgarian school: canalis cruropopliteus (posterior neurovascular bundle between the superficial and deep laminae of fascia cruris, with three openings), canalis musculoperoneus superior (n. peroneus communis enters laterally at caput fibulae, n. peroneus profundus exits 6–7 cm distally into the anterior compartment, n. peroneus superficialis exits 10–12 cm proximal to the lateral malleolus tip), and canalis musculoperoneus inferior (a. peronea along the posterior fibula). ## Tibial Shaft Approaches

The anteromedial approach to the tibial shaft uses the subcutaneous medial surface of the tibia. An incision approximately 1 cm lateral to the anterior tibial crest provides access; the skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised, and the periosteum is opened to expose the bone. The approach is straightforward but has the disadvantage of a thin soft-tissue envelope over the tibia, with high rates of wound healing problems and infection. The approach is used for plate fixation of tibial shaft fractures (with caution about soft-tissue tension), biopsy, and bone grafting.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>The anteromedial tibia is subcutaneous; plate fixation here carries high rates of wound dehiscence and infection because of the thin soft-tissue envelope. Mind the open-fracture status, soft-tissue tension at closure, and prefer IM nailing or minimally invasive plating when the soft tissues are at risk.</p>
</aside> The anterolateral approach through the interval between the anterior compartment muscles and the lateral compartment provides access to the lateral aspect of the tibia and the upper fibula. The superficial peroneal nerve is encountered in the lateral compartment and must be protected.

The posterolateral approach through the interval between the lateral and posterior compartments provides access to the posterior aspect of the tibia and the fibula. The peroneal artery is at risk. The suprapatellar approach to tibial intramedullary nailing ([Topic Trauma-27](/en/trauma/27/)) has gained popularity for ease of fracture reduction and reduced anterior knee pain compared with the traditional infrapatellar approach.

## Classical Approaches to the Tibia in the Bulgarian School

The classical Bulgarian school describes four principal approaches to the tibia, each providing access to a specific bony surface with minimal neurovascular risk. The anterior approach is performed supine with the external landmark of margo anterior tibiae. The skin incision runs along the anteromedial surface to reach facies medialis tibiae, or curves laterally along the anterior crest to reach facies lateralis. Subcutaneous tissue and fascia cruris are incised, and the bone is exposed subperiosteally without risk to the neurovascular bundle. This is the conceptual basis of the modern anteromedial approach.

The medial approach to the tibia uses an incision along the margo medialis with subperiosteal exposure of the posterior surface; the posterior neurovascular bundle is protected by m. tibialis posterior and m. flexor digitorum longus. The approach is suited to biopsy, harvest of bone graft from the facies medialis, and fracture fixation with minimal soft-tissue insult.

The posterior approach to the proximal posterior surface of the tibia (after Boychev) is performed prone. The skin incision is L-shaped: the horizontal arm crosses the popliteal fossa along the lower transverse popliteal fold, then curves distally along the inner border of the medial head of m. gastrocnemius for 7–10 cm. Subcutaneous tissue and fascia cruris are opened while preserving v. saphena parva and n. cutaneus surae medialis. The interval between the terminal tendons of m. semitendinosus, m. semimembranosus, m. gracilis, and m. sartorius (medially) and the medial head of m. gastrocnemius (laterally) is developed; deep penetration exposes m. popliteus and the posterior surface of the proximal tibia. This approach is particularly useful for tumor biopsy in the proximal tibial metaphysis, drainage of deep posterior abscesses, and reconstruction of the posterior tibial plateau (Schatzker IV–V) with posterior plate fixation.

The posterolateral approach after Harmon (1945) to the shafts of both tibia and fibula is performed semi-prone on the healthy side or prone. The external landmark is the relief of the lateral head of m. gastrocnemius. The skin incision covers the middle two-thirds of the posterolateral surface along the lateral border of m. gastrocnemius. Fascia cruris is incised along the border of m. soleus; the interval between m. soleus and m. peroneus longus is identified, and depth is gained along the posterior surface of the posterior intermuscular septum along the fibula. M. soleus is retracted posteriorly, mm. peronei anteriorly; depth exposes the lateral border of m. flexor hallucis longus, whose medial retraction gives access to the posterior fibula and to the lateral border of m. tibialis posterior. The latter is carefully detached from the fibula, from the posterior surface of the interosseous membrane, and subperiosteally from the posterior surface of the tibia — exposing the posterior surfaces of both bones; if needed, the entire fibular shaft and the whole posterior surface of the tibia except the proximal quarter may be denuded. The common peroneal nerve and peroneal artery are at risk; the nerve lies immediately beneath the fascia behind the fibular head and often shows through, so fascial division here must be very cautious. The Harmon approach remains the classical choice for posterolateral bone grafting in tibial nonunion and for fibulo-tibial synostosis in pseudoarthrosis.

## Approaches to the Leg Neurovascular Bundles

The anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve in the upper third of the leg are reached through a 6–8 cm incision along the projection line connecting a point midway between caput fibulae and tuberositas tibiae with the midpoint of the bimalleolar line. After incising subcutaneous tissue and leg aponeurosis (1–2 cm lateral to the border of m. tibialis anterior), the interval between m. tibialis anterior and m. extensor digitorum longus is developed to the interstitial border, in the depths of which the neurovascular bundle lies on the interosseous membrane. In the distal half access is easier — between the tendon of m. tibialis anterior and the adjacent tendon of m. extensor hallucis longus. Ligation of the anterior tibial artery in the upper third is classically indicated for arterial bleeding of traumatic or iatrogenic origin and in preparation for vascular reconstruction.

The posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve project along a line connecting the midpoint of the popliteal folds with the midpoint of the distance between the posterior border of the medial malleolus and the medial border of the Achilles tendon. The approach is performed along the projection line at various levels depending on indications. In the proximal third (prone position) the incision lies between the two heads of m. gastrocnemius. After incising subcutaneous tissue, v. saphena parva is identified and retracted laterally or ligated; blunt depth gains the bundle before its passage under m. soleus. Arcus tendineus m. solei is differentiated and canalis cruropopliteus is opened longitudinally by splitting m. soleus. In the middle third (lateral position on the affected limb) the bundle is reached medially under m. soleus: fascia cruris is incised behind the medial border of the tibia, the medial border of m. soleus is exposed and detached from the posterior surface of the bone to the deep lamina of fascia cruris. This dissection is the standard for ligation of the posterior tibial artery at high amputation and for vascular reconstruction in arterial injury of the leg.

## Fibular Approaches

The lateral approach to the distal fibula is the standard for ORIF of ankle fractures ([Topic Trauma-28](/en/trauma/28/)). A longitudinal lateral incision over the fibula provides direct access. The superficial peroneal nerve crosses the operative field at the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the leg, becoming subcutaneous; it must be identified and protected. The lateral approach to the fibular shaft continues the same approach proximally. The fibula is exposed through subperiosteal dissection. The proximal fibular approach (for fibular head and neck pathology, including the proximal fibular fracture in the Maisonneuve variant of ankle fracture) uses a posterolateral incision behind the fibular head. The common peroneal nerve wraps around the fibular neck and is at substantial risk; its identification and protection are critical.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>On the lateral approach to the distal fibula, the superficial peroneal nerve crosses the field at the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the leg as it becomes subcutaneous — identify and protect or expect dorsal foot neuroma. On proximal fibular exposure, the common peroneal nerve wrapping the fibular neck is the most superficial nerve in the body at that point.</p>
</aside>

The leg is the most common site of compartment syndrome, and leg fasciotomy is one of the most commonly performed orthopedic emergency procedures. The historical standard since the 1970s is the double-incision technique of S. J. Mubarak and A. R. Hargens (1977), in which two longitudinal skin incisions (lateral and medial) provide complete four-compartment decompression. The alternative single-incision technique described by Davey and Rorabeck (1984) performs the full decompression through one extensive lateral incision passing posterior to the fibula to reach the deep posterior compartment; its indications are limited to cases in which the medial soft tissues are compromised (severe burns, infection, extensive prior surgery). In acute compartment syndrome the double-incision technique is universally preferred for more reliable visualization and certainty of complete decompression:

## Lateral Incision

A longitudinal incision along the lateral leg (approximately 2 cm anterior to the fibular shaft) provides access to the anterior and lateral compartments. The fascia of each compartment is opened along its length. The anterior compartment is released through the lateral aspect of the incision; the lateral compartment is released through the medial aspect. The superficial peroneal nerve crossing the operative field at the junction of the middle and distal thirds is identified and protected.

## Medial Incision

A longitudinal incision along the medial leg (approximately 2 cm posterior to the medial tibial border) provides access to the superficial and deep posterior compartments. The fascia of the superficial posterior compartment is opened. The soleus is detached from the medial tibia to provide access to the deep posterior compartment, and its fascia is opened. The saphenous vein and nerve along the medial leg are identified and protected.


## Post-Fasciotomy Management

The wounds are left open with sterile dressings (or VAC therapy), with delayed primary closure or skin grafting at 5 to 10 days as discussed in [Topic Trauma-32](/en/trauma/32/). In severely contaminated injuries, the classical Bulgarian approach inherited from the Russo-Soviet military-orthopaedic tradition (Belarmin, Vreden) prescribes copious irrigation with saline and Vishnevsky balsam, debridement to viable tissue, and late secondary closure or grafting after complete resolution of the edema syndrome.

## Chronic Exertional Compartment Syndrome

Chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) is a distinct entity from the acute form — it typically affects runners and athletes, presenting with exercise-dependent pain and paresthesia that resolve at rest in 15–30 minutes, and is diagnosed by intracompartment pressures measured before, during, and after exercise (rest pressure rise >30 mmHg or >20 mmHg at 1 minute after exercise). The classical operative procedure is selective fasciotomy of the affected compartment (most often the anterior and lateral, less often the deep posterior) through a limited longitudinal incision with a long-handle fasciotomy instrument — unlike acute compartment syndrome, in which all four compartments are mandatorily released. Skin incisions are short (3–5 cm); the fascia is divided along the entire length of the compartment under visual control; the superficial peroneal nerve in the distal third of the lateral incision must be identified and protected. The wounds are closed primarily because the edema is not massive; return to sport is possible at 4–6 weeks.

## Approach to the Tibial Nerve and Posterior Tibial Artery

The tibial nerve and posterior tibial artery in the leg are accessed through a posteromedial approach. A longitudinal incision along the posteromedial leg provides access; the fascia is opened, and the soleus is reflected to expose the nerve and artery in the deep posterior compartment. The approach is used for vascular injury repair, decompression of the tibial nerve, and selected exposure of the deep posterior compartment. The tibial nerve at the ankle in the tarsal tunnel is approached through a curved incision posterior to the medial malleolus. The flexor retinaculum is incised, decompressing the nerve in the tarsal tunnel (tarsal tunnel release for tarsal tunnel syndrome).

## Approach to the Common Peroneal Nerve

The common peroneal nerve around the fibular neck is approached through a longitudinal incision posterior to the fibular head, providing access to the nerve as it wraps around the bone. The approach is used for common peroneal nerve decompression, exploration of nerve injury, and operative management of peroneal nerve palsy. The nerve is identified and traced from the popliteal fossa around the fibular neck and into its bifurcation into the deep and superficial peroneal branches.

## Soft-Tissue Operations on the Leg

The classical Bulgarian school describes several characteristic soft-tissue operations in the region that remain clinically relevant today. For a circular subcutaneous constriction (fibrous ring with intact skin), the operation is performed in two stages, each removing half of the circular furrow through a double or multiple Z-shaped incision with 45° angles. The exposed fibrous ring is excised and the triangular flaps are transposed and sutured — a classical Z-plasty after Limberg.

For elephantiasis of the leg (chronic lymphedema with subcutaneous fibrosis) staged resection is performed in four stages. Through an S-shaped incision along the entire length of the anteromedial side of the leg the skin is undermined on both sides covering ¼ of the circumference, the thickened subcutaneous tissue is excised, and a parallel strip is excised from the fascia cruris; the wound is closed in layers. The second stage performs the same procedure on the opposite side. A combined extension to the foot adds a stage on the ankle and dorsum pedis. The alternative free-skin variant — separating a skin-subcutaneous layer, harvesting a dermatome graft 0.6–0.8 mm thick, perforating it, and placing it directly on the fascia cruris with the original skin returned over it — combines excision and revascularization of the subcutaneous layer.

For limited deep non-healing wounds with exposed bone the classical cross-leg flap is performed in three stages. The first involves biological training of a pedicled cutaneous flap from the opposite leg with a base twice the width of its free end; the flap is raised and a sterile rubber sheet is placed beneath, periodically removed to stimulate vascularization. The second stage transposes the flap to the prepared defect with plaster immobilization of the crossed limbs for one month. The third — separation of the limbs at 1–1.5 months. This procedure has been largely replaced by free flaps with microvascular anastomosis but remains clinically relevant where microsurgery is unavailable and in elderly patients with limited vascular reserves.

Plastic coverage of exposed tibia with a fascial flap after Holevich is described for osteoplastic and reconstructive operations on the leg (especially after gunshot wounds where periosteum is absent or sclerotic). A curved skin incision opening laterally is made 1–2 cm from the margo anterior tibiae; the periosteum is incised 0.5 cm medial to the anterior crest; the bone is worked (osteotomy or graft placement after Chaklin, Khakhutov, Dzhanelidze, or wedging), and a rectangular flap is harvested from the fascia cruris with a broad pedicle from the margo medialis. The flap is turned over the bone and sutured to the periosteal remnant edge-to-edge or as a duplication, isolating the graft. This elegant principle of local fascial coverage providing bone isolation and vascularization conceptually precedes the modern local muscle rotation flaps (m. gastrocnemius for the proximal third, m. soleus for the middle, m. flexor hallucis longus or free flaps for the distal third of tibia, after Hidalgo and Mathes).

## Osteotomies of the Leg

Classical osteotomies of the leg are applied for deformities of rachitic, post-traumatic, or epiphyseal origin. The choice of technique depends on the degree and level of deformity, the patient's age, and the need for simultaneous lengthening.

The linear transverse osteotomy (e.g., subperiosteal section at the level of the greatest curvature with full anterior cortical division and incomplete posterior, the latter left attached to periosteum as a hinge) is the basis of the classical "ad casullo" ("in cast") osteotomy: after complete anterior division two wedges are removed with their base anterior, their length pre-calculated for full correction, one from the lateral and the other from the medial side of the osteotomy.

Double osteotomy after Kirschner is performed by sectioning the tibia at two adjacent points in the zone of greatest curvature with oblique angles — the upper directed anteroposteriorly and upward, the lower anteroposteriorly and downward; after osteotomy the segments are aligned and immobilized in plaster.

Cross-shaped osteotomy after Brandt is suitable for double curvatures: after subperiosteal exposure an X-shaped osteotomy is performed, permitting multiplanar correction. The chip osteotomy after Kirschner (epiperiosteal) is performed with a sharp chisel splitting the bone longitudinally along the greatest curvature and creating wedge-shaped "chips"; their position and compression allow correction in all directions, with the osteotomized parts remaining in continuous contact, allowing lengthening through direct traction. The transverse segmental osteotomy after Springer uses a wide anterior incision with full denuding of a large tibial segment followed by transverse removal — abandoned for its large denuded area and lack of advantage over Bogoraz. The segmental oblique osteotomy after V. N. Bogoraz (1948) — performed at the greatest curvature with oblique direction allowing rotation and lengthening — remains the historical reference for modern multiplanar corrections with external fixation.

The bone-plastic osteotomy after V. D. Chaklin uses the convex bone surface as donor of a longitudinal periosteal-cortical sliver excised with chisel or oscillating saw and preserved in saline during correction; the small fibula is osteotomized obliquely subperiosteally, a subperiosteal chevron osteotomy of tibia is performed, correction is achieved, and the preserved sliver is replaced inverted — combining correction with autogenous bone material to reinforce consolidation. Hadzhistamov's technique excises a thick bone piece from the crista anterior tibiae together with the facies medialis, beginning at tuberositas tibiae distally; fibula is sectioned stepwise in the curve's apex, the excised bone piece is shaped and placed in a new trough in the proximal tibia, with the fibular defect filled with cancellous chips. Ruy's technique uses a longitudinal incision over the most convex part of tibia with slight lateral curve and creates a thin adherent bone layer with periosteum through longitudinal stripping — followed by 2–3 transverse osteotomies and replacement of the adherent lever for stabilization.

Segmental rotation osteotomy with 180° rotation of the segment is performed through two incisions — an anterior slightly curved and a posterior for fibula — with pre-sterilized segment templates that allow precise marking of osteotomy lines under soft-tissue protection. The technique is particularly elegant for rotational deformities without significant curvature.

Derotation osteotomy of the tibia (for rotational deformities of paralytic, spastic, metabolic, or post-traumatic origin, in coxa vara congenita, pes varus, pes valgus) is performed through a 3 cm longitudinal incision over the crista tibiae just below tuberositas tibiae; the bone is freed from soft tissues and sectioned transversely with an electric saw or osteotome. The fibula is fractured by placing the distal part in strong varus or valgus — an assistant holds the knee fixed while the operator rotates the distal part. Fixation is with plate and screws or with plaster from the inguinal fold to the toes at 5° knee flexion for 6 weeks.

## Lengthening and Shortening of the Leg

Lengthening of the leg by external fixation is the classical Bulgarian-school technique. An external incision allows oblique osteotomy of fibula, while through an anterior incision a Z-shaped osteotomy of tibia is performed. Each fragment is anchored with two pins placed perpendicular to the long axis of tibia from the margo medialis side, before disruption of the bony continuity — a necessary condition for proper pin centering in the three spatial planes and for the subsequent direction of lengthening. If angular correction is required, pins are placed perpendicular to the fragments; on bone straightening they become parallel.

Decortication osteoclasis to disrupt tibial continuity while preserving soft tissues is performed through an anterolateral 7–8 cm incision parallel and 2–3 cm lateral to the crista tibiae. After fascial division m. tibialis anterior is retracted laterally, facies lateralis tibiae is exposed, and periosteum is incised without denuding. Controlled fracture (decortication) of the lateral surface with small chips without disrupting periosteal supply preserves bone biology and provides a barrier of cancellous chips between bone ends that activates osteogenesis. The external fixator is mounted with prepared halves, pins are tensioned, and the fragments are slightly distracted (0.5–1 cm).

Lengthening through direct traction on the heel is the alternative classical technique when no compression-distraction apparatus is available.

The Ilizarov circular apparatus (G. A. Ilizarov, Kurgan, 1951–1971) applied to the leg with a classical mount of two half-rings (¾ rings) on the proximal metaphysis and two full rings on the distal metaphysis provides stable axial fixation and controlled distraction. The daily distraction is 1 mm in four steps of ¼ mm — a regime under which the bone regenerate develops optimally. After the desired length is achieved the apparatus is maintained until full regenerate consolidation (approximately 1 month per centimeter of lengthening), followed if needed by plaster. The Ilizarov principle of "tension-stress distraction osteogenesis" remains fundamental and is the conceptual basis of later internal lengthening devices (Albizzia, ISKD, PRECICE).

Distraction epiphysiolysis in children is performed by passing two thick Kirschner wires through the proximal tibial epiphysis and distal metaphysis, anchored to a distraction apparatus. Before pin passage the skin is shifted distally in the proximal segment and proximally in the distal — to provide for subsequent lengthening. An initial dynamic force is applied with the distractor (individual for each patient) to achieve epiphysiolysis; distraction begins on day 2–3 after pin insertion and must not exceed 1 mm per day. The method is largely abandoned in favor of modern hemiepiphyseodesis and internal lengthening systems.

Shortening of the tibia (for significant length discrepancy after consolidation of a malunion) is performed through a longitudinal slightly curved 15–18 cm incision along the anteromedial side. Periosteum is incised and approximately 8–10 cm of bone is exposed with raspatorium. In the middle of the tibia a transverse osteotomy is performed with a Gigli saw or electric circular saw; from the inner half of the proximal fragment as many centimeters are removed as the desired shortening (e.g., for 1 cm resection). The same is done in the distal fragment; the two semicircular resection planes are approximated and fixed with a Spizharni–Küntscher nail through the tibial plateau medial to the patella. An alternative is a transverse osteotomy with a second parallel osteotomy at the distance of the desired shortening, with fibula osteotomized at the same level and cancellous chips packed around the osteotomy. Postoperatively a hip-spica plaster is applied with the knee flexed and slight plantar flexion for 8 weeks.

## Osteomyelitis — Classical Operative Principles

The classical Bulgarian and Russian school describes a systematic approach to tibial osteomyelitis that remains the basis of contemporary management.

For acute hematogenous osteomyelitis of the tibia a curved longitudinal 8–10 cm incision is made along the anteromedial surface centered on the affected area. Fascia cruris is divided to reach periosteum, which may be "bombed" by subperiosteal collection — its incision releases pus under pressure; if periosteum is undisturbed it is incised longitudinally and the bone is denuded carefully, economically, and atraumatically to preserve periosteal blood supply. Multiple drill or burr trephinations (mini-fenestrations) of the cortex drain the intramedullary abscess, relieve pressure, and allow placement of irrigation drains; the wound is left open with dressings and systemic antibiotic therapy by antibiogram (empirically — broad spectrum against Staphylococcus aureus including MRSA strains).

For chronic osteomyelitis with sequestra a necrectomy is performed through a longitudinal incision over the anterior surface of tibia exposing as many fistulae as possible. The periosteum (usually thickened) is incised longitudinally and stripped 1.5–2.5 cm on each side. The bone is trephined with chisel or electric burr along the osteomyelitic focus and curetted to healthy bleeding cancellous bone. All sequestra are removed, fistulae excised or marked for secondary closure; the cavity is irrigated with saline and a local antibiotic per antibiogram is placed.

Muscle plombage of a large bone cavity — first described by Schulten in 1896 for the tibia — is performed with m. tibialis anterior, m. gastrocnemius, m. peroneus longus, or m. flexor hallucis longus depending on location. For proximal and middle cavities m. tibialis anterior and m. flexor hallucis longus are typically used (with proximal insertion and preserved blood supply and innervation after distal division). For parietal cavities the muscle body is used without dividing its insertion; for full cylindrical cavities — distally divided flaps. The muscle flap tip is anchored to the cavity floor by a suture passed through a small opening made with an electroburr, fixed externally on the skin with a gauze roll.

Cross-leg muscle plombage (for distal leg with insufficient local muscle mass) uses muscles from the opposite limb — most commonly m. sartorius or the medial head of m. gastrocnemius — together with a cutaneous flap. The two limbs are crossed and immobilized in plaster for 4 weeks; separation is at 45 days. Plombage with the Filatov tube (V. P. Filatov) — for cavities in areas poor in surrounding muscle (knee, ankle regions) — is a staged procedure forming a tubular flap typically from the abdomen or chest wall, transferring it to the leg via the arm, and finally inserting it into the bone cavity.

The classical single-stage cancellous bone graft after Phemister (D. B. Phemister, 1947) is applied to tibial nonunion with intact soft-tissue cover: a posterolateral approach between mm. peronei and m. soleus exposes the lateral surface of tibia and fibula; both bones are decorticated and an interfibulotibial onlay corticocancellous graft from crista iliaca is placed across both main fragments. Results in chronic nonunion are consolidation in 80–90%. Boyd's dual onlay (H. B. Boyd) uses two solid corticocancellous grafts from the opposite tibia placed anteriorly and posteriorly on the affected bone and fixed with screws — indicated for severe tibial defects.

Open cancellous grafting after Papineau (J.-P. Papineau, 1973, Canada) is applied to infected nonunion with large bone defect and inability of primary soft-tissue closure. The procedure is performed in three stages: radical debridement of the infected focus to viable bone; open cancellous grafting with autologous bone from crista iliaca into the defect; and tertiary — granulation with secondary closure or skin grafting over the cancellous bed. The induced-membrane technique after Masquelet (A. C. Masquelet, 1986) is the modern analogue: in the first stage after debridement the defect is filled with PMMA bone cement around which a biologically active membrane with pro-angiogenic and pro-osteogenic properties develops over 6–8 weeks; in the second stage the cement is carefully removed without damaging the membrane, and autologous cancellous bone (typically from crista iliaca or harvested with RIA from the femoral canal) is placed in the new biological capsule. The Masquelet technique remains the principal modern method for defects up to 8–10 cm.

Congenital pseudoarthrosis of the tibia (typically associated with neurofibromatosis type 1) is one of the most complex problems in pediatric orthopaedics — union in no other condition is so difficult to achieve. In very early childhood operative results are poorer, while with advancing age severe deformities develop (leg curvature, pes calcaneus, shortening). Classical Bulgarian literature describes three categories of operations: with pedicled grafts, with free grafts, and combined.

The Hahn–Huntington–Codivilla method (conceptually from V. Hahn in the 1880s, developed by T. W. Huntington 1905 and A. Codivilla 1911) uses the fibula in place of the tibia ("fibula pro tibia"). After dividing fibula in its lower and upper parts it is osteosynthesized with the remaining parts of the tibia; the operation may be performed in two stages (sequential upper and lower insertion) or in one. After insertion fibula assumes tibial function and hypertrophies compensatorily. Classical indications are large pseudoarthroses of tibia — congenital or acquired — with defects greater than 4–5 cm.

Putti's technique (V. Putti) is based on the biomechanical principle of closing the posteriorly open angle of the false joint. The fibula of the same leg is used (often itself pseudoarthrotic); a long segment is harvested, freshened at both ends, and placed posteriorly across the false joint so the angle is converted into a triangle. The force lines transfer to the posterior arm of the triangle, the graft hypertrophies, and the false joint consolidates, freed of stress. After stabilization the anterior pseudoarthrotic segment is excised and the deformity corrected.

The Lexer technique (E. Lexer) places free bone grafts around the freshened false joint, while the techniques of Froelich and Codivilla add fibular osteotomy (if healthy) with multiple massive grafts for stabilization. The Hallock technique uses cancellous chips for tiered osteosynthesis after freshening the pseudoarthrotic area. Phemister, Henderson, Colonna, Delahye, and Compère place massive grafts spanning the false joint without freshening, fixed with screws. Moore inserts the bone graft into the medullary canal with external longitudinal compression; Boyd fills the pseudoarthrotic space with cancellous chips. Kocher uses two solid bone grafts plus an intramedullary Küntscher nail. Eyre-Brook takes a free graft from the healthy tibia and drives it into the proximal and distal segments of the pseudoarthrotic tibia from the concave side.

The combined method of Paus combines a free bone graft with a pedicled graft, fibula pro tibia after Hahn–Huntington–Codivilla, and intramedullary metallic osteosynthesis. It is performed in four stages: intramedullary metallic osteosynthesis with a Küntscher or Schmann nail in the proximal fragment sliding into the distal (with the fragments left apart); exposure of the proximal fibula with protection of n. peroneus communis and osteotomy with transfer of the upper end into a tibial opening; analogous distal insertion; and if needed additional free bone grafting. Results vary — the operation succeeds in 55–60%, with late tibial fracture in a substantial proportion.

V. Y. Zhukhovitsky's method for osteoplastic reconstruction of large tibial defects requires preserved soft tissues and good ankle function. Through a curved skin incision over the anterior tibia the defect is exposed and freshened, followed by exchange of semicylindrical bone autografts shaped from the two segments of the pseudoarthrotic tibia.

The classical Sofield–Millar technique (F. A. Sofield and E. A. Millar) of multiple fragmentation and nailing of long bones in osteogenesis imperfecta — particularly applicable to tibia with multiple deformities and fractures — is performed by multiple osteotomies of the affected bone, threading the segments in proper alignment, and intramedullary nailing with a growing or fixed nail. In osteogenesis imperfecta with telescoping rods (Bailey-Dubow, later Fassier-Duval) the growth problem is solved without need for repeat exchange.

Reconstruction of tibial defects with fibula ("fibula pro tibia") has the largest body of advocates in the classical Bulgarian and Soviet literature. Numerous variants have been developed, including transfer of an entire fibular shaft, osteosynthesis with screws or nails, and combination with cancellous autografts. The Viennese variant uses rotation of fibular segments, while the Russian school adds two-stage implantation (upper and lower) with an integration interval.

Plastic replacement of the entire tibia with fibula after Putti is applied for aplasia tibiae congenita, after total extirpation of the tibia for tumors, and for very wide bone defects with only tibial poles preserved. The operation is in two stages. In the first — a lateral fishing-line skin incision from the lower middle thigh through caput fibulae to the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the leg. Fascia lata is divided, m. biceps femoris and the underlying n. peroneus communis are identified (mandatory protection). The muscle is detached from caput fibulae which is freed circumferentially; the tibia (or its poles) is excised subperiosteally. After abduction and flexion of the leg, caput fibulae is displaced into fossa intercondylaris femoris with soft-tissue interposition. Two variants are possible: creation of a new femuro-fibular joint, or arthrodesis of fibula with the lateral femoral condyle. The foot is immobilized in maximal equinus so weight is borne on the metatarsal heads. After 6 months the plaster is replaced by a special orthopaedic apparatus. In the second stage (no earlier than 1 year after the first) the distal end of fibula is arthrodesed with talus or (in its absence) calcaneus through an anterolateral 7 cm incision.

K. Karchinov (1974) for bilateral complete absence of tibia describes a "support osteotomy of the fibulae": osteotomy at the border between upper and middle third of fibulae with an angle opening laterally and posteriorly, so that the distal femoral end rests on the fibulae. With a preserved distal tibial part (intact ankle mortise) the second stage osteotomizes fibula and fixes it to the freshened distal tibial segment with wire loops or screws. With a preserved proximal tibial part (with present knee joint) the first stage unites the available proximal tibia with the upper fibula.

For congenital hypoplasia of the tibia with proximal tibial epiphysis Karchinov (1975) describes the following: a lateral incision exposes the proximal third of fibula, which is denuded; a transverse osteotomy is performed at the metaphysio-diaphyseal junction. A second incision is along the lateral aspect of tuberositas tibiae; the available tibia is exposed and a bone bed is fashioned in its lateral surface at the tuberositas level, into which fibula is implanted with periosteum preserved — a Y-shaped reconstruction of the proximal end.

For absent fibula without other deformity (retroposition of the fibular malleolus or distal hypoplastic fibula) osteoplastic reconstruction of the fibular malleolus after Garrett is performed using the fibular head as donor: a lateral longitudinal incision over the proximal quarter of fibula, exposure between the peroneal muscles and caput fibulare m. gastrocnemii distally, and resection of the proximal third. The graft is placed at the missing distal fibular position with the head as a new lateral malleolus; if it sits well it is fixed with 1–2 screws to the freshened tibia.

## Osteosynthesis of Diaphyseal Fractures of the Leg

Fresh leg fractures are treated by osteosynthesis when closed reduction fails or cannot be held. Usually only tibia is fixed; fibula is fractured or osteotomized when needed to reduce its rotational stability and ensure interfragmentary contact. Non-metallic methods (bone graft, catgut or tendon sutures) have historical value; the standard is metallic osteosynthesis.

Classical plate osteosynthesis of the tibia (for transverse and slightly oblique fractures with intact soft tissues) is performed through a 12–15 cm incision slightly lateral to the crista tibiae, mildly convex laterally, centered on the fracture. After longitudinal division of fascia cruris and periosteum the entire anterior muscle group is stripped from tibia and retracted laterally. Fragment ends are exposed as economically as possible; Lambotte or Lowmann bone forceps hold the reduction. Fixation is by several methods: wire cerclage (at least two cerclages several cm apart — suited only to oblique fractures); Putti–Parnham metal band fixation (abandoned for compromising bone vascularity); fixation with several screws for oblique fractures (minimum two screws engaging both cortices spaced several cm apart); and plate-and-screw fixation for transverse fractures (which have the highest nonunion rate due to osteolysis at fragment ends). For transverse tibial fractures with intact fibula the classical recommendation is oblique fibular osteotomy several cm proximal or distal to the tibial fracture level through a small lateral incision between m. soleus and the peroneal musculature — to allow tight interfragmentary contact of tibia.

Intramedullary osteosynthesis of the tibia after Spizharni–Küntscher (G. Küntscher, Germany 1939; V. M. Spizharni, USSR) is the standard for transverse and slightly oblique fractures with displacement, false joints, and segmental osteotomy. Both closed and open techniques are used. In closed nailing the leg is reduced on a Böhler apparatus or orthopedic operating table with intraoperative fluoroscopy. A 5 cm incision is made around tuberositas tibiae along its medial border; the bone is drilled and through the trephination hole a guide wire is advanced toward the fracture. After fluoroscopic confirmation of fragment position the wire is driven into the peripheral fragment, the Küntscher nail of pre-measured length and width is advanced over it, and the fracture is stabilized. Open nailing is indicated for irreducible transverse and slightly oblique fractures. The operation is performed in two stages: open reduction through a semilunar 10 cm incision over the fracture (subperiosteal exposure of bone ends, reduction with levers, bone hooks, or Hass retractors), followed by osteosynthesis through a small incision around tuberositas tibiae with guide wire advancement along the diaphysis and nail driving.

The Zucman method (France, 1965) — derived from the German-Soviet school — introduces intramedullary tibial osteosynthesis with a straight Küntscher femoral nail introduced through the tibial plateau medial to the patella. This produces robust osteosynthesis with possible early ambulation. Zhelev and colleagues (Bulgaria, 1975) refined this method by developing a positioning stand, a nail length-measuring device, and a ruler for medullary canal width. The Zucman method is the conceptual predecessor of modern locked tibial nails (with proximal and distal interlocking screws), introduced into standard practice from the 1980s and now dominating treatment of tibial diaphyseal fractures.

Extrafocal compression osteosynthesis of the tibia (Ilizarov and successors) uses a circular compression-distraction apparatus for stabilization without opening the fracture zone — particularly valuable in open Gustilo–Anderson III B and C fractures, infected nonunion, and bone transport in defect segments. Bone transport after Ilizarov for tibial defect is performed by intermediate corticotomy and gradual segment displacement in the distal direction at 1 mm/day (4 × ¼ mm); consolidation develops on the regenerate side, while a compression docking procedure is performed on the other. This is a principal strategy for defects 4–12 cm — an alternative to Masquelet and vascularized fibula from the opposite limb.

For younger patients with tumor lesions in the proximal third of tibia requiring wide resection, Karchinov applies the following technique: caput fibulae is freshened and implanted into the lateral femoral condyle, fixed with Kirschner wires; a solid bone graft (preferably from the opposite crista iliaca) is placed for reconstruction; the wound is closed in layers and the limb is immobilized for 3–4 months. Two-stage fibular implantation (in the upper, then the lower part of tibia) gives good functional results in selected cases.

## Open Fracture Management — The Bulgarian and Russian Military Tradition

The classical Bulgarian and Russian military-orthopaedic tradition (inherited from Belarmin, R. R. Vreden, V. A. Oppel, and Yu. Yu. Dzhanelidze) formulates the principles of open fracture management that remain valid in contemporary practice:

1. Early debridement (within the first 6–8 hours) to viable tissue with removal of all free bone fragments lacking periosteal blood supply and copious saline lavage;
2. Stable internal or external fixation — in Gustilo–Anderson type I–II intramedullary osteosynthesis is acceptable, in type III A–B and C external fixation is mandatory (Ilizarov preferred for III B–C);
3. Soft-tissue coverage within the first 5–7 days — when primary closure is impossible a local muscle flap is used (m. gastrocnemius for the proximal third, m. soleus for the middle, free microvascular flap for the distal);
4. Systemic broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy with gram-positive and gram-negative coverage; for serious contamination, addition of anaerobic coverage (penicillin G against Clostridium);
5. Tetanus prophylaxis with tetanus toxoid and, if needed, hyperimmune globulin;
6. The classical local application of Vishnevsky balsam (a combination of castor oil, xeroform, and birch tar) — widely used in Soviet military medicine — remains part of the Bulgarian tradition for chronic and poorly healing wounds, although in modern practice it has been largely replaced by modern negative-pressure dressings (VAC).

## Amputations of the Leg

Leg amputations are the most common amputations in the human body; indications are vascular (Buerger's thromboangiitis, senile gangrene from atherosclerosis, acute arterial occlusion with irreversible ischemia), severe infections, frostbite, trauma, and tumors. The leg may be amputated at any level, but the longer the stump lever, the better the transmission of motion to a prosthesis. The classical recommendation is an optimal level 12–15 cm below the knee joint; distal-third amputations make prosthetic fitting more challenging because of the short stump-shoe ratio.

Amputation with a small anterior and large posterior flap (the standard modern technique, developed by E. M. Burgess in 1968) is performed supine under Esmarch hemostasis. Two incisions are made: one along the fibula, the other along the medial border of the tibia. The midpoints of these incisions are connected anteriorly and their distal ends posteriorly. After dividing soft tissues two musculocutaneous flaps are produced — a small anterior and a large posterior containing m. gastrocnemius. Bones are sectioned at the base of the skin flaps: the anterior tibial crest is rounded (to avoid a painful bone edge against the prosthesis), fibula is divided 1–1.5 cm higher. Vessels are ligated; nerves are divided 2–3 cm higher with slight traction to retract into soft tissues (prophylaxis of painful neuroma of n. tibialis and n. peroneus). The two musculocutaneous flaps are sutured to each other; the large posterior flap with m. gastrocnemius forms a biomechanically tolerant soft surface of the stump for distal loading. A drain for 48 hours is mandatory. The principle of "myoplastic closure with the posterior muscle turned forward" remains the modern standard for distal stump coverage.

Amputation with an elliptical skin incision is similar to the circular technique but with the incision angled anteriorly or posteriorly, producing a musculocutaneous flap in the low part of the ellipse. The circular classical (open wartime) amputation uses transverse division of soft tissues and bones higher up; the wound is left open or closed with a few sutures, with a drain for two days. This is applicable for open wartime wounds and gas gangrene, where primary closure is contraindicated.

Amputation with a large anterior and small posterior flap (anteroplantar) is performed when posterior soft tissues are compromised but the lateral muscle mass and skin are healthy. The skin flap is fashioned by a sagittal 10 cm incision along the anterior tibial crest, extending posteriorly along the leg.

Lateral amputation (with a lateral flap) is applicable in severe injury of the medial or lateral soft tissues that precludes anterior-posterior flap formation.

The racquet method of Farabeuf (P.-J. Farabeuf) for amputation in the upper third of the leg uses a racquet-shaped skin incision beginning at tuberositas tibiae, curving distally on one side, crossing posteriorly 4–5 cm lower, and curving from the other side back to the starting point. Skin and muscle are divided at one level, bone slightly above the racquet apex. Vessels are ligated, nerves divided higher, and skin is closed with a few sutures. This method produces a well-formed anterior edge of the amputation stump, particularly suited to high proximal amputations.

The classical Pirogoff amputation (N. I. Pirogov, 1854) — though anatomically located in the ankle region (Topic Anatomy-11) — is the conceptual predecessor of leg myoplastic amputations: the distal calcaneus (or its posterior half) is preserved and turned to cover the distal tibial resection surface for weight-bearing. The historical importance of this operation is enormous — it introduces the principle of "living weight-bearing surface" in distal amputation. The osteoplastic amputation after Yu. Yu. Dzhanelidze (1953) is the Russian variant with use of a periosteal bone cap (osteoplastic coverage).

## Closure of Amputation Stumps and Reamputation

For problematic amputation stumps (open wound with exposed bone edges, painful neuroma, insufficient soft-tissue cover) revision is performed by various classical methods. The wound surface at the stump apex is first excised; if bone protrudes, it is remodeled. The residual wound is covered with local flaps.

Yusevich method: the defect is freshened, and a 10–15 cm longitudinal skin incision along the limb axis is made through skin and subcutaneous tissue. One edge of the incision is split perpendicular to the limb axis, fashioning a rectangular flap that is undermined and shifted to cover the defect. The Frumin method uses an analogous principle but with an elliptical incision with a pedicle to the stump apex; the ellipse is left open at the cutaneous pedicle, and after flap shifting the defect is well covered.

For large defects the classical cross-leg flap described above remains applicable. Free microvascular flaps (anterolateral thigh, latissimus dorsi, scapular flap) are the modern standard for large and complex stump defects.

## Sources and Classical Literature

The classical anatomy of the leg was given by Larrey's *Mémoires* (1812) — the battlefield amputations of his Napoleonic series codified the proximal-distal pragmatics — and Malgaigne's *Traité* (1847) for tibial and fibular shaft fractures. Pirogov's *Anatome topographica* (1852–59) gave the first comprehensive cross-sectional anatomical atlas of the leg, and his eponymous osteoplastic ankle amputation (1854) preserves talus and calcaneus as the load-bearing stump.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

The lower leg contains four muscular compartments (anterior with dorsiflexors and deep peroneal nerve, lateral with peroneal muscles and superficial peroneal nerve, superficial posterior with gastrocsoleus and plantaris, deep posterior with deep flexors and tibialis posterior with the tibial nerve and posterior tibial artery). The anteromedial subcutaneous surface of the tibia is the principal landmark and is used for the anteromedial approach to the tibial shaft, with the caveat of thin soft-tissue coverage and substantial wound healing concerns. The fibular approaches use a lateral incision for the shaft (with the superficial peroneal nerve crossing the field) and a posterolateral incision for the proximal fibula (with the common peroneal nerve at substantial risk around the fibular neck). Leg fasciotomy using the Mubarak–Hargens double-incision technique (1977; lateral incision for anterior and lateral compartments; medial incision for superficial and deep posterior compartments) is the standard treatment for acute leg compartment syndrome, with the Davey–Rorabeck single-incision variant (1984) for selected indications. The principal neurovascular structures of the leg include the anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve (anterior compartment), the peroneal artery (lateral and deep posterior compartments), the posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve (deep posterior compartment), the superficial peroneal nerve (lateral compartment, becoming subcutaneous in the distal third), the sural nerve (subcutaneous posterolateral leg, the classical donor for nerve grafting), and the saphenous vein and nerve (subcutaneous medial leg).

From the classical Bulgarian and Russian orthopaedic school in lower-leg surgery the following are worth retaining: the classical approaches to the tibia (anterior, medial, posterior after Boychev, posterolateral after Harmon) with their detailed topographic mapping of canalis cruropopliteus and canalis musculoperoneus superior et inferior; the classical osteotomies of the tibia for deformity correction — linear "ad casullo", double after Kirschner, cross-shaped after Brandt, chip after Kirschner, transverse segmental after Springer, segmental oblique after Bogoraz, bone-plastic after Chaklin, Hadzhistamov, and Ruy — together with segmental rotation osteotomy with 180° rotation and derotation osteotomy; classical intramedullary osteosynthesis after Spizharni–Küntscher (1939), the Zucman method (1965) with Zhelev's Bulgarian modification (1975), and modern locked tibial nails; classical plate osteosynthesis with the historical cerclage, Putti–Parnham bands, and screw fixation; extrafocal compression osteosynthesis after Ilizarov with application in bone transport for defect segments; classical leg lengthening with external fixation via decortication osteoclasis, lengthening after Wagner (1971), and the Ilizarov circular apparatus with its classical mount; classical operative principles in osteomyelitis — necrectomy, muscle plombage (Schulten 1896 for tibia), cross-leg muscle plombage, Filatov tube plombage, Phemister cancellous onlay (1947), Boyd dual onlay, Papineau open cancellous grafting (1973), and Masquelet induced membrane (1986); classical operative techniques in congenital pseudoarthrosis of the tibia — Hahn–Huntington–Codivilla with "fibula pro tibia", Putti, Lexer, Hallock, Froelich, Moore, Kocher, Boyd, Eyre-Brook, Phemister, Sofield–Millar, and the Paus combined method; reconstruction of large bone defects after Zhukhovitsky, the Putti operation for plastic replacement of tibia with fibula, the Karchinov techniques for congenital aplasia and hypoplasia of tibia, and the Garrett osteoplastic reconstruction of the fibular malleolus; plastic coverage of exposed tibia with a fascial flap after Holevich and the cross-leg cutaneous flap; classical amputations — circular, elliptical, with small anterior and large posterior flap (Burgess 1968), with large anterior and small posterior flap, lateral, racquet after Farabeuf, and the classical Pirogoff ankle-level amputation with the Dzhanelidze osteoplastic modification; and the classical amputation stump revision after Yusevich, Frumin, and the cross-leg pedicle.

The classical Bulgarian school emphasizes that the leg — with thin soft-tissue coverage, distinctive neurovascular topography, and a critical role in the lower extremity — demands an individualized approach to each clinical scenario: the limited soft-tissue envelope at the anteromedial approach dictates alternatives (posterolateral after Harmon, minimally invasive MIPO, intramedullary nailing); the biomechanical features of the tibial diaphysis determine the choice between plate, nail, and external fixation; and reconstruction options for defect states (Masquelet, Ilizarov bone transport, vascularized fibula) form the basis of contemporary orthopaedics. This classical foundation remains relevant for understanding the evolution of tibial surgery and for daily clinical decision-making in revision and non-standard scenarios. The chapter that follows turns to the ankle and foot, completing the lower extremity anatomical sequence.
