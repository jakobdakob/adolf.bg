## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The lower leg, extending from the knee to the ankle, carries a distinct surgical profile shaped by the triangular cross-section of the tibia with its anteromedial subcutaneous surface, by the limited soft-tissue cover in the distal third, and by the strictly compartmentalised arrangement of four fascial spaces around the two bones. Surgical anatomy is dictated by the neurovascular bundles that travel through the three leg canals (the popliteo-tibial canal and the two musculo-peroneal canals) and by the characteristic vulnerability of the common peroneal nerve as it winds around the fibular neck. This chapter presents in sequence the regional bony anatomy, the four compartments and their neurovascular bundles, the biomechanical particulars of the tibia as the weight-bearing bone, the principal operative approaches (anteromedial to the tibial shaft, posterolateral by Harmon, lateral to the fibula, fasciotomy incisions for compartment syndrome, posterior approach to the proximal tibia by Boychev), modern intramedullary and plate techniques, extrafocal compression-distraction osteosynthesis by Ilizarov, lengthening by Wagner, amputations by Burgess, Pirogoff and Farabeuf, and the classical Bulgarian and Russian operative school that remains part of the orthopaedic curriculum in Bulgaria.

### Bony Anatomy
<details id="bones">
<summary>Bony Anatomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The tibia is the principal weight-bearing bone of the lower leg. In cross-section the shaft is roughly triangular with three borders (anterior, medial and interosseous, the last being posterolateral) and three surfaces: a medial subcutaneous surface (between the anterior and medial borders), a lateral extensor surface (between the anterior and interosseous borders), and a posterior flexor surface (between the medial and interosseous borders). The anteromedial subcutaneous surface is freely palpable along its entire length — from the tibial tuberosity proximally to the medial malleolus distally. This accessibility is both a strength (the anterior approach to the tibial shaft is technically straightforward) and a weakness (the thin soft-tissue cover in the distal third makes plate osteosynthesis at this level risky in terms of wound healing and infection). The bone widens into two metaphyses: proximally bearing the two tibial plateaus with medial and lateral condyles and the intercondylar eminence; and distally forming the upper articular surface of the ankle together with the medial malleolus.

The fibula is the slim lateral bone of the lower leg that carries no significant weight (approximately 6–17% of the axial load). It is almost entirely enclosed by the muscles of the four compartments, except at its proximal end (head and neck, which are subcutaneous) and in its distal quarter (where it becomes the lateral malleolus and is subcutaneous). The fibular head articulates with the lateral tibial condyle at the proximal tibiofibular joint; the distal fibula is bound to the tibia by the dense distal tibiofibular syndesmosis and forms the lateral wall of the ankle mortise. This relative freedom of the fibula makes it the ideal donor for a vascularised bone graft (Taylor 1975, see Topic Trauma-32): viable bone up to 25 cm in length can be harvested with its axial blood supply from the peroneal artery.

    <li>Triangular cross-section of the tibia: anterior, medial and interosseous borders defining a medial subcutaneous surface, a lateral extensor surface, and a posterior flexor surface.</li>
    <li>The anteromedial subcutaneous surface of the tibia runs from the tibial tuberosity to the medial malleolus — the basis of the anterior approach; the thin distal-third cover explains the frequent wound-healing problems at this level.</li>
    <li>The fibula bears only 6–17% of the axial load — permitting safe resection of a large portion of its shaft for tibial osteotomy, reconstruction, or vascularised free transfer.</li>

### Fascial Compartments
<details id="compartments">
<summary>Fascial Compartments</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

Intracompartmental pressure above 30 mmHg is an indication for fasciotomy by Mubarak–Hargens.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray440.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The deep fascia of the leg is a dense, unyielding connective-tissue envelope which along the subcutaneous tibial borders is fused directly with the periosteum. Two intermuscular septa (anterior and posterior) extend from the deep surface of the encircling fascia to the fibula and enclose the lateral peroneal compartment. Between the two bones the interosseous membrane separates the anterior from the posterior space. The result is the four classical fascial compartments of the leg (Hollinshead, 6th edition): anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, and deep posterior — each with its own muscle group, its own dominant nerve, and its own principal artery.

**Anterior (extensor) compartment.** Bounded medially by the lateral extensor surface of the tibia, laterally by the extensor surface of the fibula and the anterior intermuscular septum, posteriorly by the interosseous membrane, and anteriorly by the deep fascia. Contents: tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus, and (when present) peroneus tertius. Function: dorsiflexion of the ankle and extension of the toes; inversion (tibialis anterior). Motor innervation: deep peroneal nerve. Arterial supply: anterior tibial artery (which enters the compartment through the proximal opening of the interosseous membrane).

**Lateral (peroneal) compartment.** Bounded anteriorly by the anterior intermuscular septum, posteriorly by the posterior intermuscular septum, and medially by the fibula. Contents: peroneus longus and brevis, which cross the lateral malleolus and produce eversion of the foot. Innervation: superficial peroneal nerve. No artery runs in the compartment — the muscles receive supply from branches of the peroneal artery that perforate the posterior septum.

**Superficial posterior (flexor) compartment.** Contents: gastrocnemius (two heads, medial and lateral, from either side of the femur), soleus (with a characteristic tendinous arch that bridges the posterior neurovascular bundle), and the slender plantaris. The tendons of the three muscles fuse into the Achilles tendon, which inserts on the calcaneal tuberosity. Function: plantarflexion of the ankle; gastrocnemius is also a weak knee flexor. Innervation: tibial nerve.

**Deep posterior flexor compartment.** Contents: tibialis posterior (most medial and deepest), flexor digitorum longus (medially), and flexor hallucis longus (laterally). The compartment also carries the posterior neurovascular bundle — tibial nerve and posterior tibial artery — which in the mid-leg gives off the peroneal artery. The compartment is separated from the superficial posterior by a dense fascial sheet (transverse intermuscular septum) and from the anterior by the interosseous membrane. Function: inversion of the foot, flexion of the toes, plantarflexion of the ankle. Innervation: tibial nerve.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Four compartments, four nerves: anterior (deep peroneal, dorsiflexion plus extension), lateral (superficial peroneal, eversion), superficial posterior (tibial, plantarflexion), deep posterior (tibial, inversion plus deep flexors). This is the skeleton of compartment syndrome.</li>
    <li>Dissociation between the two posterior compartments: the superficial holds the powerful gastrocnemius-soleus group; the deep holds the posterior neurovascular bundle — that is why a medial fasciotomy must detach the soleus from the tibia to reach the deep compartment.</li>
    <li>The lateral compartment has no dedicated axial artery — its supply comes from perforating branches of the peroneal artery through the posterior septum.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Neurovascular Structures

<div class="ts-body">

**Anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve.** The artery is a branch of the popliteal artery — given off at the lower border of the popliteus — and enters the anterior compartment through the proximal opening of the interosseous membrane. It descends on the anterior surface of the membrane with its venae comitantes alongside the deep peroneal nerve (branch of the common peroneal). In the upper third the bundle lies between tibialis anterior and extensor digitorum longus; in the middle third between tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus; in the distal third it passes anterior to the ankle joint line as the dorsalis pedis artery — its pulse is an essential index of peripheral arterial supply. The deep peroneal nerve provides sensation to the first dorsal web space.

**Superficial peroneal nerve.** Branches off the common peroneal in the lateral compartment, supplying motor innervation to peroneus longus and brevis. At the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the leg (typically 10–12 cm above the tip of the lateral malleolus) it pierces the deep fascia and becomes subcutaneous, dividing into a medial and an intermediate dorsal cutaneous branch which together innervate most of the dorsum of the foot. This superficial point of emergence is critical for the lateral approach to the distal fibula and for the lateral incision used in fasciotomy — injury yields a neuroma and dorsal-foot numbness.

**Posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve.** The posterior neurovascular bundle enters the deep posterior compartment through the popliteo-tibial canal, passing beneath the tendinous arch of the soleus. The tibial nerve descends on the deep surface of the soleus, over the posterior surface of tibialis posterior, and in the distal third turns medially to enter the tarsal tunnel behind the medial malleolus. Two to three centimetres below the soleal arch the posterior tibial artery gives off the peroneal artery — a large branch which descends along the medial border of the fibula in the deep posterior compartment and reaches the distal third with a perforating branch that traverses the distal opening of the interosseous membrane.

**Sural nerve.** Forms in the popliteal fossa by the union of the medial sural cutaneous nerve (branch of the tibial) and the lateral sural cutaneous nerve (branch of the common peroneal). It descends subcutaneously along the posterolateral aspect of the leg, accompanied by the short saphenous vein. At the level of the lateral malleolus it crosses behind the tip and continues along the lateral border of the foot as a sensory nerve. The sural nerve is the classical donor for nerve autografts (Topic Trauma-31) because the residual sensory deficit is minimal.

**Great saphenous vein and saphenous nerve.** The great saphenous vein originates from the medial dorsal venous arch in front of the medial malleolus and ascends subcutaneously along the medial side of the leg to the thigh. The saphenous nerve (terminal sensory branch of the femoral) accompanies the vein in the distal leg, supplying sensation to the medial leg and the medial border of the foot. This shared superficial location explains the importance of careful dissection during a medial fasciotomy incision.

### Topography by the Classical Bulgarian School
<details id="topography">
<summary>Topography by the Classical Bulgarian School</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian topographic school (Boychev) defines the leg region with a proximal boundary at a circular line passing anteriorly through the base of the tibial tuberosity and posteriorly 7–8 cm below the lower transverse popliteal crease. The distal boundary is a circular line 7–8 cm above the tip of the medial malleolus. The region is divided into anterior and posterior, the dividing line running medially along the medial border of the tibia and laterally along the line connecting the posterior aspect of the fibular head with the posterior aspect of the lateral malleolus.

In the anterior region the skin is relatively thin and mobile, thinning further distally and medially; the subcutaneous fat is a single, sparse layer. The subcutaneous tissue carries the great saphenous vein and the saphenous nerve, branches of the lateral sural cutaneous nerve and the superficial peroneal nerve. The great saphenous vein lies along the medial border of the tibia in its own fascial sheath; distally it lies in front of the medial malleolus, proximally 2 cm behind the medial tibial border. The saphenous nerve most often runs in front of the vein and gives off cutaneous twigs to the medial side of the leg. The superficial peroneal nerve pierces the deep fascia 10–12 cm above the tip of the lateral malleolus, in the interval between peroneus brevis and extensor hallucis longus, and then divides subcutaneously into two branches.

The deep fascia of the leg is thin and translucent; medially it fuses with the periosteum of the tibia. Two longitudinal intermuscular septa (anterior and posterior) divide the anterior region into two fascial sheaths — anterior and lateral. The interosseous membrane is a dense connective-tissue partition with two constant openings: a proximal one at the level of the base of the fibular head, through which the anterior tibial artery and its venae comitantes pass into the anterior compartment; and a distal one 6–8 cm above the tip of the lateral malleolus, through which the perforating branch of the peroneal artery passes. The anterior intermuscular septum attaches to the anterior border of the fibula and in its proximal part carries an opening for the deep peroneal nerve and its muscular branches.

In the posterior region the deep leg fascia has a superficial and a deep layer; between them in the proximal half lies the short saphenous vein together with the sural nerve (in the fascial canal of Pirogov); distally the vein moves into a deeper layer of the subcutaneous tissue. Between the two layers of the deep leg fascia, at the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the leg, there is a narrow fascial cleft — the distal exit of the popliteo-tibial canal — through which the neurovascular bundle emerges into the subcutaneous tissue of the posteromedial surface.

### Leg Canals
<details id="canals">
<summary>Leg Canals</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Popliteo-tibial canal.** The canal that houses the posterior neurovascular bundle of the leg (posterior tibial artery with two venae comitantes and the tibial nerve). It lies medially between the superficial and deep muscles. Its anterior wall is formed proximally by tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus, and distally by the latter and flexor hallucis longus; its posterior wall is the soleus. The canal has three openings: one inlet and two outlets. The inlet is a slit between the tendinous arch of the soleus and popliteus, through which the popliteal vessels and the tibial nerve enter. The proximal outlet has the shape of a triangular cleft between the fibular neck (laterally), popliteus (above), and tibialis posterior (medially and below); through it the anterior tibial vessels pass into the anterior compartment. The distal outlet is a narrow fascial cleft between the two layers of the deep leg fascia at the junction of the middle and distal thirds.

**Superior musculo-peroneal canal.** Located on the lateral surface of the fibula with an inlet on the lateral aspect of the fibular head (3 cm below its apex), through which the common peroneal nerve enters. Its proximal outlet lies on the medial aspect of the fibula 6–7 cm below the head; through it the deep peroneal nerve exits into the anterior compartment. The distal outlet lies on the anterolateral surface of the leg 10–12 cm above the tip of the lateral malleolus; through it the superficial peroneal nerve emerges into the subcutaneous plane.

**Inferior musculo-peroneal canal.** Begins 5–7 cm below the origin of the peroneal artery and descends parallel and close to the fibula; the artery gives off a perforating branch 6–8 cm above the tip of the lateral malleolus and a communicating branch at the level of the ankle.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The popliteo-tibial canal carries the posterior neurovascular bundle and has three openings: an inlet (between the soleal arch and popliteus), a proximal outlet (for the anterior tibial vessels), and a distal outlet (where the bundle emerges into the subcutaneous tissue of the posteromedial surface).</li>
    <li>In the superior musculo-peroneal canal, the common peroneal nerve enters at the lateral aspect of the fibular head, the deep peroneal exits 6–7 cm below into the anterior compartment, and the superficial peroneal exits 10–12 cm above the tip of the lateral malleolus.</li>
    <li>The inferior musculo-peroneal canal contains the peroneal artery, whose perforating branch crosses 6–8 cm above the tip of the lateral malleolus — the anatomic basis for sparing the neurovascular bundle at distal incisions.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Biomechanics
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="biomechanics">
<details>
<summary>Biomechanics</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The tibia bears approximately 83–94% of the axial load of the leg; the fibula carries only 6–17%. This asymmetry explains why isolated fibular fractures rarely require osteosynthesis outside the context of the ankle mortise, while tibial fractures almost always demand stable reconstruction. The tibial shaft is approximately 25 mm in cross-sectional diameter in its mid-portion, and its medullary canal narrows in the isthmus of the middle third — the anatomic basis for typical tibial nail diameters of 8–11 mm and for the need for reaming when the canal width is below the chosen nail diameter.

The triangular shape of the tibial cross-section gives maximum bending stiffness in the antero-posterior plane. The anteromedial subcutaneous surface is approximately planar — the only surface ideal for plate osteosynthesis, when soft-tissue cover allows. The distal third of the tibia widens into the ankle plafond; in this metaphyseal zone the cortical shell is thin and cancellous bone predominates — the anatomic basis for the frequent complications of distal tibial fractures (delayed union, nonunion) and for the need to reinforce fixation with plates.

The proximal tibiofibular joint is a true synovial joint with about 10° of independent motion; the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis is a dense, almost rigid bond between the two bones which accommodates the 3–5° dorsiflexion rotation of the lateral malleolus during walking. Disruption of the syndesmosis in high fibular fractures (Maisonneuve, Weber C) requires supplementary screw fixation (Topic Trauma-28). The interosseous membrane transfers part of the axial load from the tibia to the fibula with fibre orientation about 20° to the horizontal.

In the sagittal plane the mechanical axis of the knee passes approximately 6–8 mm medial to the centre of the intercondylar eminence, and the ankle axis approximately 8–10 mm lateral to the centre of the plafond. The tibial mechanical axis from the centre of the plateau to the centre of the plafond is a critical parameter in osteotomies for genu varum/valgum correction (Topic Orthopaedics-12) and in total knee arthroplasty (Topic Orthopaedics-13).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The tibia carries 83–94% of the axial load; the fibula 6–17%. This determines the priority of tibial reduction and fixation in double fractures.</li>
    <li>The anteromedial subcutaneous surface is approximately planar — optimal for plate osteosynthesis when soft tissues permit. The distal third has thin cover and carries an elevated risk of infection on opening.</li>
    <li>The minimum diameter of the medullary canal in the middle-third isthmus determines the choice of tibial nail (typically 8–11 mm); a canal narrower than the required diameter mandates reaming.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Landmarks and Imaging
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="landmarks-imaging">
<details>
<summary>Landmarks and Imaging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Palpable surface landmarks: tibial tuberosity (with the attachment of the patellar tendon) — proximal anterior landmark; fibular head — 2–3 cm below the lateral femoral condyle, proximal lateral landmark; anterior tibial border — palpable along its entire length; medial tibial border — palpable along its entire length; medial and lateral malleoli — distal landmarks; Achilles tendon — distal posterior landmark; common peroneal nerve — can be rolled under the fingertips behind the fibular head as it winds around the neck. Surface projection of the posterior neurovascular bundle: from the midpoint of the popliteal crease to the midpoint between the posterior border of the medial malleolus and the medial edge of the Achilles tendon. Surface projection of the anterior tibial artery: from a point midway between the fibular head and the tibial tuberosity to the midpoint of the bimalleolar line.

For intraoperative navigation, fluoroscopic identification of the proximal and distal tibial ends is standard during intramedullary osteosynthesis, minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis, and external fixator placement. CT of the distal tibia is indispensable in complex pilon fractures for evaluation of articular congruency and for planning the approach — anteromedial, anterolateral, or combined.

## Classification of Approaches
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classification">
<details>
<summary>Classification of Approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Surgical approaches in the leg region are classified along four intersecting axes.

**By target bone and segment:** proximal tibia (plateau), tibial shaft, distal tibia (plafond), proximal fibula (head and neck), fibular shaft, distal fibula (lateral malleolus).

**By surface and internervous plane:** anteromedial (no internervous plane, epiperiosteal dissection to the subcutaneous medial surface); anterolateral (between anterior and lateral compartments); posterolateral by Harmon (between the gastrocnemius-soleus-flexor group medially, supplied by the tibial nerve, and the peroneal muscles laterally, supplied by the superficial peroneal); posterior by Boychev (between the tendons of semitendinosus, semimembranosus, gracilis and sartorius medially and the medial head of gastrocnemius laterally); fasciotomy (four-compartment decompression via lateral and medial incisions by Mubarak–Hargens, or a single lateral incision by Davey–Rorabeck).

**By invasiveness:** open (full exposure); minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (bridge plating with subcutaneous tunnelling); intramedullary (antegrade with the Spizharni–Küntscher nail, the Zucman nail, the modern locked nail); percutaneous (screw or pin under fluoroscopy); external (circular Ilizarov, monolateral Wagner, hybrid).

**By aim:** fracture reduction and stabilisation; decompression of compartment syndrome; bone biopsy; bone grafting; reconstruction of defects (Masquelet, vascularised fibula, bone transport); lengthening; correction of angular or rotational deformity; amputation; nerve decompression (common peroneal at the fibular neck, tibial at the tarsal tunnel).

The choice of approach is dictated by the location of the pathology, by the state of the soft tissues (open fracture, burn, radiation fibrosis, previous surgery), by comorbidities, and by the surgeon's experience. The principle that "the penalty of the approach must not exceed the benefit of the procedure" (Hoppenfeld) remains directive — anteromedial plating is technically simple but when the soft-tissue cover is compromised it is wise to choose the posterolateral Harmon approach, a minimally invasive plate technique, or intramedullary osteosynthesis.

</div>
</details>

## Treatment
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Nonoperative
<details id="nonoperative">
<summary>Nonoperative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Nonoperative management of closed tibial shaft fractures in acceptable alignment was the historical standard from Malgaigne (1847) through the mid-twentieth century and remains applicable for low-energy fractures with minimal displacement, for patients at high perioperative risk, and in resource-limited settings. The standard classical technique is closed reduction under general or regional anaesthesia followed by a long-leg cast from the groin crease to the toes with 5° knee flexion and neutral ankle position for 6–12 weeks, with a cast change at 4–6 weeks as the swelling subsides. Classical criteria for acceptable alignment: angular deformity in the sagittal or coronal plane under 5°, rotation under 10°, shortening under 1 cm, and continuous interfragmentary contact. Where the first reduction is unsatisfactory, the classical manoeuvres of Malgaigne, Charnley, or Sarmiento are applied — the last developed the functional brace in 1967, an under-knee cast with patellar tendon-bearing trim that permits early partial weight-bearing and knee motion from week 4–6.

Skeletal traction through the calcaneus (Steinmann or Kirschner pin) was the historical first step in irreducible closed fractures with displacement and in polytrauma when definitive osteosynthesis was delayed. A 4–6 kg weight for 2–4 weeks was followed by cast immobilisation. In modern practice traction is almost entirely superseded by early internal or external fixation.

For undisplaced fibular fractures (Weber A of the lateral malleolus, isolated diaphyseal fracture) and for high fibular fractures with an intact syndesmosis, immobilisation in an under-knee cast for 4–6 weeks with progressive weight-bearing is the standard treatment.

### Operative
<details id="operative">
<summary>Operative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<details>
<summary>Anteromedial approach to the tibia</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The standard approach to the tibial shaft uses the subcutaneous medial surface of the bone. It is technically simple but carries risks arising from the thin soft-tissue cover.

**Indications.** Open reduction and internal fixation of tibial shaft fractures with a plate; biopsy and bone grafting; treatment of delayed or nonunion (with supplementary plating or bone grafting); decompressive fenestration in acute osteomyelitis; secondary closure of defects in osteomyelitis.

**Positioning.** Supine with the limb slightly externally rotated to expose the medial border of the tibia. A thigh tourniquet provides a bloodless field. A small bolster under the thigh helps neutral positioning.

**Landmarks.** Anterior tibial border — palpable throughout; tibial tuberosity proximally; medial malleolus distally.

**Skin incision.** Longitudinal incision on the anterior surface of the leg parallel to the anterior tibial border, about 1 cm lateral to it. The length depends on the required exposure; the entire subcutaneous surface of the tibia can be exposed if necessary. A longer incision is preferred to forced retraction of the skin flaps because of the poor blood supply to the distal skin.

**Internervous plane.** None — the dissection is epiperiosteal and does not disturb the nerve supply of the anterior compartment.

**Surgical technique.** The skin flaps are elevated to expose the subcutaneous medial surface of the tibia. The great saphenous vein on the medial side is identified and protected when the medial flap is reflected. The deep (medial subcutaneous) surface of the tibia is exposed directly. If access to the lateral extensor surface is required the deep fascia is incised along the medial border of tibialis anterior and the muscle is reflected laterally; the periosteum is incised only as needed, without stripping isolated fragments. The posterior surface can be reached epiperiosteally around the medial border: proximally — by lifting flexor digitorum longus from the posterior surface; distally — by lifting tibialis posterior; this approach is technically more difficult than the true posterolateral.

**Dangers.** Great saphenous vein and saphenous nerve on the medial side — careful protection during reflection of the medial flap. Thin soft-tissue cover in the distal third — high risk of infection and wound dehiscence at plate osteosynthesis, especially in smokers, in patients with chronic venous insufficiency, and in elderly patients. Stripping of isolated bone fragments — leads to complete devascularisation and nonunion; an absolute contraindication.

**Special points.** Skin flaps must be closed with meticulous adaptation under no tension — longitudinal incisions heal well; transverse or irregular ones break down easily. Minimise periosteal stripping at osteosynthesis — devascularised bone does not unite.

  <strong>Cave</strong>

    <li>Stripping of isolated bone fragments — they lose blood supply and will not unite even if perfectly reduced. Preserve the soft-tissue attachments of every fragment.</li>
    <li>Alternative when soft-tissue cover is compromised: intramedullary nailing (infrapatellar or suprapatellar), minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis, or the posterolateral Harmon approach with an incision along the lateral border of the gastrocnemius.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Extension of the approach.** Local — the entire subcutaneous medial border of the tibia can be exposed. Proximal — continue along the medial side of the patella through the medial patellar retinaculum to access the knee joint. Distal — curve around the medial malleolus to access the tarsal tunnel and the medial ankle structures.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Posterolateral approach to the tibia (Harmon)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The approach to the middle two-thirds of the tibia through the interval between the gastrocnemius-soleus-flexor group medially and the peroneal group laterally — described by P. H. Harmon (1945, USA) and developed in the Bulgarian school by Boychev — is technically demanding but provides access to the posterior surface of the tibia when the anterior soft-tissue cover is compromised.

**Indications.** Internal fixation of tibial fractures with damaged or infected skin over the medial subcutaneous surface; delayed or nonunion (especially with interosseous fibulo-tibial synostosis, conceptually developed by Phemister); decompressive necrectomy in chronic tibial osteomyelitis with an intact posterior soft-tissue envelope; access to the middle of the posterior fibular surface.

**Positioning.** Lateral decubitus with the affected side up; bony prominences of the lower leg are padded to prevent pressure sores; bloodless field with tourniquet after limb elevation for 5 minutes.

**Landmarks.** The lateral border of the gastrocnemius is easily palpated in the calf; the short saphenous vein descends subcutaneously over the posterolateral surface.

**Skin incision.** Longitudinal incision along the lateral border of the gastrocnemius centred over the pathology. Minimum length 10 cm; the entire middle two-thirds of the tibia can be exposed if required.

**Internervous plane.** Between the gastrocnemius, soleus and flexor hallucis longus (all supplied by the tibial nerve) medially, and the peroneal muscles (supplied by the superficial peroneal) laterally — between the superficial posterior and lateral compartments.

**Surgical technique.** Reflect the skin flaps carefully, preserving the short saphenous vein and the sural nerve (which descend subcutaneously over the posterolateral surface). Incise the deep fascia in line with the incision and identify the interval between the lateral head of gastrocnemius and soleus posteriorly, and peroneus longus and brevis anteriorly. Muscular branches of the peroneal artery in the proximal part of the incision may require ligation. Identify the lateral border of soleus and retract it together with gastrocnemius medially and posteriorly; underneath, arising from the posterior surface of the fibula, identify flexor hallucis longus. Detach the lower part of the soleal origin from the fibula and retract it posteriorly and medially; detach the flexor hallucis longus from its fibular origin and retract it posteriorly and medially. Continue medially across the interosseous membrane, detaching the fibres of tibialis posterior that arise from the membrane. The posterior tibial artery and the tibial nerve remain protected behind the dissection — separated from it by the bulk of tibialis posterior and flexor hallucis longus. The dissection reaches the lateral border of the tibia; incise the periosteum and expose the posterior tibial surface subperiosteally.

**Dangers.** Short saphenous vein — preserve where possible but may be ligated without consequence for venous return. Branches of the peroneal artery — ligation or coagulation in the proximal end. Posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve — safe as long as the surgical plane remains on the interosseous membrane and does not wander posterior to flexor hallucis longus and tibialis posterior. Peroneal artery along the medial border of the fibula — careful dissection of the distal half.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>True internervous plane — between the tibial-nerve muscles medially (gastrocnemius-soleus-flexor hallucis longus) and the superficial-peroneal-nerve muscles laterally (peroneal group).</li>
    <li>The posterior neurovascular bundle is protected by the bulk of tibialis posterior and flexor hallucis longus; keeping the dissection on the interosseous membrane avoids the bundle plane.</li>
    <li>The approach cannot be extended into the proximal fourth of the tibia because the posterior surface is covered by popliteus and the more superficial posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Extension of the approach.** Distally — continue between the posterior border of the lateral malleolus and the Achilles tendon, transitioning into the posterolateral approach to the ankle. Proximally — not possible, because of the popliteus cover of the upper quarter of the posterior tibial surface and the more superficial position of the posterior tibial vessels and tibial nerve.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Posterior approach to the proximal posterior tibial surface (Boychev)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Described in the Bulgarian school for specific proximal indications. The patient is prone. The skin incision is L-shaped: the horizontal limb crosses the popliteal fossa at the level of the lower popliteal crease, then turns distally along the medial border of the medial head of gastrocnemius for 7–10 cm. The subcutaneous tissue and the deep leg fascia are incised, preserving the short saphenous vein and the medial sural cutaneous nerve. The space between the conjoint tendons of semitendinosus, semimembranosus, gracilis and sartorius (medially) and the medial head of the gastrocnemius (laterally) is identified; deeper dissection exposes the popliteus and the posterior surface of the proximal tibia.

**Indications.** Tumour biopsy of the proximal tibial metaphysis; drainage of deep posterior abscesses (tuberculous psoas abscess with distal collection or osteomyelitic sepsis of the proximal tibia); reconstruction of the posterior tibial plateau (Schatzker IV–V) with posterior plate fixation.

**Dangers.** Popliteal artery and vein and tibial nerve — pass through the popliteal fossa and require care at the horizontal limb of the incision. Popliteus muscle — preserve with minimal dissection.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Approach to the fibula</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The lateral approach to the fibula provides access to the entire length of the bone — from the head proximally to the lateral malleolus distally.

**Indications.** Partial fibular resection in tibial osteotomy or in the treatment of tibial nonunion; fibular resection for four-compartment decompression (rarely); tumour resection; resection in osteomyelitis; open reduction and internal fixation of fibular fractures (in the context of the ankle mortise, Topic Trauma-28); harvest of a corticocancellous graft or a vascularised fibular graft (Topic Trauma-32).

**Positioning.** Lateral decubitus with the affected side up; bony prominences of the lower leg are padded; bloodless field with tourniquet. Alternatively: supine with a sandbag under the buttock to internally rotate the limb.

**Landmarks.** Fibular head — 2–3 cm below the lateral femoral condyle, easily palpable. Common peroneal nerve — palpable as it winds around the fibular neck. The distal fourth of the fibula is subcutaneous.

**Skin incision.** Linear incision just posterior to the fibula, beginning behind the lateral malleolus and extending proximally to the fibular head. If proximal exposure is needed, continue up and back in line with the biceps femoris tendon. Beware of the common peroneal nerve, which lies subcutaneously over the neck of the fibula and is easily cut if the skin incision is too bold.

**Internervous plane.** Between the peroneal muscles (superficial peroneal nerve) and the flexor muscles (tibial nerve).

**Surgical technique.** For exposure of the fibular head and neck, incise the deep fascia in line with the incision proximally, paying particular attention to the underlying common peroneal nerve. Find the posterior border of the biceps femoris tendon and identify the common peroneal nerve behind it; trace it as it winds around the fibular neck with care. Mobilise the nerve from the groove on the posterior surface of the neck by cutting fibres of peroneus longus that cover it, and gently retract it forward with a corrugated rubber drain. Identify and preserve every branch. Develop the plane between the peroneal muscles and the soleus; with the common peroneal nerve retracted anteriorly, incise the periosteum of the fibula longitudinally in the line of cleavage. Strip the muscles from the fibula: those arising from periosteum or fascia are stripped from distal to proximal because their fibres run distally; muscles with direct bony attachment are cut. The interosseous membrane is stripped subperiosteally from proximal to distal (its oblique fibres run upward).

**Dangers.** Common peroneal nerve — vulnerable as it winds around the fibular neck. The key to its preservation is proximal identification along the posterior border of biceps femoris, followed by careful tracing through the peroneal muscle mass. Avoid retracting the nerve where possible. The dorsal cutaneous branch of the superficial peroneal nerve in the distal third (at the junction of the middle and distal thirds) — injury causes numbness and a neuroma on the dorsum of the foot. Terminal branches of the peroneal artery close to the deep surface of the lateral malleolus — preserve by keeping the dissection subperiosteal. Short saphenous vein — may be ligated if necessary.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The common peroneal nerve winds around the fibular neck immediately beneath the skin — the skin incision must not be bold; identification and tracing of the nerve is the mandatory first step before deep dissection.</li>
    <li>The superficial peroneal nerve pierces the deep fascia 10–12 cm above the tip of the lateral malleolus — at any lateral distal-third incision it is in the field and must be identified.</li>
    <li>The periosteal blood supply of the fibula comes mainly from the peroneal artery — at vascularised graft harvest the dissection must preserve the peroneal pedicle.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Extension of the approach.** Distally — curve the incision over the lateral side of the tarsus with access to the sinus tarsi and the talocalcaneal, talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints.

The leg is the most common site of compartment syndrome and leg fasciotomy is one of the most common emergency orthopaedic operations. The historical standard since 1977 remains the double-incision technique of S. J. Mubarak and A. R. Hargens, in which two longitudinal skin incisions (lateral and medial) provide complete decompression of the four compartments. The alternative single-incision technique — described by Davey and Rorabeck (1984) — performs four-compartment decompression through a single extensive lateral incision with a passage behind the fibula to the deep posterior compartment; its indications are limited to cases where medial soft tissues are compromised (severe burns, infection, extensive previous surgery).

**Positioning.** Supine without tourniquet — the tourniquet is contraindicated in compartment syndrome as it worsens the perfusion deficit.

**Lateral incision.** Longitudinal incision on the lateral leg, approximately 2 cm anterior to the fibular shaft. Begins at the level of the tibial tuberosity and ends 6 cm above the ankle. The subcutaneous tissue and deep fascia are incised in the line of the skin incision — the fascia of the anterior and lateral compartments is opened by separate incisions in both directions of the flaps (the anterior compartment is released from the lateral aspect, the lateral from the medial aspect). The superficial peroneal nerve in the distal third (at the junction of the middle and distal thirds) is identified and protected.

**Medial incision.** Longitudinal incision on the medial leg, approximately 2 cm posterior to the medial tibial border. Begins at the level of the tibial tuberosity and ends 6 cm above the ankle. The great saphenous vein and the saphenous nerve are identified and protected on reflection of the flaps. The deep fascia is incised, exposing the superficial posterior compartment. To decompress the deep posterior compartment, the soleus is detached from the medial tibial border and reflected posteriorly under direct vision; the posterior intermuscular septum is divided with care for the posterior neurovascular bundle lying deep to it.

**Dangers.** Posterior neurovascular bundle — lies just beneath the posterior intermuscular septum; division of the septum to reach the deep posterior compartment must be performed under direct vision. The principal danger of the approach, however, is not nerve or vessel injury but incomplete decompression. Compartment syndrome is not an indication for minimally invasive surgery — the fascial incisions must extend along the full length of the compartment.

**After fasciotomy.** Wounds are left open with sterile dressings or VAC therapy; delayed primary closure or skin grafting on day 5–10 (Topic Trauma-32). For heavily contaminated injuries the classical Bulgarian approach (inherited from the Russian-Soviet field tradition of Belarmin and Vreden) prescribes copious irrigation with saline and Vishnevsky balsam, debridement to viable tissue, and late secondary closure or grafting after the oedema subsides.

<details>
<summary>Chronic exertional compartment syndrome</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Chronic exertional compartment syndrome is a different entity from the acute. It typically affects runners and other athletes; it presents with exercise-dependent pain and paraesthesiae that subside in rest within 15–30 minutes; it is diagnosed by intracompartmental pressure measurement before, during and after exercise (rise above 30 mmHg at rest, or above 20 mmHg one minute after exercise). The classical operative procedure is selective fasciotomy of the involved compartment (most often anterior and lateral, less often deep posterior) through a limited longitudinal incision with a long-handled fasciotome — unlike acute compartment syndrome, where all four compartments are opened. The skin incisions are short (3–5 cm), the fascia is incised under direct vision along the full length of the compartment, and the superficial peroneal nerve in the distal third of the lateral incision must be identified and protected. Wounds are closed primarily because oedema is not massive; return to sport is possible at week 4–6.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Intramedullary nailing of the tibia</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Intramedullary nailing is the standard for most adult diaphyseal tibial fractures. Tibial nails are angled at the upper end for antegrade insertion and straight in the antero-posterior projection.

**Classical infrapatellar approach.** The patient is supine on an orthopaedic traction table or in a free-leg position (with the ankle in a boot, or on an extension table with hip flexion 60° and knee flexion 100–120°). The skin incision is 5 cm along the medial border of the patellar tendon, beginning at the inferior pole of the patella and extending down to the tibial tuberosity. The tendon is retracted laterally; the entry point lies in the centre of the tibial plateau in the AP projection and just anterior to the posterior cortex in the lateral projection (Tornetta's "safe zone" is small — deviation from the proper axis leads to apex-anterior or apex-medial angulation at locked nailing, especially in proximal fractures). The bone is opened with an awl or curved opener; a guide wire is passed through the entry portal into the medullary canal; reaming with progressive flexible reamers to 1 mm above the chosen nail diameter; the nail is passed over the guide wire and the fracture is locked with proximal and distal interlocking screws.

**Suprapatellar approach.** An alternative entry through the suprapatellar recess with the knee in semi-extension (15–20° flexion). Easier reduction of proximal tibial (metaphyseal) fractures, less anterior knee pain postoperatively. Requires a dedicated protective tube around the guide path to prevent damage to the articular cartilage during reamer and nail passage. Distal interlocking screw placement is technically more demanding.

**Classical Spizharni–Küntscher technique.** G. Küntscher (Germany 1939) and V. M. Spizharni (USSR) — the historical predecessor of modern locked tibial nails. Closed or open technique. With closed nailing the leg is reduced on a Böhler frame or an orthopaedic table under intraoperative fluoroscopy. A 5 cm incision is made about the tibial tuberosity along its medial border; the bone is opened and a guide wire is advanced through the entry portal into the medullary canal toward the fracture. After radiographic confirmation of fragment position the wire is driven into the distal fragment, the Küntscher nail of pre-determined length and width is passed over the wire, and the fracture is stabilised. Open nailing is indicated for irreducible transverse and slightly oblique fractures and is performed in two stages: open reduction through a semilunar 10 cm incision over the fracture (subperiosteal dissection, fragment adaptation with levers, bone hooks, or Hass retractors), followed by osteosynthesis through a small incision at the tibial tuberosity.

**Zucman technique with Zhelev's Bulgarian modification.** Zucman (France 1965), inheriting the German-Soviet school, introduced tibial intramedullary osteosynthesis with a straight Küntscher femoral nail introduced through the tibial plateau medial to the patella. The construct is stable enough to permit early ambulation. Zhelev and colleagues (Bulgaria 1975) improved this method with a purpose-built stand, a device for measuring the nail length, and a ruler for determining the canal width.

**Modern locked nail.** The conceptual descendant of Spizharni–Küntscher with the advantages of proximal and distal interlocking screws for rotational and length stability of segmental and comminuted fractures. The standard since the 1980s and the dominant treatment of diaphyseal tibial fractures today.

    <li>The intramedullary nail is the standard for diaphyseal tibial fractures Gustilo–Anderson type I and II; for type III A–B–C external fixators (Ilizarov, monolateral) are preferred.</li>

<details>
<summary>Plate osteosynthesis of the tibia</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Classical plate osteosynthesis of the tibia (for transverse and slightly oblique fractures with intact soft tissues) is performed through a 12–15 cm incision slightly lateral to the anterior tibial border, curving outward, centred on the fracture level. After longitudinal incision of the deep leg fascia and periosteum the whole anterior muscle group is reflected laterally. The fragment ends are exposed as economically as possible; Lambotte or Lowman bone-holding forceps maintain provisional reduction. Fixation may be by several means: wire cerclage (at least two cerclages a few centimetres apart — suitable only for oblique fractures); strap fixation by Putti–Parnham (abandoned because of damage to the nutrient blood supply); screw fixation for oblique fractures (minimum two screws through both cortices a few centimetres apart); and plate and screw fixation for transverse fractures. In transverse tibial fractures with an intact fibula the classical recommendation is an oblique fibular osteotomy a few centimetres proximal or distal to the tibial fracture through a small lateral incision between soleus and the peroneal muscles — to allow tight interfragmentary contact at the tibia.

**Minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis.** The modern standard for plate fixation of tibial diaphyseal and metaphyseal (especially distal pilon) fractures. Short skin incisions at the two ends of the plate with subcutaneous tunnelling of the plate above the periosteum and screw fixation through stab incisions. The technique preserves the periosteal blood supply and reduces soft-tissue trauma. Anatomically pre-contoured plates are placed medially (for the distal tibia) or anterolaterally (for pilon fractures with a lateral fragment).

</div>
</details>

<summary>External fixation — Ilizarov and Wagner</summary>

The circular compression-distraction apparatus of G. A. Ilizarov (Kurgan, 1951–1971) applied to the leg with a classical mount of two partial rings (¾ of a circle) through the proximal metaphysis and two complete rings through the distal metaphysis provides stable axial fixation and controlled distraction. Daily distraction is 1 mm in four steps of ¼ mm — a regime in which the regenerate develops optimally. After the desired length is reached the apparatus is left in place until full consolidation of the regenerate (approximately one month per centimetre of lengthening), followed if necessary by a cast. Ilizarov's "tension-stress" principle of distraction osteogenesis remains fundamental and is the conceptual basis of later internal lengthening devices (Albizzia, ISKD, PRECICE).

**Bone transport by Ilizarov.** A standard strategy for tibial diaphyseal defects of 4–12 cm. A proximal corticotomy is performed in the proximal metaphysis with gradual distal transport of the intercalary fragment at 1 mm per day (4 × ¼ mm); consolidation develops in the regenerate zone, while at the docking site a compression-docking procedure is performed (with supplementary cancellous grafting if the docking shows a low tendency to unite). This technique is an alternative to Masquelet's induced membrane and to a vascularised fibula from the contralateral limb.

**Wagner's distraction apparatus.** The monolateral distraction apparatus of H. Wagner (Germany 1971) for leg lengthening employs a diaphyseal osteotomy, secondary plate osteosynthesis, and cancellous grafting of the distraction gap; the technique is effective for lengthening up to 4–6 cm and remains current in selected cases.

**Decortication osteoclasis.** Controlled fracturing (decortication) of the lateral cortex with small chips without disrupting the periosteal blood supply preserves the biological potential of the bone and provides a barrier of cancellous chips between the bone ends that activates osteogenesis. An anterolateral 7–8 cm incision is made parallel and 2–3 cm lateral to the anterior tibial border. After incision of the fascia, tibialis anterior is retracted laterally, the lateral surface of the tibia is exposed, and the periosteum is incised without stripping. The external fixator is then mounted with pre-assembled halves, the wires are tensioned, and the bone fragments are slightly distracted (about 0.5–1 cm).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Osteotomies for the correction of deformity</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical leg osteotomies are used for rachitic, post-traumatic, or epiphysial deformity. The choice of technique depends on the degree and level of the deformity, the patient's age, and the need for simultaneous lengthening.

**Linear transverse osteotomy in a cast.** Subcutaneous subperiosteal transection at the level of the greatest curvature with complete division of the anterior cortex and incomplete division of the posterior cortex, leaving the latter together with the periosteum as a hinge. Basis of the classical "in-cast" osteotomy: after the complete anterior section, two wedges with bases anteriorly are excised, their length pre-calculated for full correction — one from the external and one from the internal aspect.

**Double osteotomy by Kirschner.** Section of the tibia at two adjacent levels in the zone of greatest curvature with oblique directions — the upper "front-to-back and upward", the lower "front-to-back and downward"; the segments are then straightened and held in a cast.

**Cross-shaped osteotomy by Brandt.** Suitable for double deformities: after subperiosteal exposure an X-shaped osteotomy is performed, allowing multi-planar correction.

**Splinter osteotomy by Kirschner.** Suprapeiriosteal, with a sharp osteotome splitting the bone longitudinally along the line of greatest curvature into wedge-shaped "splinters" whose position and compression allow correction in any plane; characteristically the osteotomised parts remain in continuous contact and the technique allows simultaneous lengthening by direct extension.

**Transverse segmental osteotomy by Springer.** A wide anterior incision with complete subperiosteal stripping of a large tibial segment, followed by its transverse removal — the technique has been abandoned because of the size of the stripped segment and the lack of advantage over Bogoraz's osteotomy.

**Segmental oblique osteotomy by Bogoraz.** V. N. Bogoraz (1948, USSR) — performed at the level of greatest curvature with an oblique cut allowing rotation and lengthening — remains the historical reference for modern multi-planar corrections with external fixation.

**Bone-plastic osteotomy by Chaklin.** V. D. Chaklin (USSR) uses the convex side of the bone as a donor for a longitudinal cortico-periosteal sliver, cut with osteotome or electric saw and stored in saline during the correction; the small fibula is osteotomised subperiosteally and obliquely, a subperiosteal step osteotomy of the tibia is performed, the correction is carried out, and the stored sliver is replaced reversed — a technique combining correction with autogenous bone graft to reinforce the consolidation.

**Hadzhistamov's technique.** A thick wedge of the anterior tibial border together with the medial surface is excised, starting from the tibial tuberosity distally; the fibula is step-cut at the level of greatest curvature, the excised wedge is fashioned and seated in a new bed in the upper tibia, and the fibular defect is filled with cancellous chips.

**Roy's technique.** A longitudinal incision over the most prominent aspect of the tibia with slight lateral curving creates a thin cortico-periosteal flap by longitudinal lifting — followed by 2–3 transverse osteotomies and restoration of the flap as a lever for stabilisation.

**Segmental rotational osteotomy with 180° turn.** Performed through two incisions — an anterior slightly curved one and a posterior one for the fibula — with pre-sterilised templates of the segment that allow accurate marking of the osteotomy lines under soft-tissue protection. Particularly elegant in rotational deformities without significant angulation.

**Derotational osteotomy of the tibia.** For rotation of paralytic, spastic, metabolic, or post-traumatic origin — performed through a 3 cm longitudinal incision over the anterior tibial border just below the tibial tuberosity; the bone is freed from the soft tissues and cut transversely with an electric saw or osteotome. The fibula is broken by placing the distal part in marked varus or valgus — an assistant holds the knee fixed while the operator twists the distal part. Fixation is by plate and screws or by a hip-to-toes cast with 5° knee flexion for 6 weeks.

</div>
</details>

<summary>Treatment of osteomyelitis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian and Russian school describes a systematic approach to tibial osteomyelitis that remains the basis of modern treatment today.

**Acute haematogenous osteomyelitis.** An arched longitudinal 8–10 cm incision is made along the anteromedial surface, centred on the diseased area. Incision of the deep leg fascia reaches the periosteum, which may be "bulging" from a subperiosteal collection — on incision the pus drains under pressure; if the periosteum is quiet, it is incised longitudinally and the bone is stripped carefully, economically, and atraumatically, preserving the periosteal blood supply. Multiple drill or burr fenestrations of the cortex (mini-fenestrations) drain the intramedullary abscess, relieve the pressure, and allow placement of irrigation drains; the wound is left open with dressings and the patient is treated systemically with antibiotics by sensitivity (empirically — broad-spectrum against Staphylococcus aureus including methicillin-resistant strains).

**Chronic osteomyelitis with sequestra.** Necrectomy is performed through a longitudinal anterior tibial incision with exposure of as many sinuses as possible. The thickened periosteum is incised longitudinally and lifted 1.5–2.5 cm on each side. The bone is opened with osteotome or electric burr along the osteomyelitic focus and curetted to healthy bleeding cancellous bone. All sequestra are removed; sinuses are excised or marked for secondary closure; the cavity is irrigated with saline and local antibiotic by sensitivity is instilled.

**Muscle plombage.** First described by Schulten in 1896 for the tibia — performed with tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, peroneus longus, or flexor hallucis longus depending on the location. For proximal and middle cavities, the muscle bellies of tibialis anterior and flexor hallucis longus are usually used (with the proximal insertion preserved and the distal end divided). For wall cavities the muscle body itself is used without dividing the insertion; for full cylindrical cavities a distally divided flap is used. The tip of the muscle flap is held against the floor of the cavity with a suture passed through a small electric burr hole and tied externally over a gauze roll.

**Cross-leg muscle plombage.** For distal-leg cavities with insufficient local muscle mass — uses muscles from the contralateral lower limb (usually sartorius or the medial head of gastrocnemius) together with a skin flap. The two limbs are crossed and held in a cast for 4 weeks; division at day 45. Plombage with a Filatov tube (Vladimir Filatov, USSR) — for cavities in areas with little surrounding muscle (knee and ankle) — is a staged procedure of tube formation, usually from the abdomen or chest wall, transfer to the leg through the arm, and final insertion into the bone cavity.

**Cancellous onlay by Phemister.** D. B. Phemister (1947, USA) — a classical single-stage procedure for tibial nonunion with intact soft-tissue cover: the posterolateral approach between the peroneal muscles and soleus exposes the lateral surface of the tibia and fibula; both bones are decorticated and an interfibulo-tibial onlay cancellous graft from the iliac crest is laid between the two main fragments. Long-term union rates in classical nonunions reach 80–90%.

**Double onlay by Boyd.** H. B. Boyd uses two solid corticocancellous grafts from the contralateral tibia, placed anteriorly and posteriorly on the affected bone and fixed with screws — indicated for severe tibial defects.

**Open cancellous graft by Papineau.** J.-P. Papineau (1973, Canada) — for infected nonunion with a large bone defect and an inability to close the soft tissues primarily. The procedure has three stages: radical debridement of the infected focus to viable bone; open cancellous grafting with autologous bone from the iliac crest in the defect; and granulation with secondary closure or skin grafting over the cancellous bed.

**Induced membrane by Masquelet.** A. C. Masquelet (1986, France) — the modern equivalent. In the first stage, after debridement, the defect is filled with PMMA cement around which a biologically active membrane with proangiogenic and pro-osteogenic properties develops over 6–8 weeks; in the second stage the cement is carefully removed without damaging the membrane and the cavity is filled with autologous cancellous graft (usually from the iliac crest or by RIA from the femoral canal). The technique remains the principal modern method for defects up to 8–10 cm.

  </figcaption>

Congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia (usually associated with neurofibromatosis type 1) is one of the most difficult problems in paediatric orthopaedics — union is harder to obtain than in any other condition. Operative results are worse in very early childhood, whereas with advancing age severe deformity develops (leg bowing, pes calcaneus, shortening). The classical Bulgarian literature describes three operative families: pedicled grafts, free grafts, and combined.

**Hahn–Huntington–Codivilla — "fibula pro tibia".** Conceptually V. Hahn (1880s, Germany), developed by T. W. Huntington (1905, USA) and A. Codivilla (1911, Italy) — uses the fibula as a tibial substitute. After section of the fibula at its upper and lower ends it is fixed to the remnants of the tibia; the operation can be performed in two stages (upper and lower insertion sequentially) or as a single stage. After transfer the fibula assumes the function of the tibia and hypertrophies markedly in compensation. The classical indications are large tibial pseudarthroses — congenital or acquired — with a defect length above 4–5 cm.

**Putti's technique.** V. Putti (Italy) — based on the biomechanical principle of closing the posteriorly open angle of the pseudarthrosis. The ipsilateral fibula is used (often itself pseudarthrotic); a long segment is harvested, the ends are freshened, and the graft is placed posteriorly across the pseudarthrosis so that the angle becomes a triangle. On the posterior arm of the triangle the loading lines are transferred and the graft progressively hypertrophies, relieving the pseudarthrosis. After stabilisation the anterior pseudarthrotic segment is excised and the deformity corrected.

**Other classical techniques.** Lexer (Germany) places free bone grafts around the freshened pseudarthrosis; Froelich and Codivilla add fibular osteotomy (if healthy) with multiple massive grafts for stabilisation. Hallock uses cancellous chips for step osteosynthesis after freshening of the pseudarthrosis. Phemister, Henderson, Colonna, Delahye, and Compère place massive grafts bridging the pseudarthrosis without preparation, fixed with screws. Moore introduces a bone graft into the medullary canal with external compression along the limb; Boyd fills the pseudarthrotic site with cancellous chips. Kocher uses two solid grafts and an intramedullary Küntscher nail. Eyre-Brook harvests a free graft from the healthy tibia and drives it into the proximal and distal segments of the pseudarthrotic tibia from the concave side.

**Combined method of Paus.** Combines a free graft with a pedicled graft, "fibula pro tibia" by Hahn–Huntington–Codivilla, and intramedullary osteosynthesis with a metal nail. Performed in four stages: intramedullary metal osteosynthesis with a Küntscher or Schiman nail in the proximal fragment, which is slid into the distal fragment (the two ends remain apart); exposure of the proximal fibula with protection of the common peroneal nerve and osteotomy with transfer of the upper end into a window in the tibia; analogous lower insertion; and if required — additional free bone grafting. Results vary — union is obtained in 55–60% of cases, with late tibial fractures in a significant proportion.

**Zhukhovitsky's method.** V. Y. Zhukhovitsky (USSR) — osteoplastic reconstruction in large tibial defects requires preserved soft tissues and a functioning ankle. Through an arched anterior tibial incision the defect is exposed and freshened; a semicylindrical bone autograft exchange is then performed using grafts fashioned from the two segments of the pseudarthrotic tibia.

**Sofield–Millar method.** F. A. Sofield and E. A. Millar (USA) — multiple fragmentation and intramedullary nailing of long bones in osteogenesis imperfecta, particularly applicable to the tibia with multiple deformities and fractures. Performed by multiple osteotomies of the affected bone, rebleading of the segments in the correct axis, and intramedullary nailing with a growing or fixed nail. In osteogenesis imperfecta with telescoping nails (Bailey-Dubow, later Fassier-Duval) the growth problem is solved without repeated nail changes.

Restoration of tibial defects with the fibula has the most adherents in the classical Bulgarian and Soviet literature. Many variants have been described, including transfer of the entire fibular shaft, fixation with screws or nails, and combination with cancellous autografts.

**Putti's total tibial substitution with the fibula.** Used in congenital aplasia of the tibia, after total excision of the tibia for tumour, and for very wide tibial defects in which only the tibial poles remain. The operation is two-staged. In the first stage an external hooked incision is made from the junction of the middle and lower thirds of the thigh, through the fibular head, to the junction of the middle and lower thirds of the leg. The fascia lata is incised, biceps femoris is identified and, behind it, the common peroneal nerve (which must be protected). The muscle is detached from the fibular head; the fibular head is freed on all sides; the tibia (or its poles) is excised subperiosteally. After abduction and flexion of the leg the fibular head is moved into the intercondylar fossa of the femur with interposed soft tissue. Two variants are possible: creation of a new femoro-fibular joint, or arthrodesis of the fibula to the lateral femoral condyle. The foot is held in maximum equinus so that the body weight is borne on the metatarsal heads. At 6 months the cast is replaced by a special orthopaedic device. In the second stage (no earlier than 1 year after the first operation) the distal end of the fibula is arthrodesed to the talus or (if absent) to the calcaneus through an anterolateral 7 cm incision.

**Karchinov 1974 — bilateral total absence of the tibia.** K. Karchinov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> in bilateral congenital total absence of the tibia describes "support osteotomy of the fibulae": an osteotomy at the junction of the upper and middle thirds of the fibulae with an angle open outward and backward so that the distal femoral end rests on the fibulae. With a preserved distal tibial segment (intact ankle mortise) the fibula is osteotomised and fixed to the freshened distal tibial remnant with wire loops or screws in a second stage. With a preserved proximal tibial segment (intact knee joint) the proximal tibial remnant is joined to the upper end of the fibula in the first stage.

**Karchinov 1975 — congenital hypoplasia with a proximal tibial epiphysis.** A lateral incision exposes the proximal third of the fibula, which is stripped; a transverse osteotomy is made at the level between the metaphysis and the diaphysis. A second incision is made along the lateral aspect of the tibial tuberosity; the surviving tibial remnant is exposed and a bone bed is fashioned on its lateral surface at the level of the tibial tuberosity, into which the fibula is implanted with intact periosteum — a Y-shaped reconstruction of the proximal end.

**Tumour resection in the proximal third of the tibia.** In younger patients with tumour lesions in the proximal third of the tibia requiring wide resection, Karchinov uses the following technique: the fibular head is freshened and implanted into the lateral femoral condyle, fixed with Kirschner wires; a solid bone graft (preferably from the contralateral iliac crest) is placed for reconstruction; the wound is closed in layers and the limb is immobilised for 3–4 months. Two-stage fibular implantation (into the upper and then the lower part of the tibia) gives good functional results in selected cases.

**Reconstruction of the lateral malleolus by Garrett.** With absent distal fibula but no other deformity (retropositioned fibular malleolus, distal fibular hypoplasia), osteoplastic reconstruction of the fibular malleolus with the fibular head as a donor: a longitudinal lateral incision is made over the proximal quarter of the fibula, exposure between the peroneal mass and the fibular head of the gastrocnemius distally, and resection of the proximal third. The resulting graft is placed in the position of the missing distal fibula with the head as a new lateral malleolus; if it seats well, fixation with 1–2 screws to the freshened tibia is performed.

<summary>Shortening of the tibia</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For significant length discrepancy after consolidation of a malunited fracture, shortening is performed through a 15–18 cm slightly arched incision on the anteromedial side of the tibia. The periosteum is incised and about 8–10 cm of bone are exposed. In the centre of the tibia a transverse osteotomy is made with a Gigli saw or electric circular saw; from the medial half of the proximal fragment so many centimetres are removed as required to shorten the bone (for a 1 cm resection). The same is done in the distal fragment; the two semi-circular resection surfaces are matched and fixed with a Spizharni–Küntscher nail through the tibial plateau medial to the patella. An alternative is a transverse osteotomy with a second parallel osteotomy at the distance of the desired shortening, the fibula being osteotomised at the same level and cancellous chips packed about the osteotomy. Postoperatively a hip-to-toes cast is applied with the knee flexed and slight plantarflexion for 8 weeks.

<summary>Soft tissues — Z-plasty, Holevich fascial flap</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian school describes several characteristic soft-tissue operations in the leg that remain clinically relevant.

**Z-plasty for circular constriction band.** A circular subcutaneous constriction (fibrous ring with intact skin) is operated in two stages, each removing half of the circular furrow through a double or multiple Z incision with 45° angles. The exposed fibrous ring is excised and the corners of the triangles are exchanged and sutured — classical Z-plasty after Limberg.

**Staged resection for elephantiasis.** Chronic lymphoedema with subcutaneous fibrosis is treated with staged resection in four operative sittings. Through an S-incision along the whole anteromedial leg the skin is undermined bilaterally for one-quarter of the circumference, the thickened subcutaneous tissue is excised, and a parallel strip of deep fascia is removed; the wound is closed in layers. At the second sitting the same procedure is carried out on the opposite side. If the foot is involved an extra stage is added on the ankle region and dorsum. The alternative free skin transfer — separation of a skin-subcutaneous layer, harvest of a 0.6–0.8 mm dermatome graft, perforation and direct placement on the deep fascia with subsequent re-coverage by the original skin.

**Cross-leg flap.** For limited deep non-healing wounds with exposed bone the classical cross-leg flap is performed in three stages. The first is biological training of a pedicled skin flap from the contralateral leg — its base twice as wide as its free end; the flap is elevated, a sterile rubber sheet is placed beneath it, removed periodically to stimulate vascularisation. The second stage is transfer of the flap onto the prepared defect of the affected leg with cast immobilisation of the crossed limbs for one month. The third — separation of the limbs after 1–1.5 months. This operation has been replaced by free flaps with microvascular anastomosis but remains clinically relevant where microsurgery is unavailable.

**Holevich fascial flap.** Soft-tissue cover of the bared tibia with a fascial flap by Y. Holevich <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> is described for osteoplastic and reconstructive operations in the leg, particularly after firearm injury in which the periosteum is absent or sclerosed. An arched skin incision, opening outward, is made 1–2 cm lateral to the anterior tibial border; the periosteum is incised 0.5 cm medial to the anterior border; the bone is prepared (excision or graft placement by Chaklin, Khakhutov, Dzhanelidze, or wedging), after which a rectangular fascial flap is cut from the deep leg fascia with a wide pedicle from the medial tibial border. The flap is turned over the bone and sutured to the remnants of the periosteum end-to-end or as a duplication, insulating the graft. This elegant principle — local fascial cover providing bone insulation and vascularisation — conceptually precedes the modern local muscle rotation flaps (gastrocnemius for the proximal, soleus for the middle, and flexor hallucis longus or free flaps for the distal third of the tibia, described by Hidalgo and Mathes).

<details>
<summary>Open fractures — Bulgarian and Russian military tradition</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian and Russian military-field orthopaedic tradition (inherited from G. I. Belarmin, R. R. Vreden, V. A. Oppel, and Y. Y. Dzhanelidze) formulates the principles of treatment of open fractures that remain valid in contemporary practice.

The principles are applied sequentially: early debridement within the first 6–8 hours to viable tissue with removal of all loose bone fragments without periosteal supply and copious saline lavage; stable internal or external fixation — intramedullary osteosynthesis is permissible in Gustilo–Anderson type I–II, but external fixation is mandatory in type III A–B and C (Ilizarov is preferred for type III B–C); soft-tissue cover within the first 5–7 days — when primary closure is not possible a local muscle flap is used (gastrocnemius for the proximal third, soleus for the middle, free microvascular flap for the distal); systemic broad-spectrum antibiotics with gram-positive and gram-negative cover, with added anaerobic cover for heavy contamination (penicillin G against clostridia); tetanus prophylaxis with toxoid and, if needed, hyperimmune globulin; classical local use of Vishnevsky balsam (castor oil, xeroform, and birch tar) — historically influential in Soviet military medicine — remains part of the Bulgarian tradition for chronic and slowly healing wounds, although in modern practice it has been replaced by negative-pressure dressings (VAC).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Lower-leg amputation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Lower-leg amputation is the most frequent amputation in the human body; indications are vascular (Buerger's disease, senile gangrene from atherosclerosis, acute irreversible arterial ischaemia), severe infection, frostbite, trauma, and tumour. The leg can be amputated at any level, but the longer the residual lever, the better the transmission of motion to the prosthesis. The classical recommendation is an optimum level of 12–15 cm below the knee joint; amputation in the lower third complicates prosthetic fitting because of the short femoral-stump relationship.

**Burgess amputation.** The modern standard with a small anterior and a large posterior flap, developed by E. M. Burgess (1968, USA). Performed supine under Esmarch haemostasis. Two incisions are made: one along the fibula, the other along the medial tibial border. The midpoints of these incisions are joined on the anterior surface of the leg, and the distal ends are joined on the posterior surface. After cutting the soft tissues, two musculo-cutaneous flaps are obtained — a small anterior and a large posterior containing the gastrocnemius. The bones are cut at the base of the skin flaps: the anterior tibial border is bevelled (to prevent a painful bony edge against the prosthesis), the fibula is cut 1–1.5 cm higher. The vessels are ligated, the nerves are cut 2–3 cm higher under slight tension so that they retract into the soft tissues (prophylaxis against painful neuromata of the tibial and common peroneal nerves). The two musculo-cutaneous flaps are sutured together; the large posterior flap with the gastrocnemius forms a soft, biomechanically tolerant surface for distal loading on the stump. A drain for 48 hours is mandatory. The principle of "myoplastic closure with a posteriorly turned muscle" remains the modern standard for distal stump cover.

**Amputation with an elliptical skin incision.** Similar to the circular amputation but with an oblique incision angled forward or backward, yielding a musculo-cutaneous flap in the lower part of the ellipse.

**Classical circular amputation.** Open military — performed with transverse division of the soft tissues and the bones higher; the wound is left open or closed with a few sutures and drained for two days. The technique is applicable for open war wounds and for gas gangrene, in which primary closure is contraindicated.

**Amputation with a large anterior and small posterior flap.** Used when the posterior soft tissues are compromised but the lateral muscle mass with the skin is intact. The skin flap is fashioned by a sagittal 10 cm incision along the anterior tibial border, continuing laterally and posteriorly on the leg.

**Lateral amputation.** With a lateral flap — applicable for severe injury of the medial or lateral soft-tissue groups in which anteroposterior flap separation is not possible.

**Farabeuf's racquet method.** P.-J. Farabeuf (France) for amputation in the upper third of the leg uses a racquet skin incision, beginning at the tibial tuberosity, turning down on one side, passing posteriorly 4–5 cm lower, and turning up on the other side to the starting point. The skin and muscles are cut at one level, the bone slightly above the apex of the "racquet". Ligation of vessels, division of nerves higher, and closure of the skin wound with a few sutures. This method gives a well-shaped anterior stump edge, particularly suited to high proximal amputations.

**Pirogoff's classical amputation.** N. I. Pirogov (1854, Russia) — although anatomically located in the ankle (Topic Anatomy-11), conceptually a forerunner of below-knee myoplastic amputations: the distal calcaneal segment (or its posterior half) is preserved and transferred to the resection surface of the distal tibia as a weight-bearing surface. The historical significance is enormous — it introduced the principle of a "living weight-bearing surface" in distal amputation.

**Osteoplastic amputation by Dzhanelidze.** Y. Y. Dzhanelidze (1953, USSR) — the Russian variant with a periosteal bone cap.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Closure of amputation stumps</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For problem amputation stumps (open wound with exposed bone ends, painful neuroma, insufficient soft-tissue cover) revision is performed by several classical methods. First the wound surface at the apex of the stump is excised, and if the bone protrudes it is remodelled. The residual wound is covered with local flaps.

**Yusevich method.** Y. Y. Yusevich (USSR) — the defect is freshened, a 10–15 cm longitudinal incision is made along the axis of the limb, including the skin and subcutaneous tissue. One of the edges is split perpendicular to the axis of the limb, fashioning a rectangular flap, which is undermined and shifted to cover the defect.

**Frumin method.** A. M. Frumin (USSR) — an analogous principle, but with an elliptical incision pediculated to the apex of the stump; the ellipse is not closed at the site of the pedicle, and after shifting the flap the defect is covered.

For massive defects the classical cross-leg flap described above remains applicable. Free microvascular flaps (anterolateral thigh, latissimus dorsi, scapular) are the modern standard for large and complex stump defects.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>At lower-leg amputation, principles for an optimal stump: level 12–15 cm below the knee joint (shorter stumps under 5 cm are not functionally usable), small anterior and large posterior Burgess flap with myoplastic cover by gastrocnemius (a soft biomechanical surface for distal loading), bevelling of the anterior tibial border, fibula cut 1–1.5 cm higher, and traction division of the nerves to retract them into the soft tissues (tibial and common peroneal 2–3 cm higher — prophylaxis of painful neuromata).</li>
    <li>Pirogoff's classical amputation (1854) with transfer of the posterior half of the calcaneus remains the conceptual predecessor of the "living load-bearing" surface in distal amputations.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Outcomes and Complications
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Complications of the approaches in the leg region vary by anatomic region and by procedure.

**Anteromedial approach to the tibia.** Wound dehiscence and superficial infection — especially in the distal third with its thin cover (5–15% at plate osteosynthesis of distal fractures in smokers and patients with venous insufficiency); deep osteomyelitic infection (1–3% in fresh closed fractures, 5–10% in open); devascularised nonunion; injury to the great saphenous vein and saphenous nerve (rare with gentle dissection).

**Posterolateral approach (Harmon).** Technically demanding — requires strict adherence to the internervous plane; peroneal artery injury at medial fibular dissection; injury to the posterior neurovascular bundle on straying from the interosseous membrane; limited coverage of the proximal quarter of the tibia.

**Approach to the fibula.** Common peroneal nerve injury around the neck of the fibula (catastrophic foot drop); superficial peroneal nerve injury in the distal third (dorsal-foot neuroma); peroneal artery injury at medial fibular dissection.

**Fasciotomy.** Incomplete decompression — the most frequent problem; an improperly placed incision missing a compartment; superficial peroneal nerve injury in the distal third of the lateral incision; delayed closure with cosmetic defect; post-fasciotomy weakness (especially in missed deep posterior decompression with sustained ischaemia of the deep flexors).

**Intramedullary nailing.** Anterior knee pain (20–40% with the infrapatellar approach, less with the suprapatellar); malalignment in proximal fractures (apex-anterior, valgus, rotation); medial meniscal injury at infrapatellar approach; iatrogenic comminution of the fracture; cartilage injury at the suprapatellar approach without a protective tube.

**External fixation.** Pin- and wire-track infection (10–30%); local cellulitis; pin or wire breakage at prolonged frames; ankle and knee contracture during prolonged lengthening regimes if mobilisation is insufficient.

**Amputation.** Painful neuroma of the tibial or common peroneal nerve; phantom pain (20–80% of amputees in varying degree); prosthetic fitting difficulty with a stump shorter than 5 cm below the knee; compromised soft-tissue cover with necrosis of the large posterior flap; sterilisation of infection on amputation through an infected zone (mandates delayed primary closure).

## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the orthopaedic school in Bulgaria; the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. His three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curriculum reference and contains detailed descriptions of the classical tibial approaches (anterior, medial, posterior by Boychev, posterolateral by Harmon with the topographic mapping of the popliteo-tibial canal and the two musculo-peroneal canals), fasciotomies in compartment syndrome, and techniques for the treatment of osteomyelitis, pseudarthrosis, and amputations.

**K. Karchinov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1974, 1975).** Original operative techniques for bridging large tibial diaphyseal defects. In bilateral congenital total absence of the tibia — "support osteotomy of the fibulae" with an angle open outward and backward so that the distal femoral end rests on the fibulae. In congenital tibial hypoplasia with a proximal tibial epiphysis — a Y-shaped reconstruction of the proximal end with fibular osteotomy and insertion into a lateral bone bed at the tibial tuberosity. In tumour resection of the proximal third of the tibia — the fibular head is freshened and implanted into the lateral femoral condyle, fixed with Kirschner wires, together with a solid bone graft from the contralateral iliac crest.

**Zhelev et al. <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1975).** Bulgarian modification of the Zucman method for intramedullary nailing with a straight Küntscher nail through the medial tibial plateau: a purpose-built stand, a nail-length measuring device, and a ruler for medullary canal width. A conceptual predecessor of modern locked tibial nails.

**Jordan Holevich <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Fascial flap for soft-tissue cover of the bared tibia — an arched skin incision opening outward, harvest of a rectangular fascial flap with a wide pedicle from the medial tibial border, turning it over the bone and suturing to the periosteal remnant as a duplication. A conceptual predecessor of the modern local muscle rotation flaps for tibial cover.

**Chavdar Imamaliev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian contributions to reconstructive orthopaedics of deep tibial defects; biological assessment of transplantation possibilities.

**Hadzhistamov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Classical bone-plastic osteotomy with excision of a thick wedge of the anterior tibial border together with the medial surface, beginning at the tibial tuberosity distally; the fibula is step-cut at the level of greatest curvature, the excised wedge is fashioned and seated in a new bed in the upper tibia, and the fibular defect is filled with cancellous chips.

From the classical Russian and Soviet school, woven into the Bulgarian curriculum:

**V. N. Bogoraz <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1948).** Segmental oblique tibial osteotomy — performed at the level of the greatest curvature with an oblique direction allowing rotation and lengthening. The historical reference for modern multi-planar corrections with external fixation.

**V. D. Chaklin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Bone-plastic osteotomy using a longitudinal cortico-periosteal sliver from the convex side of the tibia, cut with an osteotome and stored in saline during the correction; the fibula is osteotomised subperiosteally and obliquely, a subperiosteal step osteotomy of the tibia is performed, the correction is carried out, and the stored sliver is replaced reversed.

**G. A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1951–1971).** The circular compression-distraction apparatus — a fundamental contribution to modern orthopaedics. The principle of "tension-stress" distraction osteogenesis remains the conceptual basis of all modern lengthening and reconstructive techniques. Standard leg mount: two partial rings proximally and two complete rings distally; daily distraction of 1 mm in 4 steps of ¼ mm; consolidation 1 month per centimetre of lengthening. Bone transport for defects 4–12 cm with regenerate on one side and docking on the other.

**V. M. Spizharni <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Intramedullary nailing — the Soviet pioneer of the method, parallel to Küntscher (Germany). The Spizharni–Küntscher nail (1939) remains the conceptual basis of modern tibial nails.

**G. I. Belarmin, R. R. Vreden, V. A. Oppel, Y. Y. Dzhanelidze <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** The Russian-Soviet military-field orthopaedic tradition that formulated the principles of treatment of open fractures — early debridement, stable fixation, soft-tissue cover, antimicrobial therapy, local agents (Vishnevsky balsam), and the principles of amputation in wartime.

**Y. Y. Yusevich, A. M. Frumin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Classical methods for the closure of problem amputation stumps (Yusevich — rectangular flap with a longitudinal incision; Frumin — elliptical pedicled flap). Standard procedures in the Bulgarian orthopaedic tradition.

**V. Y. Zhukhovitsky <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Osteoplastic reconstruction in large tibial defects with exchange of semicylindrical bone autografts fashioned from the two segments of the pseudarthrotic bone.

**N. I. Pirogov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1854).** Osteoplastic amputation at the ankle level — the conceptual predecessor of the "living load-bearing" surface in distal amputations (anatomically located in the ankle region, Topic Anatomy-11, but methodologically relevant for leg amputation).

Foreign classical techniques deeply embedded in the Bulgarian school: V. Hahn (1880s, Germany), T. W. Huntington (1905, USA), A. Codivilla (1911, Italy) with "fibula pro tibia"; V. Putti (Italy) — total tibial substitution with the fibula; E. Lexer (Germany); G. Küntscher (1939, Germany) — intramedullary nail; H. Wagner (1971, Germany) — monolateral distraction apparatus; J.-P. Papineau (1973, Canada) — open cancellous graft; A. C. Masquelet (1986, France) — induced membrane; D. B. Phemister (1947, USA) — cancellous onlay; H. B. Boyd (USA) — dual onlay; F. A. Sofield and E. A. Millar (USA) — nailing in osteogenesis imperfecta; S. J. Mubarak and A. R. Hargens (1977, USA) — double-incision fasciotomy; E. M. Burgess (1968, USA) — myoplastic amputation with small anterior and large posterior flap; P. H. Harmon (1945, USA) — posterolateral approach; P.-J. Farabeuf (France) — racquet method for amputation in the upper third. These classical names are woven into the Bulgarian orthopaedic curriculum through Boychev's textbook and the teaching tradition of the orthopaedic departments in Sofia, Varna, Pleven, and Plovdiv.

</div>
</details>

## Classical Sources
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">
<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical line — surgery of the leg</strong>
  <p>The surgery of the leg in its modern sense was shaped between the Napoleonic wars and the mid-twentieth century. Dominique-Jean Larrey codified field amputations (*Mémoires de chirurgie militaire*, 1812); Joseph-François Malgaigne systematised tibial and fibular fractures in *Traité des fractures et des luxations* (1847); Nikolai Ivanovich Pirogov produced the first comprehensive cross-sectional anatomic atlas of the leg (*Anatome topographica*, 1852–1859) and introduced osteoplastic ankle amputation (1854). The first half of the twentieth century brought the Küntscher intramedullary nail (1939) and the posterolateral approach by Harmon (1945); the second half — Ilizarov's distraction osteogenesis, Phemister's cancellous onlay, Mubarak–Hargens fasciotomy, and Burgess's myoplastic amputation.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Larrey 1812</strong> — *Mémoires de chirurgie militaire et campagnes*, vol. 2 — field amputations of the leg (<a href="https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdechiru02larr">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Malgaigne 1847</strong> — *Traité des fractures et des luxations*, vol. 2 — tibia and fibula (<a href="https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_32985x02">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Pirogov 1852–1859</strong> — *Anatome topographica* — first cross-sectional atlas; osteoplastic ankle amputation (1854).</li>
    <li><strong>Schulten 1896 → Phemister 1947 → Papineau 1973 → Masquelet 1986</strong> — the evolution of treatment of large bone defects from muscle plombage through cancellous onlay to induced membrane.</li>
    <li><strong>Küntscher 1939 → Spizharni 1940s → Zucman 1965 → Zhelev 1975 → modern locked nails</strong> — the evolution of intramedullary osteosynthesis.</li>
    <li><strong>Ilizarov 1951–1971</strong> — the circular external fixator; the principle of "tension-stress" distraction osteogenesis.</li>
    <li><strong>Mubarak–Hargens 1977</strong> — double-incision fasciotomy; the standard for acute compartment syndrome.</li>
    <li><strong>Burgess 1968</strong> — myoplastic below-knee amputation with small anterior and large posterior flap.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>
