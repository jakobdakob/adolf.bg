## Introduction

The ankle and foot together form an intricate anatomical complex with 28 bones, more than 30 joints, and a dense network of tendons, ligaments, and neurovascular structures organized into a region of complex weight-bearing and propulsive function. The surgical approaches to the region must navigate this complexity, with attention to the thin soft-tissue envelope (particularly around the medial and anterior ankle), the principal neurovascular structures (anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve anteriorly, posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve in the tarsal tunnel, saphenous vein and nerve medially, sural nerve laterally, superficial peroneal nerve crossing anterolaterally), and the multiple distinct compartments of the foot. The principal approaches — anterior ankle, anterolateral ankle, posterolateral ankle, medial ankle, sinus tarsi, extensile lateral calcaneal, dorsal foot, plantar foot, and the multiple specific approaches to particular bones and joints, each provide access to specific regions. This chapter draws on Orthopaedic Surgical Approaches, Netter's Concise Orthopaedic Anatomy, Gray's Anatomy, and the classical Bulgarian "Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology" of Boycho Boychev — namesake of the University Hospital for Active Treatment in Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev", Gorna Banya — supplemented with the classical foot-and-ankle procedures of Boychev, Holevich, Paneva, Tchaklin, Zatsepin, Katsarov, Gerchev, Tchokanov, Karchinov, Ikonomov, Codivilla, Lambrinudi, Hoke, Dwyer, Grice-Green, Keller-Brandes, McBride, Lapidus, Lelièvre, Watson-Jones, Chrisman-Snook, Phelps, Strayer, Silfverskiöld, Steindler, Hoffmann, Putti, Charnley, Müller, Stöffel, Heyman-Strong, Lowman, Miller, Camera, Kuslik, Chaplin, Meary, and Syme, which remain the spine of the Bulgarian paediatric and adult foot-and-ankle curriculum.

The ankle joint comprises the tibial plafond, medial malleolus, lateral malleolus (distal fibula), and the talar dome ([Topic Trauma-28](/en/trauma/28/)). The subtalar joint is between the talus (superiorly) and the calcaneus (inferiorly), with three articular facets (anterior, middle, and posterior) separated by the tarsal canal. The midfoot comprises the navicular, cuboid, and three cuneiforms ([Topic Trauma-29](/en/trauma/29/)). The forefoot comprises the five metatarsals and the phalanges (similar to the hand pattern, with two phalanges in the great toe and three in the lesser toes). Key surgical landmarks include the medial and lateral malleoli, sustentaculum tali (medial calcaneus), tibialis anterior tubercle of the navicular, and the fifth metatarsal tuberosity.

## Regional Topographic Anatomy

The classical Bulgarian topographic school divides the ankle and foot into well-delineated zones corresponding to the principal operative planes. The ankle region is separated from the leg by an arched line 7–8 cm proximal to the tip of the medial malleolus; distally the border to the foot runs along an arch connecting the tips of the two malleoli, with anterior convexity 2 cm from the anterior articular margin of the tibia. The ankle region is divided into four sectors: anterior (over the dorsum of the foot), medial (regio malleolaris medialis), lateral (regio malleolaris lateralis), and posterior — regio tendinis calcanei.

In the medial malleolar region the skin is thin and mobile and the subcutaneous tissue is poorly developed. The great saphenous vein ascends along its anterior margin, accompanied most often medially by the saphenous nerve. The anterior medial malleolar artery (a branch of the anterior tibial artery) and medial malleolar branches of the posterior tibial artery form the medial malleolar network. Beneath the superficial fascia lies the superior extensor retinaculum, and slightly more distally the proximal limb of the inferior extensor retinaculum. Between the posterior border of the medial malleolus and the calcaneus lies the flexor retinaculum, which together with the medial malleolar groove forms the medial malleolar canal — containing, anterior to posterior: the tibialis posterior tendon, flexor digitorum longus tendon, posterior neurovascular bundle (posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve), and the flexor hallucis longus tendon. The artery most often lies anterior and medial to the nerve; in rare cases it may be absent and replaced by a communicating branch of the peroneal artery (Adachi variant).

The lateral malleolar region is bordered anteriorly by a line from the anterior margin of the lateral malleolus to the fifth metatarsal tuberosity, and posteriorly by the lateral border of the Achilles tendon. Between the lateral malleolus and the calcaneus the superior and inferior peroneal retinacula form two separate canals for the peroneus longus (posterior) and peroneus brevis (anterior). The peroneus longus tendon crosses the brevis posteriorly, then disappears beneath the abductor digiti minimi pedis to enter the cuboid groove. Both tendons are invested in a common synovial sheath that divides distally.

The regio tendinis calcanei coincides with the Achilles tendon relief, flanked by the two retromalleolar fossae. Anterior to the Achilles is the triangular-pyramid fat-and-connective tissue space with proximal apex (Kager's space), bounded anteriorly by the posterior tibial surface and joint capsule, posteriorly by the paratenon, and laterally and medially by the muscle compartments. Five to seven cm proximal to the subtalar joint the peroneal (fibular) artery gives off the perforating branch, the communicating branch, and the lateral malleolar branches that form the lateral malleolar network; each of these branches must be identified during posterior or posterolateral approaches.

In the anterior ankle region the skin is thin and mobile; the venous network consists of anastomoses between the great and small saphenous veins, and the sensory nerves are branches of the superficial peroneal nerve and the saphenous nerve. The superior extensor retinaculum runs transversely and forms a deep septum separating two osseofibrous canals; the inferior extensor retinaculum has a Y-shape with three limbs and forms three canals for the tendons of the anterior compartment. The anterior tibial vessels and deep peroneal nerve lie in the canal of the extensor hallucis longus, posterior and lateral to its tendon; at this level the nerve is medial to the artery.

The foot is divided into dorsum pedis, planta pedis, and digiti. The medial border between the dorsum and the sole corresponds to the superior border of the abductor hallucis (from the middle of the heel to the head of the first metatarsal); the lateral border corresponds to the superior border of the abductor digiti minimi (to the head of the fifth metatarsal). The toes are separated from the foot by an arch 2.0–2.5 cm proximal to the interdigital folds.

The dorsal venous arch forms over the mid-shaft level of the metatarsals from anastomoses between the two saphenous veins; from it arise the great saphenous (medially, above the navicular tuberosity) and the small saphenous (laterally, around the fifth metatarsal tuberosity and posterior to the lateral malleolus). The superficial peroneal nerve pierces the fascia 10–12 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus and divides into the medial and intermediate dorsal cutaneous nerves — both at risk in dorsal approaches. The sural nerve accompanies the small saphenous vein and continues as the lateral dorsal cutaneous nerve of the foot.

On the dorsum of the foot the dorsalis pedis artery lies between the tendons of the extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus, directly on bone; its branches (lateral tarsal, arcuate, deep plantar branch, first dorsal metatarsal) define the vascular risk during tarsometatarsal approaches.

The plantar space is divided by the plantar aponeurosis and the medial and lateral fascial septa into three fascial compartments — medial, central, and lateral; modern classification adds the calcaneal, the four interosseous, and sometimes the adductor compartments, totalling 9. The plantar canal lies in the proximal part of the central compartment, between the long plantar ligament (dorsally) and the deep plantar fascia (plantarly); it contains the quadratus plantae, the flexor digitorum longus tendon, and the medial and lateral plantar neurovascular bundles, and proximally continues into the calcaneal canal. The medial plantar bundle emerges 7–8 cm distal to the calcaneal tuber, beneath the abductor hallucis; the medial plantar nerve innervates the flexor digitorum brevis, abductor hallucis, flexor hallucis brevis, first lumbrical, and the skin of the medial 3.5 toes. The lateral bundle emerges 4–5 cm distal to the tuber along the lateral border of the abductor hallucis; the lateral plantar nerve innervates the quadratus plantae, abductor digiti minimi, and the skin of the lateral one-and-a-half toes.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Topographic rule for plantar approaches: the medial plantar bundle exits 7–8 cm distal to the calcaneal tuber beneath the abductor hallucis; the lateral bundle 4–5 cm distal to the tuber along the lateral border of the same muscle. For plantar incisions (Steindler stripping, plantar fasciotomy, plantar decompression in compartment syndrome) the incision remains on the medial non-weight-bearing border, does not extend more than 3 cm anterior to the heel apex, and is always developed with a blunt periosteal elevator on bone to spare the bundle.</p>
</aside>

Between metatarsals the dorsal metatarsal arteries are protected by the dorsal interossei and are not vulnerable to typical longitudinal dorsal incisions; 1–1.5 cm proximal to the interdigital folds the dorsal metatarsal arteries and nerves divide into the proper dorsal digital branches — the bifurcation projects at the level of the metatarsophalangeal joints.

The anterior ankle structures, from medial to lateral, are: tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus (EHL), anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve, extensor digitorum longus (EDL), peroneus tertius. The medial ankle contains the tarsal tunnel posterior to the medial malleolus, with the contents (deep to the flexor retinaculum, from anterior to posterior): tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve, flexor hallucis longus, the “Tom, Dick, And a Nervous Harry” mnemonic.

The lateral ankle contains the peroneus brevis anteriorly and peroneus longus posteriorly behind the lateral malleolus, with the sural nerve running posterolaterally. The superficial peroneal nerve crosses the anterolateral ankle and dorsum of the foot subcutaneously, providing most of the dorsal foot sensation; injury produces a recognizable sensory deficit and is a recognized concern in anterolateral ankle approaches. The saphenous nerve descends along the medial side of the ankle and foot, providing medial sensation; injury produces medial dorsal sensory deficit.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The superficial peroneal nerve becomes subcutaneous and crosses the anterolateral ankle to supply most of the dorsum of the foot — identify and protect it during anterior and anterolateral ankle approaches. The deep peroneal nerve supplies first webspace sensation only; the sural nerve supplies the lateral border; the saphenous, the medial.</p>
</aside>

The anterior ankle approach is used for anterior ankle arthrodesis, total ankle arthroplasty, distal tibial osteotomy, tumor resection, and other anterior procedures. A longitudinal incision is made over the anterior ankle in the line of the second metatarsal, approximately 10 to 15 cm in length. The superficial peroneal nerve crosses the lateral aspect of the incision (most surgeons make the skin incision medial to the SPN crossings). The deep dissection develops the interval between the tibialis anterior (medial) and the extensor hallucis longus / extensor digitorum longus (lateral), with the anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve retracted laterally. The ankle capsule is opened to access the joint.

## Anterolateral Ankle Approach

The anterolateral approach is the standard for lateral plafond (pilon) fractures ([Topic Trauma-27](/en/trauma/27/)). A longitudinal incision is made over the anterolateral ankle, between the EHL/EDL medially and the peroneus tertius laterally. The deep peroneal nerve and the anterior tibial vessels are identified and retracted medially. The ankle joint and the distal tibia are exposed. The approach provides excellent visualization of the anterior ankle joint and the distal tibial articular surface.

## Posterolateral Ankle Approach

The posterolateral approach is used for posterior malleolar fracture fixation, posterior pilon fractures, and exposure of the peroneal tendons and the lateral aspect of the posterior ankle. A longitudinal incision is made over the posterolateral leg behind the fibula. The sural nerve and small saphenous vein are identified and protected. The interval between the peroneal tendons (laterally) and the flexor hallucis longus (medially) is developed, with the FHL retracted medially. The posterior aspect of the ankle joint and the posterior malleolus are exposed.

## Posterior Para-Achilles and Trans-Achilles Approaches

The para-Achilles posterior approach runs along the lateral edge of the Achilles tendon; the tendon is retracted medially and in the depth the joint capsule and the posterior aspects of the tibia, talus and calcaneus are exposed. It is convenient for posterior pilon, posterior capsulotomy in equinus, and access to the posterior process of the talus.

The trans-Achilles approach (Boychev, Holevich) runs along the lateral edge of the tendon to its insertion, the tendon is incised in a Z-pattern in the frontal plane and the two limbs are retracted proximally and distally. Through Kager's fat pad the dissection enters the interval between the flexor hallucis longus (medially) and the peronei (laterally); via this route the posterior surfaces of the talus and calcaneus and the subtalar joint are exposed. The posterior tibial neurovascular bundle lies anterior to the flexor hallucis longus, so dissection medial to that muscle is discouraged. At closure the Achilles is repaired end-to-end with heavy non-absorbable sutures (Bunnell or Krackow technique).

## Medial Trans-Malleolar (Osteotomy) Approach

The medial trans-malleolar approach is used for osteochondral lesions of the medial talar dome, certain pilon fracture patterns, and ankle arthrodesis with malleolar resection. The patient is supine with external rotation of the limb. A gently arched transverse incision is made at the base of the malleolus; the bone is osteotomized before the joint dissection so that the soft-tissue attachments (deltoid) remain intact. The malleolus is reflected distally with the deltoid ligament, opening the joint widely. At closure the malleolus is refixed with a malleolar screw or 1–2 K-wires.

## Bimalleolar Approach of Holevich

The Holevich bimalleolar approach provides maximum exposure of the tibiotalar joint for complex arthroplasty, talar tumour resection, and difficult ankle arthrodesis. A longitudinal 5–6 cm incision is made over each malleolus, reaching the tip. Both malleoli are depriostied and obliquely osteotomized at their bases — the osteotome is directed at about 45° toward the joint line into the upper corners of the mortise. The ligaments along the anterior and posterior borders of each malleolus are incised, and the malleoli are reflected distally, "hanging" on the ligaments attached to their tips. The tibiotalar joint thus opens panoramically. At closure the malleoli are returned and fixed with screws; the lateral malleolus may receive an additional cerclage as needed. This approach remains the Bulgarian classical choice for total ankle arthroplasty and for osteochondral lesions of the posteromedial half of the tibial plafond.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The Boychev-Holevich trans-Achilles posterior approach remains the widest posterior exposure of the tibiotalar joint. Z-incision of the tendon in the frontal plane preserves length after anatomic repair; the posterior neurovascular bundle lies anterior to the flexor hallucis longus, so entry into Kager's space is always lateral to this muscle. The tibial nerve and posterior tibial artery may be injured by dissection passing medial to FHL.</p>
</aside>

## Medial Ankle Approach

The medial ankle approach is used for medial malleolus fracture fixation, deltoid ligament repair, tarsal tunnel release, and posterior tibial tendon procedures. A longitudinal or curved incision is made over the medial malleolus and the medial ankle, depending on the planned procedure.

For medial malleolus fixation, a vertical incision over the malleolus provides direct access. For tarsal tunnel release, a curved incision behind the medial malleolus provides access to the tarsal tunnel; the flexor retinaculum is incised, decompressing the tibial nerve. For posterior tibial tendon procedures (PTTD reconstruction with FDL transfer), a longitudinal incision along the course of the tendon provides access from the medial malleolus to the navicular insertion.

## Sinus Tarsi Approach to the Calcaneus

The sinus tarsi approach has largely supplanted the extensile lateral approach for calcaneal fracture fixation ([Topic Trauma-29](/en/trauma/29/)), because of substantially lower wound complication rates. A small oblique incision is made over the sinus tarsi (a depression palpable just anterior to the lateral malleolus). The peroneal tendons are retracted, and the calcaneocuboid joint and the posterior facet of the subtalar joint are exposed.

## Extensile Lateral Approach to the Calcaneus

The extensile lateral approach (Letournel/Benirschke) is the historical standard for calcaneal fracture fixation. An L-shaped or hockey-stick incision is made along the lateral calcaneus, with vertical and horizontal limbs. The skin and subcutaneous tissue are reflected, with the peroneal tendons elevated as a single soft-tissue flap. The lateral wall of the calcaneus is exposed for plating. The wound complication rates of 5 to 25 percent have led to the substantial preference for the sinus tarsi approach.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>The extensile lateral L-shaped approach to the calcaneus carries 5-25% wound complications because the entire lateral flap with overlying skin is reflected on the lateral calcaneal artery — late-presenting fractures, smokers, diabetics, and open fractures should sway choice toward the smaller sinus tarsi approach.</p>
</aside>

## Approaches in the Tarsal Region

The anteromedial approach to the talus runs longitudinally lateral to the tibialis anterior tendon; the interval may be developed either between the extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus, or between the tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus, with the extensor hallucis brevis swept laterally in the depth. Beneath these muscles the dorsalis pedis artery lies directly on bone between the EHL and EDL tendons and must be preserved.

The medial approach to the calcaneus (Katsarov) runs distal to the medial malleolus, curving toward the Achilles insertion. It is used for drainage of a talocalcaneal cyst, osteomyelitis, and medialising calcaneal osteotomy (see below). The horseshoe posterior approach below the Achilles insertion exposes the plantar surface of the calcaneal tuberosity for resection of a calcaneal spur (Du Vries) and in Haglund's disease.

The L-shaped Kocher approach to the calcaneus (T-shaped in the Bulgarian literature) combines a horizontal limb from the sustentaculum tali to the fifth metatarsal tuberosity with a vertical limb along the lateral edge of the Achilles; it is used for classical extra-articular subtalar arthrodesis and for wide exposure of the calcaneocuboid joint.

The approaches to the cuneiforms and the navicular run longitudinally along the dorsomedial surface of the foot; for the medial cuneiform — along the medial border of the extensor hallucis longus tendon with medial retraction of the tibialis anterior. For the cuboid — an incision along the superior border of the peroneus brevis, retracting the tendon downward and the lateral border of the extensor digitorum brevis upward.

The Kocher posterolateral approach to the tarsal joints begins 5–7 cm proximal to the ankle, about 2 cm behind the fibula; it runs parallel to the fibula, curves arch-like 2 cm below the tip of the lateral malleolus and continues over the dorsum of the foot to the navicular. The sural nerve is usually identified and protected in the subcutaneous tissue. This approach simultaneously exposes the subtalar, calcaneocuboid, and talonavicular joints and is the traditional approach for triple arthrodesis.

The medial longitudinal approach to the medial column begins at the posterior border of the medial malleolus, curves around it, and continues distally along the first metatarsal. The flexor retinaculum and the inferior extensor retinaculum are incised; the tibialis anterior and tibialis posterior tendons are retracted dorsally and plantarly to expose all the bones and joints of the medial border of the foot. The approach is convenient for tendon work — particularly posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD) reconstruction with FDL transfer (Mann) and medial column arthrodesis.

## Approaches in the Metatarsal Region

The approach to the first metatarsal is dorsomedial; above the extensor hallucis longus tendon, medial to the origin of the great saphenous vein. Dissection is subperiosteal; laterally the long extensor tendon is retracted with the great saphenous vein and the medial dorsal digital nerve of the hallux, medially the abductor hallucis.

The approaches to metatarsals II–V are longitudinal on the dorsal surface or in the corresponding intermetatarsal spaces; used for correction of hammer toes, osteotomies, and fracture fixation. The recommended approach is lateral to the long extensor tendon to spare the dorsal digital nerves.

The longitudinal approaches to the tarsometatarsal joints (Lisfranc) cross the joint projection line along the metatarsal axis. For the first joint the incision runs medial to the long extensor of the hallux; for the second — between the long extensors of the first and second toes, with medial retraction of the extensor hallucis brevis and ligation of the terminal dorsalis pedis and the beginning of the arcuate artery; for the third — medial or lateral to the long extensor of the third toe, with ligation of the arcuate artery above the joint; for the fourth — lateral to its long extensor; for the fifth — lateral to the long extensor of the fifth toe. The sensory nerves over the joints run longitudinally parallel to the extensor tendons and usually coincide with the incisions.

## Approaches to the Toes

The approaches to the first MTP joint (for hallux valgus, hallux rigidus, rheumatoid forefoot surgery) may be medial, dorsomedial, or dorsal. The medial begins 2–2.5 cm distal to the MTP-1 joint, curves arch-wise over the dorsal surface of the joint medial to the extensor hallucis longus, and continues proximally to the mid-shaft of the first metatarsal. A capsular flap may be raised, attached to the base of the proximal phalanx. The dorsomedial approach disrupts less plantar skin and vascular supply and is often preferred.

For the lesser MTP joints (II–V) the incisions are dorsolateral, parallel to the extensor tendons; for the interphalangeal joints of the first and fifth toes — medial and lateral; for the interphalangeal joints of the II–IV toes — immediately lateral and parallel to the extensor tendons in the dorsal aponeurosis. The dorsolateral neurovascular bundle is retracted laterally and the extensor elements medially; the proper dorsal and plantar digital bundles converge at the level of the proximal interphalangeal joints, so incisions at this level are placed between the joint line and the nerve.

## Approaches to the Foot


## Dorsal Foot Approaches

Dorsal foot incisions are used for forefoot procedures (bunion surgery, lesser toe deformity correction, foot fracture fixation). The incisions are placed to avoid the dorsal sensory nerves (branches of the superficial peroneal nerve) and the dorsalis pedis artery and deep peroneal nerve (which runs in the first dorsal interspace).

## Medial Foot Approach

A medial foot incision along the medial border of the foot provides access to the medial column (navicular, medial cuneiform, first metatarsal base). The approach is used for medial column fractures, hallux valgus surgery, and medial column arthrodesis.

## Lateral Foot Approach

A lateral foot incision along the lateral border provides access to the cuboid, the fourth and fifth metatarsals, and the lateral column. The approach is used for cuboid fracture fixation, lateral column lengthening, and lateral column reconstructive procedures.


## Plantar Approaches

Plantar incisions are generally avoided because of the thick plantar fat and the risk of painful scars on the weight-bearing surface. When necessary (for plantar fascia release, deep plantar abscess), incisions are placed along the medial or lateral plantar non-weight-bearing surface.

## Foot Compartments and Fasciotomy

The foot has 9 compartments (medial, central, lateral, calcaneal, and four interosseous, with some classification variants). Foot compartment syndrome requires recognition and fasciotomy through dorsal incisions (over the second and fourth metatarsals to release the dorsal and palmar interossei) with additional medial incision if the medial compartment is involved.

For acute lateral ligament injury, operative indication is based on stress radiographs: lateral talar tilt of more than 10° or anterior subluxation of the talus. The classical Bulgarian criterion of Hackenbruch and Hoesterberg (1966) requires more than 6 mm of lateral joint opening.

The Staples-Black-Brostrom technique uses an L-shaped skin incision over the lateral malleolus; the ATFL and CFL are repaired directly with non-absorbable sutures through bone tunnels in the fibula for pure avulsion injuries. The Brostrom-Gould modification adds augmentation with the lateral extensor retinaculum, transferred proximally over the repair.

Chronic lateral instability requires a more robust reconstruction. The classical modified Watson-Jones technique is performed through an arched incision from the junction of the middle and distal third of the fibula to 5 cm anterior to the tip of the lateral malleolus; the peroneus brevis tendon is cut high at the musculotendinous junction and passed through a tunnel in the distal fibula from posterior to anterior, then through a second tunnel in the talar neck, and sutured to itself — the construct reconstructs the ATFL direction.

The Chrisman-Snook technique uses a long lateral incision from 7.5 cm proximal to the malleolar tip to the base of the fifth metatarsal; the peroneus brevis tendon is split longitudinally in two halves, one half is withdrawn beneath the retinacula and passed through a horizontal tunnel in the lateral malleolus and through the calcaneus, reconstructing both the ATFL and CFL — the technique controls both ankle and subtalar instability.

The Bulgarian Kopchev technique uses a flap from the Achilles tendon; silk sutures repair the Achilles with a lateral fascial flap, while two vertical parallel canals and one horizontal tunnel are made through the lateral malleolus between the calcaneal tuber and the lateral talar process, through which a tendon flap is passed, reconstructing the CFL and ATFL.

## Reconstruction of Recurrent Peroneal Tendon Dislocation

For recurrent peroneal tendon dislocation (absent or shallow fibular groove, torn superior peroneal retinaculum) a 10–12 cm incision is made parallel to the fibula behind the lateral malleolus. A wide U-shaped flap is raised from the deep leg fascia with its base on the malleolus. The retromalleolar groove is cleared and deepened with a chisel if necessary; the tendons are reduced and covered with the preformed fascial flap, which is sutured to the remnants of the retinaculum or to the lateral calcaneal periosteum.

## Malleolar Fracture Fixation

For the lateral malleolus, an anterolateral longitudinal incision is used with care for the sural nerve. For an oblique fracture line (supination–external rotation or pronation–external rotation mechanisms), the fragments are compressed with two interfragmentary screws from anterior to posterior. For a transverse fracture with avulsion of the calcaneofibular ligament, tension-band wiring is performed — cerclage and K-wire through the malleolar tip. For comminution — lateral plating (a one-third tubular, reconstruction, or anatomic distal fibular plate).

For the medial malleolus, an anterior-L-shaped incision is preferred, allowing inspection of the anteromedial joint surface (to rule out osteochondral lesions). Small avulsion fragments are fixed with a pull-out suture or anchor; larger fragments — with two 4.0 mm cancellous screws driven from the malleolar tip upward into the distal tibia. Periosteum may be interposed between the fragments and must be removed before reduction.

Sequence in bimalleolar fractures: in pronation–abduction mechanisms, the lateral malleolus is fixed first, because fibular reduction automatically realigns the medial malleolus. Supination–adduction injuries are the exception: the medial malleolus is fixed first.

## Syndesmosis Repair

According to C. Heim (1964), conservatively treated torn syndesmosis leads in 56% of cases to recurrent mortise insufficiency and subsequent arthritis. Therefore, the syndesmosis must be repaired and avulsion fragments refixed. An approximately 5 cm incision is made parallel to the anterior border of the distal fibula; 1–4 cm proximal to the joint line, a positioning screw (3.5 mm) is passed through the fibula into the tibia in 30° of dorsiflexion and maximal compression. The screw engages 3 or 4 cortices according to preference and is removed at around 12 weeks before full weight bearing. For chronic syndesmotic insufficiency — plastic reconstruction with fascia (Heim, 1964), with the peroneus brevis tendon (Petracic-Dürr, 1976), or with heterologous tendon (Voorhoeve-Vriemoed, 1973). Permanent intersyndesmotic screws without removal lead to tibiofibular synostosis, which also provokes arthritis.

## Posterior Malleolus (Volkmann Triangle)

When the fragment involves more than one-third of the tibial articular surface, it must be anatomically reduced and fixed. Restoration of fibular length frequently reduces the Volkmann triangle via the posterior tibiofibular ligament. Definitive fixation is by an anterior-to-posterior cancellous screw inserted through a separate small anterior incision, proximal to the joint line, with care for the extensor tendons. Alternatively — antiglide plating through a posterolateral approach.

## Trimalleolar and Pilon Fractures — Operative Strategy

For severely comminuted pilon fractures the principle of M. E. Müller (1970) is: first restore fibular length through an anterolateral approach (fibula serves as an axis reference for tibial alignment); then through a second anteromedial approach the tibial plafond is reduced with interfragmentary screws and a medial tibial plate. Voids are filled with autograft from the iliac crest. The minimum distance between the two skin incisions is 7 cm to preserve the skin bridge vascularity.

For talar neck fracture a medial osteotomy approach or combined dual approach (anteromedial + anterolateral) is used; anatomic reduction and fixation with two cancellous screws or a screw-and-wire combination (Ikonomov technique) — from medial-distal toward dorsal into the trochlea. For talar body fractures — a medial trans-malleolar approach with osteotomy of the medial malleolus and screw or K-wire fixation. For posterior process fractures — a posterolateral incision between the Achilles and peroneal tendons with dorsal capsulotomy and fixation with microscrews or K-wire.

For comminuted talar fracture, primary astragalectomy is classically performed. To prevent shortening and instability, H. C. Blair (1943) proposed a tibiotalar arthrodesis with a cortical graft from the anterior distal tibia, slid into a preformed slot in the talar neck — Blair's technique remains a historical option, clinically applicable when the talar head and neck are relatively preserved.

## Ankle Arthrodesis

Ankle arthrodesis remains the principal solution in advanced arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, post-traumatic deformity, paralytic instability, talar AVN (stage III–IV), and after failed arthroplasty. Arthrodesis relieves pain and provides stability at the cost of motion loss; compensatory motion at the subtalar and midtarsal joints increases and gait remains functional.

The classical intra-articular arthrodesis of Schwartz (1946) uses the L-shaped Kocher lateral approach; Charnley uses the anterolateral; Barr-Record (1953) uses two anterior incisions over each malleolus. The articular cartilages are removed to bleeding subchondral bone; the void is filled with cancellous chips from the iliac crest or ipsilateral tibia. Chuinard-Petersen (1963) described a technique preserving the medial and lateral malleoli as natural buttresses after articular cartilage resection.

Compression arthrodesis of Charnley (1951) — refined by Müller (1977) — uses two longitudinal incisions, one over each malleolus. An oblique osteotomy of the lateral malleolus about 3 cm from its tip is performed, the medial malleolus, the distal tibial articular surface, and the trochlea of the talus are resected parallel to the tibial plafond. The arthrodesis is positioned at 90° in women and 10° dorsiflexion in men (corresponding to heel height). Two Steinmann pins are placed — the first through the calcaneus and the second transversely through the tibia; a Charnley external compression fixator provides dynamic compression with initial loading from days 5–6. From the 1980s onward, the compression-distraction self-regulating systems of Ilizarov and related devices provide both dosed correction and compression.

The Putti technique uses an anterior midline approach; the skin wound is opened together with the tendons. If needed, the tibialis anterior is incised. The capsule is opened transversely, the malleoli are obliquely osteotomized, the articular cartilages are freshened, and after 2–3 cm distraction a cortical graft from the tibia is inserted, fixed with a screw or supported by a bone bridge.

Anterior lateral plate arthrodesis (R. Anderson 1945, R. Gärtner 1910, W. Lasker 1923) uses the anterolateral approach; after articular cartilage resection, a corticocancellous graft from the distal tibia with a sharpened tip is driven into the trochlea (Adams triangular graft — the joint capsule is opened transversely, surfaces resected, and bony defects filled with autograft). The Crawford-Adams technique is a modification with multilevel resection and a wide anterior plate.

Posterior tibial bone graft from tibia to calcaneus is an extra-articular arthrodesis (Brittain modification), historically used for tuberculous ankle and in cases where intra-articular fusion is not feasible.

## Total Ankle Arthroplasty

Arthrodesis remains the mainstay, but for selected patients (older with bilateral disease, rheumatoid arthritis, adjacent foot pathology), total ankle arthroplasty preserves motion. The classical 1970s–1980s designs — Smith (with a biaxial component), St. Georg (Buchholz), Mayo, Newton, Imperial College London (Kempson), TPR (Pappas), Schöll (Scholz), Buechel-Pappas (1973), and Kofoed (1980s) — introduced the principle of a semi-constrained tibiotalar interface with a polyethylene insert.

The typical operative position is lateral (on the healthy side) with Esmarch tourniquet. The approach is usually the Kocher lateral with sural nerve protection. The fibula is depriostied and transversely osteotomized at the planned tibial resection level; before this, a temporary malleolar screw is passed through the fibula into the tibia to preserve the length reference. The calcaneus is fixed with a transverse K-wire and a distraction frame for supination. With an oscillating saw the distal tibial surface is cut at the level of the mid-syndesmosis; the trochlea is cut parallel. Tibial and talar components are placed with a polyethylene insert. After fibular refixation with the malleolar screw, the tibiofibular ligaments and retinacula are repaired. Early rehabilitation includes a boot for 4–6 weeks and progressive weight-bearing.

Relative contraindications: severe deformity (>20° varus or valgus), prior infection, Charcot, severe osteoporosis, low BMI, and a young active patient. The arthroplasty revolution in Bulgaria (1990s–2000s) restricted arthrodesis to severe deformities and young active patients.

Transverse plantar fasciotomy (Steindler stripping in its classical form) is used in pes cavus as part of soft-tissue correction. A 3–4 cm incision is made along the plantar-medial border in the middle third of the foot; the medial border of the fascia is identified, separated from the subcutaneous tissue and underlying muscles, and at passive dorsiflexion (which tightens the fascia) it is transected across its full width. Experienced surgeons may perform a closed fasciotomy with a tenotome.

Detachment of the plantar fascia and short plantar flexors from the calcaneal tuberosity per Steindler is the key step in operative correction of rigid pes cavus. Through a medial incision curving toward the calcaneal tuberosity (no more than 3–4 cm to avoid injuring the medial plantar nerve), a scalpel and periosteal elevator are advanced along the medial and plantar surfaces of the calcaneus. The muscles are elevated with a retractor, the scalpel slides along the bone, and the plantar fascia plus the short plantar muscles (abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, abductor digiti minimi) are detached as a single muscle-fascial block from the tuber.

### Tendon and Muscle Transfers

Transfers for residual paralysis (post-polio, peripheral nerve injuries, cerebral palsy, congenital and acquired deformities) require: no fixed contracture, agonist action of the donor tendon, preserved structure and strength of the donor, and a smooth tendon course.

Transfer of the tibialis posterior to the dorsum of the foot (Watkins-Barr) for paralytic equinus to provide active dorsiflexion. Four incisions: (1) medial over the navicular for tendon detachment with or without a bony fragment; (2) above the medial malleolus along the posterior tibia — for retrograde delivery into a subcutaneous tunnel along the anterior leg; (3) just above the ankle for a wide window in the interosseous membrane and anterior passage of the tendon; (4) dorsally over the appropriate metatarsal (usually II–III) for subperiosteal insertion under tension. Alternatively, the tendon may be transferred to the lateral cuneiform or the base of the fifth metatarsal, depending on the valgus–varus balance.

Transfer of both plantar flexors (tibialis posterior and flexor hallucis longus) to the foot — Tchaklin-Kaplan technique — is performed through a longitudinal posteromedial leg incision. The interosseous membrane is incised; the tibialis posterior tendon is passed along the medial border of the foot and sutured into a slot in the first metatarsal; the flexor hallucis longus tendon is passed through the membrane and inserted into the fifth metatarsal or cuboid (stabilising the lateral column); flexor digitorum longus, if transferred, is inserted into the navicular. A tenodesis of the long extensors to the periosteum of the metatarsals completes the construct. Cast immobilisation for 3 weeks in ankle dorsiflexion and moderate knee flexion.

For triceps surae palsy substitution transfers are used; the principal posterior midline incision along the Achilles is extended proximally to the middle third of the leg. Donor tendons (tibialis anterior, peroneus longus, flexor digitorum communis, and/or flexor hallucis longus) are routed posteriorly and fixed either through a transverse calcaneal tunnel (requiring prior division and re-suturing of the Achilles) or directly to the Achilles tendon or its insertion.

### Tenodeses of the Foot

Tenodesis (passive stabilisation) restricts unwanted joint motion when an agonist muscle is unavailable for active action.

Peroneus brevis tenodesis for paralytic pes varus: an incision along the peroneals, transection of the peroneus brevis 5–6 cm above the ankle, passage through a transverse tunnel in the lateral malleolus and suture in pronated position.

Extensor tenodesis (Tchaklin): transection of the tibialis anterior and long extensors at the upper end of the incision; a transverse canal is made in the tibia about 5 cm above the joint, through which the tendons are passed against each other and sutured under tension. Tchaklin uses two superimposed canals; Camera routes the tendons through two additional tarsal canals to shorten them before tibial anchoring.

Anterior tenodesis of Holevich: detachment of peroneus brevis, peroneus longus, and tibialis anterior — all brought anteriorly and sutured to each other and to themselves through an anterior tibial incision. Possible modifications: (a) for mild forms the tendons are routed through the interosseous membrane without compromising the Achilles; (b) in Achilles retraction it is split frontally (separating the soleus and gastrocnemius components), with gastrocnemius routed anteriorly through the membrane and sutured into the anti-flexion construct.

Posterior tenodesis of Boychev with Achilles shortening: the Achilles is detached from the calcaneus, split longitudinally in two, a transverse canal is drilled in the tuber calcanei, the two limbs are passed against each other through the canal and sutured under tension. This simultaneously shortens the tendon and creates a tenodesis.

Posterior tenodesis of Zatsepin: a flap from the medial half of the Achilles is passed through a canal in the medial malleolus 6 cm above the joint and sutured at equinus.

For fixed equinus deformity in pes equinus, recurrent clubfoot, spastic cerebral palsy without fibrotic triceps surae, residual polio deformities, and congenital shortening.

Open Z-lengthening: a 10 cm posteromedial incision; the Achilles is dissected and Z-cut in the sagittal or frontal plane, the distal cut in the posteromedial half (protecting the medial vascular supply) and the proximal in the posterolateral. After passive dorsiflexion the two limbs are sutured at controlled lengthening with horizontal mattress sutures.

Hoke percutaneous technique (1931): three transverse hemitenotomies — two medial and one lateral (or vice versa) at staggered heights (usually 2–3 cm apart); at passive dorsiflexion the tendon "slides" along its two halves and lengthens. The percutaneous technique is the standard within the Ponseti protocol for congenital clubfoot (TAL — tendo Achillis lengthening in a child under 1 year).

White technique: incomplete transverse division of the tendon at two different levels (distally in the lateral two-thirds, proximally 5–8 cm higher in the medial two-thirds) and passive longitudinal splitting at dorsiflexion. An elegant semi-open percutaneous alternative.

Strayer gastrocnemius recession (1950): through a transverse posterior incision in the upper half of the leg, the gastrocnemius aponeurosis is selectively transected at its junction with the soleus, without engaging the soleus. Indication: isolated equinus from gastrocnemius (positive Silfverskiöld test — equinus that resolves with knee flexion). The Silfverskiöld original detachment of both gastrocnemius heads from the femoral condyles (1923) — converting the muscle from biarticular to monoarticular with action equivalent to the soleus — is increasingly rarely used in modern practice.

## Phelps and Stoffel Neurectomy

Phelps' tibial nerve neurectomy is an alternative in spastic equinus deformity without fibrotic changes in the triceps surae. Through a posterior 7–8 cm incision in the popliteal fossa the tibial nerve (superficial to the vessels) and its three motor branches to the gastrocnemius (medial and lateral) are identified, with sparing of the sensory branch. Mechanical or electrical stimulation identifies the branches to be cut — usually only the hyperactive heads. Over-denervation causes complete motor loss and is not recommended.

Stoffel described selective peripheral motor neurectomy on the same principle — only the hyperactive motor branches are cut, the test performed with bipolar stimulation and visible contraction.

The classical Codivilla method (1906) is staged in three. Stage I: a medial longitudinal incision from the first metatarsal, curving around the medial malleolus and continuing proximally along the leg; lengthening of the tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus, opening of the talonavicular, naviculocuneiform and cuneo-metatarsal joints from the medial side, transection of the contracted deltoid ligament. Stage II: lateral opening of the calcaneocuboid joint and, when necessary, of the subtalar joint; if resistance is encountered — Z-lengthening of the Achilles and posterior capsulotomy. Stage III: passive correction of the foot into neutral, followed by anatomic restoration of the tendons with appropriate lengthening. Immobilisation for 4–6 weeks.

The Zatsepin method — the iconic Russian/Soviet standard for congenital pes equino-varus correction — is performed through a vertical incision through the middle of the medial malleolus. The tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus tendons are Z-cut; the deltoid ligament is transversely divided; the joint is widely opened. The talocalcaneal interosseous ligament and other rigid structures are precisely cut. If needed, the flexor hallucis longus is lengthened. The tendons are repaired under tension so the heel is held in neutral. For forefoot adduction correction, a second incision at the base of the proximal phalanx of the hallux allows lengthening of the abductor and short flexor of the hallux. Immobilisation is without hypercorrection (to avoid skin necrosis); the cast is changed on day 10–12 for supination correction; gradual extension to 6 months.

The Gerchev method (Bulgarian school) is staged in four. Stage I: medial incision from the first metatarsal base to the middle-lower third of the leg; transection and lengthening of the tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus; opening of the medial joints. Stage II: lateral incision along the Achilles; Z-lengthening in the sagittal plane with detachment of the medial half from the tuber. Stage III: if the heel remains high in equinus due to residual anterior talar subluxation, a small transverse incision below the lateral malleolar tip allows transection of the posterior portions of the lateral ligaments and reduction of the talus into the mortise. Stage IV: with the foot in full correction, tendons are repaired under tension and incisions closed.

The Katsarov method for recurrent pes equino-varus combines surgery with frame correction (Ilizarov-type distraction apparatus). The Achilles is Z-lengthened, the tibialis posterior is lengthened, the tibialis anterior is detached for later transfer; three crossing K-wires are passed through the heel, midtarsus and forefoot, mounted on the distraction apparatus, and from day 2 progressive correction of equinus, adduction and supination plus foot lengthening begins. After correction — interposition (cancellous autografts in the subtalar zone for extra-articular arthrodesis).

Medial skin lengthening by Z-plasty is performed with opposing 60° triangular flaps; the lengthening formula governs the gain, since excessive tension causes necrosis.

The Stöffel staged correction is indicated in adolescents and adults with very rigid equino-varus feet, where wedge bony resections would shorten the limb excessively. It is performed in three stages a month apart: stage I — soft-tissue release; stage II — Z-lengthening of the Achilles and lateral transfer of the tibialis anterior; stage III — wedge resection of the subtalar and midtarsal joints with detachment of the peroneal tendons. The advantage is a minimal bone wedge, because the soft tissues have already "released" the correction.

In true congenital pes plano-valgus (vertical talus, congenital "rocker-bottom"), the talus is rotated plantar-medially, its head dislocated medioplantar from the navicular. Classical open reduction requires repositioning the talus in the mortise, replacing the navicular in front of the talar head, and correcting calcaneal pronation.

Lowman method: medial approach to the talonavicular joint; the talonavicular ligament is divided at the joint level; the navicular is isolated and from it (and partly from the talar head) a bone wedge is cut. If needed, the Achilles is lengthened. The tibialis anterior tendon is transferred into a slot in the inferior surface of the navicular for active longitudinal arch support.

Hoke method (1931) for plano-valgus: creation of an arthrodesis between talus, navicular and medial cuneiform; the tibialis posterior tendon is moved forward (to the tibialis anterior level) onto the tubercle of the first metatarsal, lengthening its lever arm and reinforcing active arch support.

Miller-Niedereker method (Cole-Miller): a double arthrodesis between talus, navicular and medial cuneiform, plus anterior transfer of the tibialis posterior and Achilles lengthening.

Lelièvre method: subtalar arthroereisis through a lateral incision from the posterior heel to the cuboid, directed dorsally; entry into the sinus tarsi and opening of the subtalar joint; from the anterior part of the calcaneus an osteotomy is made directed posteroinferior, and a corticocancellous graft from the tibia is placed in the sinus tarsi, limiting calcaneal pronation and thus correcting valgus.

Chambers method — subtalar arthroereisis to limit calcaneal pronation. A lateral incision from the posterior heel to the cuboid; entry into the sinus tarsi; from the anterior calcaneus an osteotomy directed posteroinferior, and an iliac crest graft in the sinus tarsi blocks eversion. Arthroereisis is particularly suited to paediatric flexible flat foot with the calcaneal apophysis still open.

The Bulgarian Karchinov modification of the Young method: a curved medial incision to the medial malleolus; the tibialis anterior tendon is cut 2 cm above its navicular insertion and reflected proximally; beneath the navicular and medial cuneiform tuberosities a slot is created; the tendon is pulled posteriorly and downward into the slot beneath the distal portion of the tibialis posterior tendon. The tibialis posterior is repaired under tension. Immobilisation 6–8 weeks.

Grice-Green extra-articular subtalar arthrodesis (1952) — the iconic paediatric procedure for plano-valgus correction without disturbing calcaneal growth. A short arched incision over the sinus tarsi; excision of soft tissues from the subtalar surfaces; transection of the talocalcaneal interosseous ligament; transverse slots are made in the posterior part of the talar neck (inferior surface) and the anterior part of the upper calcaneal surface, into which a corticocancellous graft from the iliac crest or fibula (3 cm long) is placed. The graft blocks the subtalar joint in neutral while preserving growth. Immobilisation 3 months; the cast is staged down with progressive weight bearing.

Wedge metatarsotomy — a linear incision along the medial foot centred over the navicular; a wedge across the entire foot width through the midtarsus; corrects planus and forefoot abduction simultaneously.

## Correction of Pes Excavatus (Cavus)

The various plantar release procedures (Steindler stripping) are described above. Bony correction can be performed in several combinations:

Kuslik lateral crescent-shaped tarsectomy: a dorsolateral arched incision; exposure of the talar head, navicular and cuboid; a wide gouge cuts a crescent-shaped wedge from the midtarsus. The foot is realigned. With concomitant heel varus, a wedge resection of the subtalar joint is added.

Combined Tchaklin technique: two longitudinal incisions on the dorsum over Chopart's joint; retraction of the extensors and dorsalis pedis; opening of Chopart's joint and excision of a dorsally based wedge, including the talar head, navicular, anterior calcaneus and part of the cuboid. If equinus persists — Achilles lengthening. For varus — lateral transfer of the tibialis anterior over the dorsum.

Lefèvre and Cole wedge resections — variants of tarsectomy with different wedge orientations.

Dwyer calcaneal osteotomy (1959): for heel varus that cannot be corrected by other methods; suitable from age 3–4. A linear calcaneal osteotomy with opening or closing of the wedge. "Opening" Dwyer — incision below and behind the medial malleolus; a linear osteotomy beneath the sustentaculum tali and behind the posterior facet directed posterior-superior; opening with chisel and placement of a corticocancellous wedge. "Closing" Dwyer — incision behind the lateral malleolus; depriosteation of the lateral calcaneus; an elliptical wedge with apex medial, closing for varus correction with internal fixation.

## Triple Arthrodesis

Triple arthrodesis (fusion of the subtalar, talonavicular, and calcaneocuboid joints in a single bone block) is the standard correction of rigid three-joint hindfoot deformity (pes cavus, pes plano-valgus, post-traumatic, post-polio, recurrent clubfoot). A large portion of the talus loses its blood supply and is treated as a free graft, prolonging consolidation and risking avascular necrosis.

Hoke method (1921, original): a lateral incision from the talar head posteriorly to the tip of the lateral malleolus; division of the talonavicular ligament and wide opening of the talonavicular joint; subtalar joint resection; transverse osteotomy of the talar neck with removal of the talar neck and head.

Dunn method (1922) — double resection; arched incision behind the lateral malleolus, curving around it anteriorly to the navicular; wide opening of the subtalar and midtarsal joints; an anteriorly-based subtalar wedge (at the expense of talar neck and head resection); a horizontal slot at the navicular base to receive the anterior part of the talus.

Lambrinudi method (1927) — iconic for paralytic or post-polio equinus foot: an arched incision behind the lateral malleolus, curving anteriorly and ending before the navicular; retraction of the peroneals and dorsiflexors; opening of the subtalar and midtarsal joints; a large anteriorly-based subtalar wedge (with talar neck and head resection); a slot at the navicular base. The Lambrinudi point: the distal talar resection is angled so that on insertion of the talus into the navicular slot the foot rests in neutral (no equinus) — the talus "serves" as a static dorsiflexor, counteracting the paralysed tibialis anterior. Immobilisation 3 months.

Ryerson method (1923) — modified triple arthrodesis with smaller wedges and preservation of the talar head; appropriate for younger patients with smaller deformities.

Modified Hoke (fig. 332) — lateral incision, opening of all three joints, resection with small wedges. The Nedrigailova and Novachenko-Nikolaev methods are Russian variants with different wedge orientations.

Alternatives in pan-talar (four-joint) arthrodesis: Vainio and Djanelidze remove the talus, excise its cartilage and reinsert it as a free graft — reducing consolidation time and AVN risk. Ivanov's method adds talar block sanitation.

Compression triple arthrodesis of Katsarov — with external fixator and K-wires providing dosed compression with weight bearing from day 5–6.

## Foot Resections and Pan-Talar Arthrodesis

Isolated subtalar arthrodesis is indicated for painful post-traumatic stasis after calcaneal or talar fracture, arthritic states, and some variants of rigid paediatric plano-valgus. An arched lateral incision, opening of the peroneal tendon sheath with retraction posteriorly and inferiorly; entry into the sinus tarsi and opening of the subtalar joint; forced supination of the foot and cartilage resection to bleeding bone; if needed — varus/valgus correction with a wedge resection. Fixation with a screw or K-wires; immobilisation 3 months.

Katsarov compression subtalar and midtarsal arthrodesis — anterolateral approach; resection of the articular surfaces of talus, calcaneus, navicular and cuboid; two parallel K-wires through the talus into the navicular; external fixator compression and weight bearing from day 5–6.

## Calcaneal Spur and Haglund's Disease

The calcaneal spur (beneath the plantar fascia insertion) is resected through a horseshoe medial incision below the heel apex; detachment of the fascia and short plantar flexors from the calcaneal tuberosity; exposure of the exostosis and chisel resection. Weight bearing is allowed at 15 days.

Haglund's disease (posterior superolateral calcaneal exostosis with retrocalcaneal bursitis and Achilles enthesopathy) — techniques per Du Vries (lateral incision along the Achilles contour with depriosteation of the posterosuperior surface and chisel excision of the protruding bony segment; care for the Achilles insertion), Keck-Kelly (lateral wedge osteotomy with dorsal rotation), and Hoffa (horizontal calcaneal osteotomy with dorsal rotation of the posterior part and screw fixation; often with concomitant Achilles shortening).

Hallux valgus correction requires elimination of the first metatarsal varus, correction of the hallux valgus angle, potential sesamoid repositioning, and preservation of muscle balance.

Boychev method (fig. 353): for mild deformities that are passively correctible. A medial longitudinal incision from the hallux to the navicular, around the dorsal exostosis. The bursa is excised, the MTP-1 is opened, the exostosis is resected from the sagittal sulcus separating it from the head. Just below the metatarsal head a medially based closing wedge is excised, sized to fully correct the valgus. Arcuate closing of the osteotomy corrects the hallux. Stabilisation — a single transverse suture between the I and II MTP capsules, with K-wire or splint fixation.

Tchokanov method (Boychev modification): instead of two metatarsal osteotomies, a wedge is made in the medial cuneiform (opening-wedge medial cuneiform osteotomy); after correction of the first metatarsal varus, a bone block taken from the metatarsal head is placed in the opened wedge.

McBride method (1928): an arched longitudinal incision over MTP-1 dorsum from the lateral border of the hallux base, posterior and downward, crossing the long extensor and first metatarsal. It ends about 1 cm posterior to the exostosis. Entry into the first interspace, detachment of the adductor hallucis tendon from the base of the proximal phalanx and its redirection (modern analogue — transfer of the adductor hallucis to the first metatarsal neck). Exostosectomy and medial capsulorrhaphy. The sesamoids are released and repositioned beneath the head.

Lelièvre method — five principles: (1) preserve the length of the first metatarsal and its head; (2) preserve MTP-1 mobility; (3) reposition laterally subluxed sesamoids; (4) eliminate first metatarsal varus; (5) eliminate hallux rotation. The technique includes distal chevron or scarf osteotomy with lateral release, medial capsulorrhaphy, and exostosectomy.

Keller-Brandes method (1904): a dorsomedial arched incision from the distal third of the proximal phalanx to the metatarso-cuneiform joint; a capsular flap; resection of 1/3 to 1/2 of the proximal phalanx; resection of the exostosis and osteophytes. The freed hallux allows passive correction of varus; in very severe deformity — additional transverse osteotomy at the metatarsal base. Used primarily in older patients with advanced MTP-1 arthritis (hallux rigidus).

Swanson silicone arthroplasty — single-stem for rigid moderate hallux valgus with relatively preserved metatarsal head; double-stem for severe rheumatoid deformities with lateral toe drift, hallux valgus senilis, or after failed prior surgery where the head may be sacrificed.

Hoffmann method (1912): for severe rheumatoid deformity with dislocations, lateral drift of the toes, and callosities under the metatarsal heads. A transverse plantar or dorsal elliptical incision at the metatarsal head level; resection of the heads of II–V (or I–V in the most severe cases); resection of sesamoids and proximal subluxations; closure after excision of redundant plantar flap (Fowler modification).

## Hammer Toe and Claw Toe Correction

Taylor method: a 2.5 cm dorsolateral incision from the metatarsal neck to the DIP; exposure of the extensor expansion; detachment of the short and long flexor tendons and dorsal rerouting around the lateral phalangeal surface for self-suture — flexor-to-extensor transfer per Girdlestone-Taylor.

PIP arthroplasty (Du Vries): elliptical skin excision over the PIP; resection of the distal end of the proximal phalanx and pegging of the distal end into a recess in the middle phalanx; fixation with a longitudinal K-wire. A simple and reliable variant.

Plantar-flexed hallux (hallux flexus) correction per Lapidus: wide MTP-1 capsulotomy, detachment of the long flexor hallucis, and capsulotomy of the naviculocuneiform and cuneo-metatarsal joints. With overactive tibialis anterior — Meary transfer of the tendon onto the lateral border.

Congenital varus of the fifth toe (quintus varus) correction per Karchinov: lateral incision along the anterior half of the foot and toe; wide medial and dorsal capsulotomy of MTP-5; release of extensor contracture; detachment of the abductor digiti minimi and subcutaneous transfer of the distal portion of the extensor through a bone canal in the proximal phalanx for passive stabilisation. Alternative — Lapidus technique with extensor tenotomy and transfer through a bone canal.

## Correction of Metatarsus Varus and Fallen Transverse Arch

Metatarsus varus (paediatric metatarsus adductus) correction per Gerchev — three-stage for children 3–6 years before rigidisation. Stage I: medial incision from MTP-1 to the tip of the medial malleolus; Z-lengthening of the adductor hallucis; detachment of the tibialis anterior; capsulotomy of the cuneo-metatarsal-1 and the navicular-cuneiform joints. Stage II: lateral incision; detachment and oblique division of the peroneus longus, reinsertion at a more distal point under tension. Stage III: K-wire fixation of the correction.

Heyman-Strong method: a wide dorsal transverse incision or a series of parallel longitudinal incisions in the intermetatarsal spaces; wide capsulotomies of the tarsometatarsal joints of all five metatarsals; passive correction of the adductus and fixation with two K-wires, one through the first and one through the fifth metatarsal into the midtarsus.

Fallen transverse arch correction per Camera: a longitudinal dorsal incision over the second interspace; detachment of the long extensor of the second or third toe at the toe base and transfer through a subcutaneous tunnel and through a canal in the metatarsal, sutured to itself under tension. Often supplemented with tibialis anterior transfer for construct reinforcement.

## Extra-Articular Subtalar Arthroereisis

In addition to the classical Grice-Green arthrodesis and the Chambers method, modern practice uses arthroereisis with an implant — a conical metallic or biodegradable plug (Maxwell-Brancheau, MBA), inserted via the sinus tarsi, blocking calcaneal eversion and stabilising the medial arch in paediatric flexible flat foot. Results are durable in correctly selected patients; with pain or impingement — implant removal at 18–24 months.

## Foot Amputations

Classical foot amputations build a functional residual limb with dorsal and plantar skin flaps, with the plantar flap always longer and covering the entire weight-bearing surface.

Toe amputation (phalangeal line or MTP disarticulation): two skin flaps, longer plantar; tendon transection of the extensors and flexors with high nerve transection (to avoid neuroma).

Single metatarsal amputation: racquet-shaped flap with longer handle along the dorsum; subperiosteal dissection and tendon transection at distal traction; nerve transection at a higher level.

Sharp transmetatarsal amputation: longer plantar flap covering the stump; metatarsals transected transversely at a level providing support and balance (usually proximal third). Postoperative care with splint and progressive prosthetic fitting.

Chopart disarticulation (between the midtarsal and tarsal bones): removes the dorsiflexor function while preserving the Achilles insertion, causing the residual foot to drift progressively into equinus deformity. Technical approach — transverse incision along the projection of Chopart's joint; opening of the talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints; division of capsules and plantar ligaments. Concurrent Achilles transfer anteriorly to the talar neck is recommended for neutralisation (Boyd adds talocalcaneal arthrodesis for added stability). Because of the equinus and plantar ulcer risk, Chopart is often abandoned in favour of transmetatarsal or Syme amputation.

Lisfranc disarticulation (between the tarsals and metatarsals): on the Hey-Lisfranc principle, with a longer plantar flap; division of Lisfranc's ligament and resection of the second metatarsal base if preserved.

Syme transankle amputation (1842): essential prerequisite — the plantar heel skin must be fully preserved and vascularised. The incision begins at one malleolus, encircles the heel from the plantar surface, and ends at the tip of the other malleolus; from here it turns over the anterior ankle and joins the start. The anterior half of the joint is opened; the foot is forced into maximal plantar flexion and the joint opened panoramically; the talofibular, deltoid and Achilles ligaments are sequentially divided. The foot is removed together with the talus and calcaneus, preserving the cutaneous-fat heel pad with full vascular supply from plantar branches of the posterior tibial artery. Both malleoli are transversely cut at their bases, leaving a small cartilage-bearing tibial articular zone. The skin flap is rotated upward and anteriorly and sutured to the anterior flap. The alternative "high Syme" resects the tibia above its articular surface. Syme is convenient, reliable, and provides a limb with natural heel support for prosthetic fitting — the standard for diabetic forefoot gangrene with intact plantar circulation and for severe congenital deformities.

The classical Pirogoff amputation (1854) — the iconic Russian technique: the foot is amputated at the ankle level, but the heel (with the posterior half of the calcaneus) is preserved and rotated vertically against the resected tibia for bony fusion. It achieves a functional residual limb with natural heel support; rarely used today but remains part of the Russo-Soviet orthopaedic heritage.

## Anaesthesia for Foot Surgery

The classical ankle block (blockade of the posterior tibial nerve behind the medial malleolus, superficial peroneal nerve subcutaneously across the anterior ankle line, deep peroneal nerve deep between the tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus tendons, saphenous nerve subcutaneously medially, and sural nerve subcutaneously posterolaterally) is standard for distal foot surgery. Bulgarian tradition prefers spinal or epidural anaesthesia for arthrodesis, triple arthrodesis and Syme amputation; ankle block — for hallux valgus, hammer toes, ingrown toenail, and the elderly with high anaesthetic risk.

## Ingrown Toenail (Unguis Incarnatus)

For unguis incarnatus — total or partial nail plate removal. Total: the nail is undermined with a probe, scissors or other instrument and avulsed with a Kocher; the granulations are curetted; sterile ointment dressing. Partial: undermining of the ingrown part; longitudinal nail plate division and removal; granulation curettage; single suture to close the dead space and sterile dressing. The Hadzhistamov improvement creates a triangular skin flap at the nail base, which after curettage is returned to its place and sutured with separate stitches — reducing recurrence.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

The ankle and foot offer a wide variety of surgical approaches, each tailored to specific regional pathology. The anterior ankle approach uses the interval between TA and EHL/EDL, retracting the anterior tibial vessels and deep peroneal nerve laterally. The anterolateral approach is the standard for pilon fractures, between EHL/EDL and peroneus tertius. The posterolateral approach with sural nerve protection accesses the posterior malleolus and posterior pilon. The medial ankle approach is used for medial malleolar fixation, tarsal tunnel release, and posterior tibial tendon procedures. The sinus tarsi approach has largely supplanted the extensile lateral approach for calcaneal fracture fixation, with substantially lower wound complication rates. The dorsal foot approaches for forefoot procedures must avoid the superficial peroneal nerve branches and the dorsalis pedis artery. The medial and lateral foot approaches access the respective columns of the midfoot. Foot fasciotomy through dorsal incisions releases the multiple foot compartments in compartment syndrome. The principal neurovascular structures include the anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve (anterior ankle, becoming the dorsalis pedis vessels and DPN in the foot), the posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve in the tarsal tunnel (medial ankle), the superficial peroneal nerve (subcutaneous over the dorsum of the foot), the sural nerve (subcutaneous posterolaterally), and the saphenous nerve (medially).

From the classical Bulgarian and Russian orthopaedic school in foot-and-ankle surgery the following deserve to be remembered: the Boychev and Holevich posterior trans-Achilles and bimalleolar approaches; the classical ankle arthrodeses (Charnley external compression, Putti, Crawford-Adams, Adams open, Blair for talar loss); the Müller and Katsarov compression arthrodeses; the classical triple arthrodesis (Lambrinudi, Dunn, Hoke, Ryerson) and its Russian modifications (Nedrigailova, Novachenko-Nikolaev); the Codivilla, Zatsepin, Gerchev and Katsarov methods for congenital pes equino-varus; the Stöffel staged correction; the classical pes plano-valgus corrections (Lowman, Miller-Hoke, Lelièvre, Chambers, Karchinov-Young) and the Grice-Green extra-articular subtalar arthrodesis; the pes cavus corrections (Kuslik, Lefèvre, Cole, Steindler stripping, Dwyer calcaneal osteotomy); the lateral instability surgery (Watson-Jones, Chrisman-Snook, Brostrom-Gould, Kopchev); the tendon-and-muscle transfers (tibialis posterior to the dorsum, Tchaklin-Kaplan, Holevich anterior tenodesis, Boychev posterior tenodesis with Achilles shortening, Zatsepin posterior tenodesis); the Achilles lengthening repertoire (open Z, Hoke percutaneous, White partial transection, Strayer gastrocnemius recession, Silfverskiöld); Phelps and Stoffel tibial nerve neurectomy for spastic equinus; the classical hallux valgus corrections (Boychev, Tchokanov, McBride, Lelièvre, Keller-Brandes, Swanson silicone, Hoffmann for rheumatoid); the resections of Sharp, Chopart, Lisfranc, Syme and Pirogoff; and the Hadzhistamov technique for ingrown toenail. This classical base remains relevant both for understanding the evolution of foot-and-ankle surgery and for contemporary practice in Bulgarian conditions.
