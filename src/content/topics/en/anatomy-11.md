## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The ankle and foot together form an intricate anatomical complex of 28 bones, more than 30 joints, and a dense network of tendons, ligaments, and neurovascular structures organised into a region of complex weight-bearing and propulsive function. Surgical approaches must navigate this complexity, with attention to the thin soft-tissue envelope (particularly around the medial and anterior ankle), the principal neurovascular structures (anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve anteriorly; posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve in the tarsal tunnel; great saphenous vein and saphenous nerve medially; sural nerve laterally; superficial peroneal nerve crossing anterolaterally), and the multiple fascial compartments of the foot. This chapter presents the regional surgical anatomy along the classical Bulgarian school of Boychev and Holevich, enriched with the quantitative data of Sarrafian (3rd ed., 2015) for the talar blood supply and the architecture of the tarsal canal, the approach principles of Hoppenfeld and deBoer, and the classical procedures of Codivilla, Zatsepin, Lambrinudi, Hoke, Dwyer, Grice-Green, McBride, Keller, Lelièvre, Watson-Jones, Chrisman-Snook, and Brostrom-Gould — supplemented with the Bulgarian curriculum: Boychev-Holevich parahallux and trans-Achilles approaches, the Holevich bimalleolar approach, Katsarov for the calcaneus, Ikonomov for the talus, Tchokanov for hallux valgus, Karchinov for quintus varus and pes plano-valgus, Gerchev for metatarsus varus and clubfoot, Hadzhistamov for ingrown nail, and Kopchev for lateral ankle instability.

**Ankle mortise.** The tibial plafond, medial malleolus, distal fibula (lateral malleolus), and talar dome (trochlea tali) form the body of the ankle joint. The trochlea is wider anteriorly than posteriorly — the mortise "locks" in dorsiflexion and "unlocks" in plantar flexion, which anatomically explains why plantar flexion is the vulnerable position for inversion sprains. The tibiofibular syndesmosis (anterior, posterior, interosseous, and transverse tibiofibular ligaments) controls the bond between the two malleoli under axial and rotational load.

**Subtalar joint.** Between talus (superiorly) and calcaneus (inferiorly), with three articular facets — anterior, middle, posterior — separated by the tarsal canal and tarsal sinus. In Sarrafian's large series the anterior facet is confluent with the middle facet in a significant proportion (variation tables 2.7–2.8); absent in 0.9% (Laidlaw). The sustentaculum tali carries the middle facet and is the principal medial bony landmark in calcaneal fracture fixation.

**Midfoot.** Navicular, cuboid, and three cuneiforms. The navicular tuberosity is a critical landmark — insertion of the tibialis posterior and possible site of accessory navicular (Geist type I–III). The cuboid carries the groove for the peroneus longus tendon.

**Chopart line** separates the hindfoot from the midfoot — talonavicular + calcaneocuboid joints; the disarticulation point of the classical Chopart amputation.

**Lisfranc line** separates the midfoot from the forefoot — the tarsometatarsal joints. The Roman arch architecture (Sarrafian): C2 (intermediate cuneiform) is recessed 8 mm proximal to C1 and 4 mm proximal to C3, forming a cuneiform mortise for the M2 base — the "keystone" anatomical principle responsible for longitudinal midfoot stability. The Lisfranc ligament (between C1 and the M2 base) is the strongest of the three interosseous ligaments at this line, height about 1 cm and thickness about 5 mm; per Sloan et al. the plantar Lisfranc ligament is stronger and stiffer than the dorsal.

**Forefoot.** Five metatarsals and the phalanges (two phalanges for the hallux, three for the lesser toes). The fifth metatarsal base (tuberosity) is a common landmark and site of avulsion fractures (zone I by Lawrence-Botte).

    <li>Lisfranc Roman arch: M2 is the "keystone" — its base is recessed proximally between the three cuneiforms. Displacement greater than 2 mm requires reduction and fixation.</li>
    <li>Sustentaculum tali — the principal medial bony landmark in calcaneal fracture fixation and the bearer of the middle subtalar facet.</li>

### Regional Topographic Anatomy
<details id="topography">
<summary>Regional Topographic Anatomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian topographic school divides the ankle and foot into well-defined zones corresponding to the principal operative planes. The ankle region is separated from the leg by an arched line 7–8 cm proximal to the tip of the medial malleolus; distally the border to the foot runs along an arch connecting the tips of the two malleoli, with anterior convexity 2 cm from the anterior articular margin of the tibia. Four sectors are recognised: anterior (over the dorsum of the foot), medial (regio malleolaris medialis), lateral (regio malleolaris lateralis), and posterior (regio tendinis calcanei).

**Medial malleolus.** The skin is thin and mobile, the subcutaneous tissue poorly developed. The great saphenous vein ascends along the anterior border, accompanied medially by the saphenous nerve. The anterior medial malleolar artery (branch of the anterior tibial) and the medial malleolar branches of the posterior tibial form the medial malleolar network. Beneath the superficial fascia lies the superior extensor retinaculum, and just distally the proximal limb of the inferior extensor retinaculum. Between the posterior border of the medial malleolus and the calcaneus the flexor retinaculum forms the tarsal tunnel, containing, anterior to posterior: tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, posterior neurovascular bundle (posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve), and flexor hallucis longus. The artery usually lies anterior and medial to the nerve; rare cases lack the artery, replaced by a communicating branch of the peroneal artery (Adachi variant).

**Lateral malleolus.** The region is bounded anteriorly by a line from the anterior margin of the lateral malleolus to the fifth metatarsal tuberosity, and posteriorly by the lateral border of the Achilles. Between the lateral malleolus and the calcaneus the superior and inferior peroneal retinacula form two separate canals for peroneus longus (posterior) and peroneus brevis (anterior). The peroneus longus tendon crosses the brevis posteriorly, then disappears beneath the abductor digiti minimi pedis to enter the cuboid groove.

**Posterior ankle (Achilles).** The regio tendinis calcanei coincides with the Achilles relief, flanked by the two retromalleolar fossae. Anterior to the tendon is the triangular-pyramid fat-connective tissue space with proximal apex (Kager's space), bounded anteriorly by the posterior tibial surface and joint capsule, posteriorly by the paratenon, and laterally and medially by the muscle compartments. Five to seven cm proximal to the subtalar joint the peroneal (fibular) artery gives off the perforating branch, the communicating branch, and the lateral malleolar branches that form the lateral malleolar network.

**Anterior ankle.** The skin is thin and mobile; the venous network is the anastomotic web between great and small saphenous; sensory nerves are branches of the superficial peroneal and saphenous. The superior extensor retinaculum runs transversely and forms a deep septum separating two osseofibrous canals; the inferior extensor retinaculum has a Y-shape with three limbs forming three canals for the anterior compartment tendons. The anterior tibial vessels and deep peroneal nerve lie in the canal of the extensor hallucis longus, posterior and lateral to its tendon; at this level the nerve is medial to the artery.

**Foot.** Divided into dorsum pedis, planta pedis and digiti. The medial border between dorsum and sole corresponds to the superior border of the abductor hallucis (from the middle of the heel to the head of the first metatarsal); the lateral border to the superior border of the abductor digiti minimi (to the head of the fifth metatarsal). The toes are separated from the foot by an arch 2.0–2.5 cm proximal to the interdigital folds. The dorsal venous arch forms at the mid-metatarsal level from anastomoses between the two saphenous veins; from it arise the great saphenous (medially, above the navicular tuberosity) and the small saphenous (laterally, around the fifth metatarsal tuberosity and posterior to the lateral malleolus).

**Plantar space.** Divided by the plantar aponeurosis and medial and lateral fascial septa into four compartments per Sarrafian: medial (tibial), lateral (peroneal), central (with superficial, intermediary, and deep [adductor] subcompartments), and interosseous (subdivided into four spaces by vertical septa). Grodinsky described six fascial spaces (F1–F6) in total; modern surgical classification adds a calcaneal compartment for nine recognised compartments.

</div>
</details>

**Anterior ankle.** From medial to lateral: tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus (EHL), anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve, extensor digitorum longus (EDL), peroneus tertius. At this level the nerve lies medial to the artery. The anterior tibial artery continues distally as the dorsalis pedis, lying between EHL and EDL tendons directly on bone; its branches — lateral tarsal, arcuate, deep plantar branch, and first dorsal metatarsal — define the vascular risk during tarsometatarsal approaches.

**Tarsal tunnel.** Posterior to the medial malleolus, deep to the flexor retinaculum, the contents (anterior to posterior): tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve, flexor hallucis longus — mnemonic "Tom, Dick, And a Nervous Harry". The tibial nerve usually divides in the tunnel into the medial plantar nerve (larger; innervates flexor digitorum brevis, abductor hallucis, flexor hallucis brevis, first lumbrical, and the skin of the medial 3.5 toes) and the lateral plantar nerve (quadratus plantae, abductor digiti minimi, and lateral one-and-a-half toes), as well as the calcaneal branch to the heel (Baxter's nerve).

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Tarsal tunnel decompression treats tarsal tunnel syndrome: compression of the tibial nerve or its first branches with distal paraesthesia, pain along the medial foot and plantar branches, positive Tinel sign posterior to the medial malleolus. The flexor retinaculum and the fibrous septa between the two plantar branches are released.</p>
</aside>

**Lateral ankle.** Peroneus brevis anteriorly and peroneus longus posteriorly behind the lateral malleolus; the sural nerve runs posterolaterally, accompanied by the small saphenous vein. The sural nerve supplies the lateral border of the foot and the fifth toe; it is vulnerable in posterolateral and lateral Kocher approaches.

**Dorsum of the foot.** The superficial peroneal nerve pierces the fascia 10–12 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus, dividing into the medial and intermediate dorsal cutaneous nerves — both subcutaneous over the anterolateral ankle and dorsum of the foot, and at risk in dorsal longitudinal approaches. The deep peroneal nerve supplies only the first webspace.

**Plantar bundles.** The medial plantar bundle emerges 7–8 cm distal to the calcaneal tuber, beneath the abductor hallucis; the lateral bundle 4–5 cm distal to the tuber along the lateral border of the same muscle.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Sensory territories at the ankle: superficial peroneal nerve — almost the entire dorsum (except the first webspace); deep peroneal nerve — first webspace only; sural nerve — lateral border of the foot; saphenous nerve — medial border and medial malleolar region; tibial nerve (through the two plantar branches) — the entire sole. This distribution governs the choice of regional anaesthesia for an ankle block.</p>
</aside>

### Talar Blood Supply
<details id="talar-blood">
<summary>Talar Blood Supply</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The blood supply to the talus is critical for assessing surgical risk in neck and body fractures, because 60% of the bone is covered by cartilage and has no muscular attachments that might carry vascular inputs. The classical work of Mulfinger and Trueta (1970), confirmed and quantitatively extended by Gelberman and Mortensen, describes three principal arterial systems.

**Artery of the tarsal canal.** A branch of the posterior tibial artery, arising about 1 cm proximal to its division into medial and lateral plantar arteries; arises in 16.6% from the medial plantar artery, duplicated in 3.3%, absent in 3.3% (Mulfinger-Trueta, n=30). It runs in the tarsal canal and supplies the main feed to the talar body — the lateral two-thirds of the corpus tali (4–5 main branches in a posterolateral direction). Per Gelberman-Mortensen this is "the single major arterial supply to the body of the talus" — its injury in neck fractures is the direct cause of avascular necrosis.

**Deltoid branch.** Arises from the artery of the tarsal canal about 5 mm from its origin (in 30% directly from the posterior tibial; duplicated in 6.6%). Supplies the medial one-quarter to one-third of the corpus tali through the deltoid ligament. In 91% of cases the deltoid branch is present — functioning as a collateral channel that preserves blood supply even when the artery of the tarsal canal is interrupted.

**Artery of the sinus tarsi.** Most often arises from the dorsalis pedis (or from the proximal lateral tarsal, the anterior lateral malleolar, or the perforating branch of the peroneal). It anastomoses with the artery of the tarsal canal behind the talar neck, forming the main arterial axis of the bone. Supplies the inferolateral half of the talar head and the neck from below; through the canal–sinus anastomosis it provides dual blood supply to the inferior neck surface in 83% of cases.

**Talar head.** Dual blood supply — superomedial half from branches of the dorsalis pedis (superior neck branches); inferolateral half from the artery of the sinus tarsi.

**Posterior tubercle.** Supplied by the posterior anastomotic network (peroneal + posterior tibial); an independent vascular territory usually preserved in neck fractures.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Avascular necrosis after talar neck fracture by Hawkins (1970): type I (no dislocation) — 0%; type II (subtalar dislocation) — 20–50%; type III (tibiotalar + subtalar dislocation) — 80–100%; type IV (with talonavicular subluxation) — almost 100%. Hawkins sign (a subchondral lucent line on the talar dome at 6–8 weeks) excludes AVN.</li>
    <li>The artery of the tarsal canal is the single major supply to the corpus tali — dissection in fixation must preserve the tarsal canal and the medial deltoid attachment.</li>
    <li>The deltoid branch preserves about a third of the corpus tali when the canal is disrupted — one reason modern series report higher survival than the classical percentages.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Lateral ligament complex.** Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) — thin, most vulnerable in plantar-flexion inversion (the classic sprain); calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) — maintains subtalar stability; posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL) — very strong, torn only with dislocation.

**Medial deltoid complex.** Superficial layer: tibionavicular, tibiocalcaneal, posterior tibiotalar superficial. Deep layer: anterior tibiotalar and posterior tibiotalar — the principal restraints against lateral talar translation.

**Spring ligament complex (calcaneonavicular).** Sarrafian distinguishes three components:
- *Superomedial calcaneonavicular ligament* (ligamentum neglectum) — a broad quadrilateral ligament from the sustentaculum tali, wrapping medial to the talar head; its surface is fibrocartilaginous and supports the talar head as an articular surface. It interlaces with the tibionavicular, tibioligamentous, and tibiocalcaneal components of the deltoid.
- *Inferior calcaneonavicular* (the true "spring") — trapezoidal, fasciculated, from the coronoid fossa on the inferior calcaneus to the inferior navicular surface.
- *Third plantar calcaneonavicular ligament* (Tanigushi et al., n=48) — between the two calcaneal facets, identified in 100%.

The spring complex provides static support to the medial talar head and the medial longitudinal arch; its dysfunction (together with rupture of the tibialis posterior tendon) is central to the pathogenesis of adult acquired flatfoot (PTTD).

**Tibio-spring ligament** (a superficial deltoid component) — withstands 351 N to ultimate load (in vitro), underscoring its mechanical role in resisting gravitational and dynamic depression of the talar head.

**Lisfranc ligament.** Between the lateral surface of C1 (medial cuneiform) and the medial surface of the M2 base. Height about 1 cm, thickness about 5 mm; duplicated in 22% (two bands of 3–4 mm). The strongest of the three interosseous ligaments at the Lisfranc line. Per Sloan the plantar Lisfranc ligament is stronger and stiffer than the dorsal; transection of Lisfranc + plantar C1-M2/M3 → transverse instability; transection of Lisfranc + C1-C2 → longitudinal instability. Instability threshold (Kaar): diastasis greater than 2 mm.

**Chopart ligaments.** Bifurcate ligament (Y-shaped) from the calcaneus to the navicular (medial limb) and cuboid (lateral limb); a key ligament in flexion-rotation injuries of the midfoot.

### Foot Compartments
<details id="compartments">
<summary>Foot Compartments</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The foot has four principal compartments (Sarrafian) or nine (modern surgical classification) — medial, lateral, central (with three subcompartments), calcaneal, and four interossei. Foot compartment syndrome requires recognition and fasciotomy:
- The calcaneal compartment contains the lateral plantar nerve and lateral plantar artery — the typical site of compartment syndrome after severe calcaneal fractures.
- Standard fasciotomy is performed through two dorsal incisions over the dorsum of the second and fourth metatarsals (decompression of the four interossei and the medial, central, and lateral compartments), with an additional medial incision if the clinical picture or measured tissue pressures demand it.
- Thresholds for operative decompression: greater than 30 mmHg at rest or Δp (diastolic − compartment) less than 30 mmHg by the Whitesides/McQueen criteria.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The calcaneal compartment after closed calcaneal fractures is a frequently missed source of long-term pain and clawing of the toes. Measure pressures in high-energy mechanisms, especially in polytrauma where the clinical examination is unreliable.</li>
    <li>Plantar fasciotomy along the medial non-weight-bearing border (Henry line) does not cross the plantar neurovascular bundles, but risks the calcaneal branch of the tibial nerve (Baxter) — dissect bluntly along bone.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Biomechanics
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="biomechanics">
<details>
<summary>Biomechanics</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The tibiotalar joint performs predominantly sagittal motion — about 20° dorsiflexion and 50° plantar flexion in the normal foot. Translational and rotational components are minimal. Because of the wider anterior diameter of the talar trochlea, the joint "locks" in dorsiflexion and "unlocks" in plantar flexion — the anatomic substrate for the vulnerability of the lateral ligament complex to inversion injury in plantar flexion.

The subtalar joint performs triplanar motion about the Henke axis (per Manter and Inman: 42° sagittal, 16–23° transverse). Pronation (eversion + abduction + dorsiflexion) and supination (inversion + adduction + plantar flexion) are coupled. The talus advances about 1.5–1.7 mm for every 10° of subtalar rotation. After tibiotalar arthrodesis the subtalar and Chopart joints carry increased compensation — the anatomical basis for the good functional gait that follows ankle arthrodesis.

The Chopart joint (talonavicular + calcaneocuboid) functions as a "drive belt": in supination the two joint axes are non-parallel and the midfoot locks; in pronation the axes become parallel and the midfoot unlocks, allowing it to absorb impact at heel contact.

The Lisfranc joint is essentially rigid in the medial column (M1) and much more mobile laterally (M4-M5), which explains the broader indication for medial column arthrodesis (Lapidus at M1-C1) and flexible stabilisation of M4-M5 (without arthrodesis in typical cases).

The plantar aponeurosis is the Hicks "windlass mechanism": with dorsiflexion of the toes (push-off) the fascia winds around the metatarsal heads and actively raises the medial longitudinal arch. Contracture of the plantar fascia is therefore a key component of pes cavus, and its detachment from the calcaneal tuber (Steindler) the principal soft-tissue step in correction.

## Landmarks and Imaging

<summary>Landmarks and Imaging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Palpable landmarks.** Medial and lateral malleoli, sustentaculum tali (2.5 cm distal to the tip of the medial malleolus), tibialis anterior tubercle on the navicular, fifth metatarsal base tuberosity, the Achilles relief and calcaneal projections, the Henry point on the lateral wall of the calcaneus.

**Standard radiographs.** Ankle — anteroposterior (AP), lateral, and mortise (15–20° internal rotation); stress views for the lateral and medial complexes (talar tilt > 10° → ATFL rupture; over 6 mm of lateral opening → Bulgarian criterion of Hackenbruch-Hoesterberg, 1966). Foot — anteroposterior, lateral, and oblique; weight-bearing views are mandatory for hallux valgus and pes plano-valgus.

**Key radiographic angles.**
- Bohler tuber-joint angle — normal 30–35° (Sarrafian series of 50 calcanei: mean 32°); reduced in calcaneal compression fractures.
- Gissane "critical angle" — 95–105° (between the posterior facet and the anterior calcaneal facet); increased with compression of the posterior facet.
- Hallux valgus angle (HVA) — normal < 15° (Hardy-Chapman: mean 15.7°, range 0–36°).
- Intermetatarsal angle M1-M2 (IMA) — normal < 9° (Hardy-Chapman: mean 8.5°, mode 8–9°).
- Distal metatarsal articular angle (DMAA) — < 10° (ElSaid: mean 8.21°, range −14° to +30°).
- Hallux interphalangeal angle — normal about 14.5° (range 6–24°).
- Talo-metatarsal angle of Meary — normal about 0° (talus and M1 collinear); apex dorsal (positive) in pes cavus; apex plantar (negative) in pes planus.

**Hindfoot alignment view** (Saltzman) — standing hindfoot view for objective assessment of heel varus/valgus relative to the tibial axis; a critical preoperative image before substantial correction of the trigonal pyramidal complex.

**CT.** Standard in calcaneal and pilon fractures; coronal sections through the posterior facet are the basis of the Sanders classification; 3D reconstructions in complex Lisfranc and tarsal injuries.

**MRI.** Talar dome osteochondral lesions (Berndt-Hardy, Hepple), PTTD (Johnson-Strom stages I–IV), navicular stress fractures, and tarsal coalitions (talocalcaneal, calcaneonavicular).

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Classification of Approaches
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classification">
<details>
<summary>Classification of Approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Ankle and foot approaches are classified along four axes.

**By column:** anterior / anterolateral / lateral / posterolateral / posterior (trans-Achilles and para-Achilles) / medial / sinus tarsi / extensile lateral.

**By soft-tissue architecture:** soft-tissue–sparing (sinus tarsi for the calcaneus, dorsomedial for the hallux) versus extensile (L-shaped lateral of Letournel-Benirschke for the calcaneus; Holevich bimalleolar for the ankle).

**By goal:** reduction and fixation (fractures); reconstruction and transfer (instability, paralytic deformities); arthrodesis (arthritic and deforming conditions); arthroplasty (total ankle); amputation (Sharp, Chopart, Lisfranc, Syme, Pirogov).

**By soft-tissue coverage and timing.** The thin medial and anterior soft-tissue envelope dictates the "span and stage" principle in pilon fractures — Müller (1970): minimum skin bridge of 7 cm between incisions and staged fixation, only after the swelling subsides.

The Hoppenfeld principle remains directive: the approach is chosen by the location of the pathology, the neurovascular topography, and the state of the soft tissues — not by the technical preferences of the surgeon. "The approach should not be more painful than the procedure."

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Treatment
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Nonoperative
<details id="nonoperative">
<summary>Nonoperative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For stable malleolar fractures (Weber A, isolated distal fibular fragment without medial instability and no diastasis) — full-contact cast or walking boot for 4–6 weeks with progressive weight bearing.

For acute lateral ligament injury without instability on stress views — functional management with a semi-rigid brace, early mobilisation and proprioceptive training; randomised studies show equivalent long-term results compared with classical primary surgical repair in isolated ATFL injury.

For congenital pes equino-varus (clubfoot) under one year — the Ponseti method with serial casting following the CAVE hierarchy (Cavus → Adductus → Varus → Equinus), supplemented by percutaneous Achilles tenotomy (TAL of Hoke) and prolonged night bracing (Denis-Browne, Mitchell) until age 4. Percutaneous Achilles tenotomy is the near-mandatory final step in about 90% of children.

For hallux valgus — footwear correction (wide forefoot, abandonment of high heels), insoles with metatarsal pads, night-time hallux braces — palliative measures only; they do not alter the natural history of the deformity.

For pes plano-valgus — flexible forms in children rarely require surgery; insoles with medial arch support are acceptable; rigid forms (tarsal coalition) require workup with CT/MRI and, if symptomatic, operative correction.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Ponseti serial casting in congenital clubfoot — 5–7 casts from age 1 week, sequentially correcting cavus → adductus → varus; equinus is corrected with percutaneous Achilles tenotomy in about 90% of cases.</li>
    <li>Functional treatment of an inversion sprain gives equivalent long-term results to primary surgical reconstruction in isolated ATFL injury without chronic instability.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Operative
<details id="operative">
<summary>Operative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<details>
<summary>Anterior ankle approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Used for anterior ankle arthrodesis, total ankle arthroplasty, distal tibial osteotomy, tumour resection, and anterior osteochondral lesions.

**Position and landmarks.** Supine with thigh tourniquet. Skin incision in the line of the second metatarsal, 10–15 cm long centred over the ankle joint.

**Internervous plane.** Superficially — between the two branches of the superficial peroneal nerve (medial and intermediate dorsal cutaneous); deep — between tibialis anterior (L4, deep peroneal nerve medially) and extensor hallucis longus / extensor digitorum longus (L5, S1, deep peroneal nerve laterally).

**Technique.** The skin incision is placed medial to the SPN crossings (the nerve is identified before deep dissection). The superior extensor retinaculum is opened along the line of the incision; tibialis anterior is retracted medially, EHL and EDL laterally. The anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve are identified at the lateral edge of the EHL canal and retracted laterally. The capsule is opened transversely.

**Dangers.** Superficial peroneal nerve (superficial); anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve (deep, lateral).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Anterolateral ankle approach (for pilon fractures)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Standard for lateral pilon fractures. Longitudinal incision over the anterolateral ankle, between EHL/EDL medially and peroneus tertius laterally. The deep peroneal nerve and anterior tibial artery are identified and retracted medially. The ankle joint and distal tibia are exposed with excellent visualisation of the anterior joint surface and distal tibial articular surface.

For combined pilon fractures the Müller principle (1970): first restore fibular length through an anterolateral approach (the fibula is the axis reference for tibial alignment); only then through a second anteromedial approach reduce the tibial plafond with interfragmentary screws and a medial tibial plate. The minimum distance between the two skin incisions is 7 cm to preserve the soft-tissue bridge vascularity.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Posterolateral ankle approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Used for fixation of the posterior malleolus (Volkmann fragment), posterior pilon fractures, and access to the peroneal tendons. Longitudinal incision behind the fibula. The sural nerve and small saphenous vein are identified and protected. The interval between the peroneal tendons (laterally) and flexor hallucis longus (medially) is developed; FHL is retracted medially. The posterior aspect of the ankle joint and the posterior malleolus are exposed. Always inspect the posterior tibiofibular ligament for an avulsion fragment.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Posterior para-Achilles and trans-Achilles approaches (Boychev-Holevich)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Para-Achilles.** Along the lateral edge of the Achilles; the tendon is retracted medially and in the depth the joint capsule and the posterior aspects of tibia, talus and calcaneus are exposed. Convenient for posterior pilon, posterior capsulotomy in equinus, and access to the posterior process of the talus.

**Trans-Achilles (Boychev-Holevich).** Classical Bulgarian wide posterior approach. Along the lateral edge of the Achilles to its insertion; the tendon is incised in a Z-pattern in the frontal plane and the two limbs are retracted proximally and distally. Through Kager's space the dissection enters the interval between flexor hallucis longus (medially) and the peronei (laterally); via this route the posterior surfaces of the talus and calcaneus and the subtalar joint are exposed. The posterior tibial neurovascular bundle lies anterior to FHL — dissection medial to that muscle is discouraged. At closure the Achilles is repaired end-to-end with heavy non-absorbable sutures (Bunnell or Krackow technique).

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The Boychev-Holevich trans-Achilles posterior approach remains the widest posterior exposure of the tibiotalar joint. Z-incision of the tendon in the frontal plane preserves length after anatomic repair. The posterior neurovascular bundle lies anterior to flexor hallucis longus, so entry into Kager's space is always lateral to FHL. The tibial nerve and posterior tibial artery may be injured by dissection passing medial to FHL.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Medial trans-malleolar (osteotomy) approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Used for osteochondral lesions of the medial talar dome, some pilon patterns, and ankle arthrodesis with malleolar resection. Supine with external rotation of the limb. A gently arched incision at the base of the malleolus; the bone is osteotomised before joint dissection so that the soft-tissue attachments (deltoid) remain intact. The malleolus is reflected distally with the deltoid ligament, opening the joint widely. At closure the malleolus is refixed with a malleolar screw (4.0 mm cancellous, bicortical) or 2 K-wires with a tension band.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Holevich bimalleolar approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Bulgarian classical wide approach for total ankle arthroplasty, talar tumours, and complex ankle arthrodesis. A longitudinal 5–6 cm incision over each malleolus to its tip. Both malleoli are depriostied and obliquely osteotomised at their bases — the osteotome directed at about 45° toward the joint line into the upper corners of the mortise. The ligaments along the anterior and posterior borders are divided, and the malleoli reflected distally, "hanging" on the ligaments attached to their tips. The tibiotalar joint thus opens panoramically. At closure the malleoli are returned and fixed with screws; the lateral malleolus may receive an additional cerclage as needed.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Medial ankle approach and tarsal tunnel release</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Used for fixation of the medial malleolus (vertical incision over the malleolus), deltoid ligament repair, tarsal tunnel release (curved incision behind the medial malleolus with division of the flexor retinaculum and decompression of the tibial nerve and its two plantar branches), and procedures on the tibialis posterior tendon (PTTD reconstruction with FDL transfer per Mann).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Sinus tarsi and extensile lateral approaches to the calcaneus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Sinus tarsi.** A small oblique incision over the sinus tarsi (a depression palpable just anterior to the lateral malleolus). The peroneal tendons are retracted and the calcaneocuboid joint and posterior subtalar facet are exposed. Has largely replaced the extensile lateral approach for calcaneal fracture fixation because of substantially lower wound complication rates.

**Extensile lateral (Letournel-Benirschke).** The historical standard. L-shaped or hockey-stick incision along the lateral calcaneus with a vertical limb behind the fibula and a horizontal limb above the fifth metatarsal. The skin and subcutaneous tissue are reflected as a single soft-tissue flap, vascularised by the lateral calcaneal artery (a branch of the peroneal). The lateral wall of the calcaneus is exposed for plating. Wound complication rates of 5–25% have led to substantial preference for sinus tarsi.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>The extensile L-shaped lateral approach to the calcaneus carries 5–25% wound complications, because the entire lateral flap with overlying skin is reflected on the lateral calcaneal artery. Late-presenting fractures, smokers, diabetics, and open fractures should sway choice toward sinus tarsi.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Tarsal and midfoot approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Anteromedial to the talus.** Longitudinal incision lateral to the tibialis anterior tendon; the interval develops between EHL and EDL (or TA and EHL); the extensor hallucis brevis is retracted laterally. The dorsalis pedis lies between EHL and EDL directly on bone and is preserved.

**Medial approach to the calcaneus (Katsarov).** Distal to the medial malleolus, curving toward the Achilles insertion; for drainage of a talocalcaneal cyst, osteomyelitis, and medialising calcaneal osteotomy.

**Horseshoe posterior approach.** Below the Achilles insertion — exposes the plantar surface of the calcaneal tuber for resection of a calcaneal spur (Du Vries) and Haglund's disease.

**L-shaped Kocher approach to the calcaneus** (T-shaped in the Bulgarian literature). Horizontal limb from sustentaculum tali to the fifth metatarsal tuberosity with a vertical limb along the lateral edge of the Achilles; for classical extra-articular subtalar arthrodesis and wide exposure of the calcaneocuboid joint.

**Approaches to the cuneiforms and navicular.** Longitudinal along the dorsomedial surface; for the medial cuneiform — along the medial border of the EHL tendon, retracting tibialis anterior medially. For the cuboid — an incision along the superior border of peroneus brevis, retracting the tendon downward and the lateral border of the extensor digitorum brevis upward.

**Posterolateral Kocher approach to the tarsal joints** — begins 5–7 cm proximal to the ankle, about 2 cm behind the fibula, continues parallel to the fibula, curves arch-like 2 cm below the tip of the lateral malleolus and runs across the dorsum to the navicular. The sural nerve is identified and protected subcutaneously. Simultaneously exposes the subtalar, calcaneocuboid, and talonavicular joints — the traditional approach for triple arthrodesis.

**Medial longitudinal to the medial column** — from the posterior border of the medial malleolus around it and distally along M1. The flexor retinaculum and inferior extensor retinaculum are incised; tibialis anterior and tibialis posterior are retracted dorsally and plantarly to expose all the bones and joints of the medial border — particularly convenient for PTTD reconstruction with FDL transfer per Mann and for medial column arthrodesis.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Metatarsal and toe approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Metatarsals.** Approach to M1 — dorsomedial, above the EHL tendon, medial to the origin of the great saphenous vein. To M2–M5 — longitudinal on the dorsal surface or in the corresponding interspaces; for hammer toe correction, osteotomies and fixation; incisions run lateral to the corresponding long extensors to spare the dorsal digital nerves.

**Tarsometatarsals (Lisfranc).** Longitudinal incisions cross the joint projection along the metatarsal axis. For M1 — medial to EHL; for M2 — between the EHL of the first and second toes, with medial retraction of the extensor hallucis brevis and ligation of the terminal dorsalis pedis and beginning of the arcuate artery; for M3 — medial or lateral to the long extensor of the third toe, with ligation of the arcuate artery; for M4 — lateral to its long extensor; for M5 — lateral to the long extensor of the fifth toe.

**MTP-1 (hallux).** Medial or dorsomedial incision from 2–2.5 cm distal to the joint, curving arch-wise over the dorsum medial to the EHL to mid-shaft M1; the dorsomedial preserves more plantarmedial vascular supply and is often preferred.

**Lesser MTP joints (II–V).** Dorsolateral, parallel to the corresponding extensor tendons. For the interphalangeal joints of the first and fifth toes — medial and lateral; for II–IV — immediately lateral and parallel to the extensors in the dorsal aponeurosis.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Dorsal, medial, lateral, and plantar foot approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Dorsal.** For forefoot procedures (hallux valgus, lesser toe correction, fracture fixation). Incisions are placed sparing the superficial peroneal nerve branches and the dorsalis pedis artery with the deep peroneal nerve (in the first dorsal interspace).

**Medial.** Along the medial border — to the medial column (navicular, medial cuneiform, M1 base); for medial column fractures, hallux valgus and medial column arthrodesis.

**Lateral.** Along the lateral border — to the cuboid, M4 and M5 and the lateral column; for cuboid fracture fixation, lateral column lengthening (Evans).

**Plantar.** Generally avoided because of the thick plantar fat and the risk of painful scars on the weight-bearing surface. When required (plantar fascia release, deep plantar abscess) — along the medial or lateral plantar non-weight-bearing border.

**Foot fasciotomy.** Two dorsal incisions over the dorsum of the second and fourth metatarsals, opening the dorsal and plantar interossei; an additional medial incision along the medial non-weight-bearing border for the medial and calcaneal compartments.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Lateral ankle instability surgery</summary>

**Acute injury.** Operative indication is based on stress radiographs: talar tilt greater than 10° or anterior subluxation. The Bulgarian criterion of Hackenbruch and Hoesterberg (1966) requires over 6 mm of lateral joint opening.

**Staples-Black-Brostrom.** L-shaped skin incision over the lateral malleolus; ATFL and CFL are repaired directly with non-absorbable sutures through bone tunnels in the fibula for pure avulsion injuries. The **Brostrom-Gould** modification adds augmentation with the lateral extensor retinaculum, transferred proximally over the repair.

**Modified Watson-Jones.** For chronic instability. Arched incision from the junction of the middle and distal third of the fibula to 5 cm anterior to the tip of the lateral malleolus; the peroneus brevis tendon is cut high at the musculotendinous junction and passed through a tunnel in the distal fibula from posterior to anterior, then through a second tunnel in the talar neck, and sutured to itself — the construct reconstructs the ATFL direction.

**Chrisman-Snook.** A long lateral incision from 7.5 cm proximal to the malleolar tip to the base of M5; the peroneus brevis tendon is split longitudinally in two halves, one half withdrawn beneath the retinacula and passed through a horizontal tunnel in the lateral malleolus and through the calcaneus, reconstructing both ATFL and CFL — controls both ankle and subtalar instability, at the cost of greater stiffness and varus risk.

**Kopchev (Bulgarian school).** An Achilles tendon flap; silk sutures repair the Achilles with a lateral fascial flap, while two vertical parallel canals and one horizontal tunnel are made through the lateral malleolus between the calcaneal tuber and the lateral talar process, through which a tendon flap is passed — reconstructing both CFL and ATFL.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Choice of technique in chronic lateral instability: Brostrom-Gould (anatomic repair + retinaculum augmentation) — young patient with a good ATFL remnant and no hyperlaxity; Watson-Jones (peroneus brevis tenodesis to fibula and talus) — reconstructs the ATFL direction; Chrisman-Snook (half peroneus brevis through fibula and calcaneus) — simultaneous control of ankle and subtalar instability; Kopchev — Bulgarian classical alternative in open post-traumatic Achilles defects.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Reconstruction of recurrent peroneal tendon dislocation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For recurrent peroneal tendon dislocation (absent or shallow fibular groove, torn superior peroneal retinaculum) — a 10–12 cm incision parallel to the fibula behind the lateral malleolus. A wide U-shaped flap is raised from the deep leg fascia with its base on the malleolus. The retromalleolar groove is cleared and deepened with a chisel if necessary; the tendons are reduced and covered with the preformed fascial flap, which is sutured to the retinaculum remnant or to the lateral calcaneal periosteum.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Malleolar fracture fixation and syndesmosis (Heim)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Lateral malleolus.** Anterolateral longitudinal incision with care for the sural nerve. For an oblique fracture line (SER or PER mechanisms) — two interfragmentary screws from anterior to posterior. For a transverse fracture with calcaneofibular ligament avulsion — tension-band wiring (cerclage and K-wire through the malleolar tip). For comminution — lateral plating (one-third tubular, reconstruction or anatomic distal fibular plate).

**Medial malleolus.** Anterior L-shaped incision allows inspection of the anteromedial joint surface (to rule out osteochondral lesions). Small avulsion fragments — pull-out suture or anchor; larger fragments — two 4.0 mm cancellous screws from the malleolar tip upward. Interposed periosteum must be removed before reduction.

**Sequence in bimalleolar fractures.** In PER mechanisms the lateral malleolus is fixed first (fibular reduction automatically realigns the medial). In SAD the opposite: medial first.

**Syndesmosis (Heim, 1964).** Conservatively treated torn syndesmosis leads in 56% of cases to recurrent mortise insufficiency and subsequent arthritis. Therefore the syndesmosis must be repaired and avulsion fragments refixed. An approximately 5 cm incision parallel to the anterior border of the distal fibula; 1–4 cm proximal to the joint line a positioning screw (3.5 mm) is passed through the fibula into the tibia in 30° of dorsiflexion and maximal compression. The screw engages 3 or 4 cortices according to preference and is removed at about 12 weeks before full weight bearing. For chronic syndesmotic insufficiency — plastic reconstruction with fascia (Heim, 1964), with peroneus brevis tendon (Petracic-Dürr, 1976), or heterologous tendon (Voorhoeve-Vriemoed, 1973). A permanent intersyndesmotic screw without removal leads to tibiofibular synostosis, which also provokes arthritis.

**Posterior malleolus (Volkmann).** When the fragment involves more than one-third of the tibial articular surface, anatomic reduction and fixation are required. Restoration of fibular length often reduces the Volkmann triangle via the posterior tibiofibular ligament. Definitive fixation — anterior-to-posterior cancellous screw through a separate small anterior incision proximal to the joint line, with care for the extensor tendons; alternatively — antiglide plating through a posterolateral approach.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Talar fractures (Ikonomov, Hawkins)</summary>

**Talar neck.** Medial osteotomy approach or combined dual approach (anteromedial + anterolateral); anatomic reduction and fixation with two cancellous screws or a screw-and-wire combination — Ikonomov technique from medial-distal toward dorsal into the trochlea. The approach spares the medial and plantar nutrient branches of the posterior tibial artery and the tarsal branch of the dorsalis pedis.

**Talar body.** Medial trans-malleolar approach with osteotomy of the medial malleolus and fixation with screws or K-wires.

**Posterior process of the talus (Cedell, Shepherd).** Posterolateral incision between the Achilles and peroneal tendons with dorsal capsulotomy and fixation with microscrews or K-wire; to be distinguished from symptomatic os trigonum.

**Comminuted talar fracture.** Classically — primary astragalectomy. To avoid shortening and instability, H. C. Blair (1943) proposed tibiotalar arthrodesis with a cortical graft from the anterior distal tibia, slid into a preformed slot in the talar neck — Blair's technique remains historical but is clinically applicable in severe talar loss with relatively preserved head and neck.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Ankle arthrodesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The principal solution in advanced arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, post-traumatic deformity, paralytic instability, talar AVN (stage III–IV), and after failed arthroplasty.

**Schwartz (1946).** Classical intra-articular arthrodesis through an L-shaped lateral Kocher approach.

**Charnley (1951; Müller, 1977).** Compression arthrodesis with external fixation. Two longitudinal incisions, one over each malleolus. Oblique osteotomy of the lateral malleolus about 3 cm from its tip; resection of the medial malleolus, distal tibial articular surface, and trochlea of the talus parallel to the tibial plafond. The arthrodesis is positioned at 90° (neutral flexion–extension) in women and 10° dorsiflexion in men (corresponding to heel height). Two Steinmann pins — first through the calcaneus, second transversely through the tibia; a Charnley external fixator provides dynamic compression with initial loading from days 5–6. From the 1980s onward, Ilizarov-type compression-distraction self-regulating systems provide dosed correction and compression simultaneously.

**Putti.** Anterior midline approach; the skin wound is opened together with the tendons. The tibialis anterior is incised if needed. The capsule is opened transversely, the malleoli obliquely osteotomised, the articular cartilages freshened, and after 2–3 cm of distraction a cortical graft from the tibia is inserted, fixed with a screw or supported by a bone bridge.

**Anterior plate (Anderson 1945, Crawford-Adams).** Anterolateral approach; after articular cartilage resection a corticocancellous graft from the distal tibia with a sharpened tip is driven into the trochlea (Adams triangular graft). The Crawford-Adams technique is a modification with multilevel resection and a wide anterior plate.

**Posterior tibial bone graft (Brittain modification).** Tibia-to-calcaneus extra-articular arthrodesis, historically used in tuberculous ankle and where intra-articular fusion is not feasible.

**Chuinard-Petersen (1963).** Preservation of the medial and lateral malleoli as natural buttresses after articular cartilage resection.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Desired position for ankle arthrodesis: neutral flexion–extension (0°), 5° external rotation relative to the contralateral limb, 5° hindfoot valgus, no anterior or posterior translational offset of the talus. In women 10° equinus may be tolerated if a high heel is routinely worn; in men strict neutral. Common errors: extension equinus (causes genu recurvatum), hindfoot varus (loads the lateral border and provokes M5 stress fractures), and posterior translational offset (shortens the lever arm and produces base-of-heel pain).</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Total ankle arthroplasty</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In selected patients (older with bilateral disease, rheumatoid arthritis, adjacent foot pathology) — an alternative to arthrodesis preserving motion. Classical 1970s–1980s designs: Smith, St. Georg (Buchholz), Mayo, Newton, Imperial College London (Kempson), TPR (Pappas), Schöll (Scholz), Buechel-Pappas (1973), Kofoed (1980s); they introduced the principle of a semi-constrained tibiotalar interface with a polyethylene insert.

**Technique.** Lateral decubitus (on the healthy side), Esmarch tourniquet. Approach — lateral Kocher with sural nerve protection. The fibula is depriostied and transversely osteotomised at the planned tibial resection level; before this a temporary malleolar screw is passed through the fibula into the tibia to preserve the length reference. The calcaneus is fixed with a transverse K-wire and a distraction frame for supination. With an oscillating saw the distal tibial surface is cut at the level of the mid-syndesmosis; the trochlea is cut parallel. Tibial and talar components are placed with a polyethylene insert. After fibular refixation with the malleolar screw, the tibiofibular ligaments and retinacula are repaired. Early rehabilitation — boot for 4–6 weeks and progressive weight bearing.

**Relative contraindications.** Severe deformity (> 20° varus or valgus), prior infection, Charcot, severe osteoporosis, low BMI, and a young active patient.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Soft-tissue correction: plantar fasciotomy, transfers, tenodeses</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-11/plantar-fascia.svg" data-figure-alt="Plantar fascia and arches of the foot" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/anatomy-11/plantar-fascia.svg" alt="Plantar fascia and arches of the foot" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Plantar aponeurosis with medial, central, and lateral bands radiating from the calcaneal tuber to the bases of the proximal phalanges. The static supporter of the medial longitudinal arch; its contracture contributes to pes cavus and is the target of Steindler stripping.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · InjuryMap, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Plantar_fascia.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Plantar fasciotomy (Steindler stripping).** In pes cavus, as part of soft-tissue correction. A 3–4 cm incision along the plantar-medial border in the middle third of the foot; the medial border of the fascia is identified, separated from the subcutaneous tissue and underlying muscles, and at passive dorsiflexion (which tightens the fascia) it is transected across its full width. Experienced surgeons may perform a closed fasciotomy with a tenotome.

**Detachment from the calcaneal tuber per Steindler.** The key step in rigid pes cavus. A medial incision curving toward the calcaneal tuber (no more than 3–4 cm to avoid injuring the medial plantar nerve); a scalpel and periosteal elevator along the medial and plantar surfaces of the calcaneus; the plantar fascia and short plantar muscles (abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, abductor digiti minimi) are detached as a single muscle-fascial block from the tuber.

**Tibialis posterior transfer to the dorsum (Watkins-Barr).** For paralytic equinus to provide active dorsiflexion. Four incisions: medial over the navicular for tendon detachment with or without a bony fragment; above the medial malleolus along the posterior tibia — for retrograde delivery into a subcutaneous tunnel on the anterior leg; just above the ankle for a wide window in the interosseous membrane and anterior passage of the tendon; dorsally over the appropriate metatarsal (usually II–III) for subperiosteal insertion under tension.

**Transfer of both plantar flexors (Tchaklin-Kaplan).** Tibialis posterior and flexor hallucis longus. Longitudinal posteromedial leg incision. The interosseous membrane is incised; tibialis posterior is passed along the medial border of the foot and sutured into a slot in M1; flexor hallucis longus is passed through the membrane and inserted into M5 or the cuboid (stabilising the lateral column); flexor digitorum longus, if transferred — into the navicular. A tenodesis of the long extensors to the metatarsal periosteum completes the construct. Cast immobilisation 3 weeks in ankle dorsiflexion and moderate knee flexion.

**Tchaklin extensor tenodesis.** Transection of tibialis anterior and the long extensors at the upper end of the incision; a transverse canal is made in the tibia about 5 cm above the joint, through which the tendons are passed against each other and sutured under tension. Tchaklin uses two superimposed canals; Camera routes the tendons through two additional tarsal canals to shorten them before tibial anchoring.

**Anterior tenodesis of Holevich.** Detachment of peroneus brevis, peroneus longus, and tibialis anterior — all brought anteriorly and sutured to each other and to themselves through an anterior tibial incision. Variants: in mild forms the tendons are routed through the interosseous membrane without compromising the Achilles; in Achilles retraction it is split frontally (separating the soleus and gastrocnemius components), with gastrocnemius routed anteriorly through the membrane and sutured into the anti-flexion construct.

**Posterior tenodesis of Boychev with Achilles shortening.** The Achilles is detached from the calcaneus, split longitudinally in two, a transverse canal is drilled in the tuber calcanei, and the two limbs are passed against each other through the canal and sutured under tension. This simultaneously shortens the tendon and creates a tenodesis.

**Posterior tenodesis of Zatsepin.** A flap from the medial half of the Achilles is passed through a canal in the medial malleolus 6 cm above the joint and sutured in equinus.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Achilles tendon lengthening (Z-, Hoke, White, Strayer, Silfverskiöld)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-11/achilles-rupture.jpg" data-figure-alt="Achilles tendon rupture" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/anatomy-11/achilles-rupture.jpg" alt="Achilles tendon rupture" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Spontaneous Achilles rupture, typically 4–6 cm proximal to the calcaneal insertion — characteristic in men aged 40–50 during sport; Lagergren-Lindholm hypovascular zone 2–6 cm above insertion. Palpable gap sign and positive Thompson test. Treatment: percutaneous suture (Cuneo-Bunnell-Ma), open Krackow-Mayo repair, or V–Y advancement (Abraham-Pankovich) for chronic ruptures with a defect.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · BruceBlaus, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Achilles_Tendon_Rupture.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>

For fixed equinus deformity in pes equinus, recurrent clubfoot, spastic cerebral palsy without fibrotic triceps surae, residual polio deformities, and congenital shortening.

**Open Z-lengthening.** A 10 cm posteromedial incision; the Achilles is dissected and Z-cut in the sagittal or frontal plane, the distal cut in the posteromedial half (protecting the medial vascular supply) and the proximal in the posterolateral. After passive dorsiflexion the two limbs are sutured at controlled lengthening with horizontal mattress sutures.

**Hoke percutaneous (1931).** Three transverse hemitenotomies — two medial and one lateral (or vice versa) at staggered heights (usually 2–3 cm apart); at passive dorsiflexion the tendon "slides" along its two halves and lengthens. The standard in the Ponseti protocol for congenital clubfoot (TAL — tendo Achillis lengthening in a child under 1 year).

**White.** Incomplete transverse division of the tendon at two different levels (distally in the lateral two-thirds, proximally 5–8 cm higher in the medial two-thirds) and passive longitudinal splitting at dorsiflexion — an elegant semi-open percutaneous alternative.

**Strayer gastrocnemius recession (1950).** Through a transverse posterior incision in the upper half of the leg — selective transection of the gastrocnemius aponeurosis at its junction with the soleus, without engaging the soleus. Indication: isolated equinus from gastrocnemius (positive Silfverskiöld test — equinus that resolves with knee flexion).

**Silfverskiöld (1923).** Detachment of both gastrocnemius heads from the femoral condyles — the original historical technique; converts the muscle from biarticular to monoarticular with action equivalent to the soleus and neutralises spasticity; increasingly rarely used in modern practice.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Phelps and Stoffel tibial nerve neurectomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

An alternative in spastic equinus deformity without fibrotic triceps surae changes. Posterior incision in the popliteal fossa 7–8 cm; the tibial nerve (superficial to the vessels) and its three motor branches to the gastrocnemius (medial and lateral) are identified, with sparing of the sensory branch. Mechanical or electrical stimulation identifies the branches to be cut — usually only the hyperactive heads. Over-denervation causes complete motor loss and is not recommended.

Stoffel described selective peripheral motor neurectomy on the same principle — only the hyperactive motor branches are cut, with bipolar stimulation testing and visible contraction.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Correction of pes equino-varus (clubfoot)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-11/clubfoot.jpg" data-figure-alt="Congenital pes equino-varus (clubfoot) — clinical photograph" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/anatomy-11/clubfoot.jpg" alt="Congenital pes equino-varus (clubfoot) — clinical photograph" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Congenital pes equino-varus (clubfoot): four components by the CAVE mnemonic — Cavus (high medial arch from plantar flexion of M1), Adductus (forefoot adduction), Varus (hindfoot varus), and Equinus (subtalar and ankle equinus). The Ponseti corrective sequence follows exactly this hierarchy.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Dr. Rajatsubhra, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CTEV_pre_management.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY 4.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Codivilla (1906) — three-stage.** Stage one: medial longitudinal incision from M1, curving around the medial malleolus and continuing proximally along the leg; lengthening of tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus, opening of the talonavicular, naviculocuneiform and cuneo-metatarsal joints from the medial side, transection of the contracted deltoid ligament. Stage two: lateral opening of the calcaneocuboid joint and, when necessary, the subtalar joint; with resistance — Z-lengthening of the Achilles and posterior capsulotomy. Stage three: passive correction into neutral followed by anatomic repair of the tendons under appropriate lengthening. Immobilisation 4–6 weeks.

**Zatsepin — the iconic Russian/Soviet standard.** Vertical incision through the middle of the medial malleolus. Z-cut of tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus; transverse division of the deltoid ligament; the joint is widely opened. The talocalcaneal interosseous ligament and other rigid structures are precisely divided; FHL is lengthened if needed. The tendons are repaired under tension so the heel rests in neutral. For forefoot adduction — a second incision at the base of the proximal phalanx of the hallux with lengthening of the abductor and short flexor. Immobilisation without hypercorrection (to avoid skin necrosis); the cast is changed on day 10–12 for supination correction; gradual extension to 6 months.

**Gerchev (Bulgarian school) — four-stage.** Stage one: medial incision from the M1 base to the middle-lower third of the leg; transection and lengthening of tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus; opening of the medial joints. Stage two: lateral incision along the Achilles; Z-lengthening in the sagittal plane with detachment of the medial half from the tuber. Stage three: if the heel remains high in equinus due to residual anterior talar subluxation, a small transverse incision below the lateral malleolar tip allows division of the posterior portions of the lateral ligaments and reduction of the talus into the mortise. Stage four: with the foot in full correction, the tendons are repaired under tension and the incisions closed.

**Katsarov — combined with Ilizarov apparatus.** The Achilles is Z-lengthened, tibialis posterior is lengthened, tibialis anterior is detached for later transfer; three crossing K-wires are passed through the heel, midtarsus and forefoot, mounted on the distraction apparatus, and from day 2 progressive correction of equinus, adduction and supination plus foot lengthening begins. After correction — interposition (cancellous autografts in the subtalar zone for extra-articular arthrodesis).

**Stöffel — staged correction for adolescents and adults.** Three stages a month apart: first — soft-tissue release; second — Z-lengthening of the Achilles and lateral transfer of tibialis anterior; third — wedge resection of the subtalar and midtarsal joints with detachment of the peroneal tendons. The advantage is a minimal bone wedge, because the soft tissues have already "released" the correction.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The classical paediatric sequence in congenital pes equino-varus: under 1 year — Ponseti (serial casts, percutaneous Achilles tenotomy, brace); 1–5 years — posterior and posteromedial soft-tissue release (Turco, McKay, Cincinnati incision, Codivilla, Zatsepin); 5–10 years — combined bony and soft-tissue (Katsarov with distraction frame); over 10 years and for rigid recurrent — triple arthrodesis (Lambrinudi, Dunn, Hoke, Ryerson). Choice is governed by age, rigidity, and amount of bony deformity.</p>
</aside>

<summary>Correction of pes plano-valgus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-11/flat-feet.jpg" data-figure-alt="Pes planus — clinical view" aria-label="Enlarge">

  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Bilateral pes plano-valgus — collapse of the medial longitudinal arch with hindfoot valgus. Classification: flexible (paediatric; rarely requires surgery) vs. rigid (tarsal coalition, post-traumatic, neurogenic). Treatment ladder: insole → Achilles lengthening → medial soft-tissue procedures (Karchinov-Young, Lowman, Miller-Niedereker) → calcaneal osteotomy (Evans lengthening, Koutsogiannis medialising) → arthroereisis (MBA) in paediatric cases; Grice-Green extra-articular subtalar arthrodesis for rigid paediatric pes plano-valgus.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Χαράλαμπος Γκούβας, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flat_feet_(1).jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>

**Lowman.** Medial approach to the talonavicular joint; the talonavicular ligament is divided at the joint level; the navicular is isolated and from it (and partly from the talar head) a bone wedge is cut. If needed, the Achilles is lengthened. The tibialis anterior tendon is transferred into a slot in the inferior surface of the navicular for active longitudinal arch support.

**Hoke (1931).** Arthrodesis between talus, navicular and medial cuneiform; the tibialis posterior tendon is moved forward (to the tibialis anterior level) onto the tubercle of M1, lengthening its lever arm and reinforcing active arch support.

**Miller-Niedereker (Cole-Miller).** Double arthrodesis between talus, navicular and medial cuneiform, plus anterior transfer of tibialis posterior and Achilles lengthening.

**Lelièvre.** Subtalar arthroereisis through a lateral incision from the posterior heel to the cuboid, directed dorsally; entry into the sinus tarsi and opening of the subtalar joint; from the anterior calcaneus an osteotomy directed posteroinferiorly, and a corticocancellous graft from the tibia in the sinus tarsi limits calcaneal pronation.

**Chambers.** Subtalar arthroereisis to limit calcaneal pronation — a longitudinal lateral incision from the posterior heel to the cuboid; entry into the sinus tarsi; an osteotomy from the anterior calcaneus directed posteroinferiorly; an iliac crest graft in the sinus tarsi blocks eversion. Particularly suited to paediatric flexible flatfoot with the calcaneal apophysis still open.

**Karchinov-Young (Bulgarian modification).** A curved medial incision to the medial malleolus; the tibialis anterior tendon is cut 2 cm above its navicular insertion and reflected proximally; beneath the navicular and medial cuneiform tuberosities a slot is created; the tendon is pulled posteriorly and downward into the slot beneath the distal portion of tibialis posterior. The tibialis posterior is repaired under tension. Immobilisation 6–8 weeks.

**Grice-Green extra-articular subtalar arthrodesis (1952).** The iconic paediatric procedure for plano-valgus correction without disturbing calcaneal growth. A short arched incision over the sinus tarsi; excision of soft tissues from the subtalar surfaces; transection of the talocalcaneal interosseous ligament; transverse slots are made in the posterior part of the talar neck (inferior surface) and the anterior part of the upper calcaneal surface, into which a corticocancellous graft from the iliac crest or fibula (3 cm long) is placed. The graft blocks the subtalar joint in neutral while preserving growth. Immobilisation 3 months; cast is staged down with progressive weight bearing.

**Arthroereisis with an implant (MBA — Maxwell-Brancheau).** A modern alternative: a conical metallic or biodegradable plug inserted through the sinus tarsi, blocking calcaneal eversion. Results are durable in correctly selected patients; with pain or impingement — implant removal at 18–24 months.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Correction of pes cavus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Plantar release procedures (Steindler stripping) are described above. Bony correction:

**Kuslik lateral crescent-shaped tarsectomy.** Dorsolateral arched incision; exposure of the talar head, navicular and cuboid; with a wide gouge a crescent-shaped wedge is cut from the midtarsus. The foot is realigned. With concomitant heel varus — additional wedge resection of the subtalar joint.

**Combined Tchaklin technique.** Two longitudinal incisions on the dorsum over Chopart's joint; retraction of the extensors and dorsalis pedis; opening of Chopart's joint and excision of a dorsally based wedge, including the talar head, navicular, anterior calcaneus and part of the cuboid. If equinus persists — Achilles lengthening. For varus — lateral transfer of tibialis anterior over the dorsum.

**Lefèvre and Cole.** Variants of tarsectomy with different wedge orientations.

**Dwyer calcaneal osteotomy (1959).** For heel varus that cannot be corrected by other methods; suitable from age 3–4. "Opening" Dwyer — incision below and behind the medial malleolus; a linear osteotomy beneath the sustentaculum tali and behind the posterior facet directed posterosuperior; opening with chisel and placement of a corticocancellous wedge. "Closing" Dwyer — incision behind the lateral malleolus; depriostiation of the lateral calcaneus; an elliptical wedge with apex medial, closing with internal fixation.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Triple arthrodesis (Lambrinudi, Hoke, Dunn, Ryerson; Russian modifications)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Triple arthrodesis (fusion of the subtalar, talonavicular, and calcaneocuboid joints in a single bone block) is the standard correction of rigid three-joint hindfoot deformity (pes cavus, pes plano-valgus, post-traumatic, post-polio, recurrent clubfoot). A large portion of the talus loses its blood supply and is treated as a free graft — prolonging consolidation and creating a risk of avascular necrosis.

**Hoke (1921, original).** A lateral incision from the talar head posteriorly to the tip of the lateral malleolus; division of the talonavicular ligament and wide opening of the talonavicular joint; subtalar joint resection; transverse osteotomy of the talar neck with removal of the talar neck and head.

**Dunn (1922) — double resection.** Arched incision behind the lateral malleolus, curving around it anteriorly to the navicular; wide opening of the subtalar and midtarsal joints; an anteriorly-based subtalar wedge (with talar neck and head resection); a horizontal slot at the navicular base to receive the anterior part of the talus.

**Lambrinudi (1927) — iconic for paralytic or post-polio equinus foot.** An arched incision behind the lateral malleolus, curving anteriorly and ending before the navicular; retraction of the peroneals and dorsiflexors; opening of the subtalar and midtarsal joints; a large anteriorly-based subtalar wedge (with talar neck and head resection); a slot at the navicular base. **The Lambrinudi point:** the distal talar resection is angled so that on insertion of the talus into the navicular slot the foot rests in neutral (no equinus) — the talus "serves" as a static dorsiflexor, counteracting the paralysed tibialis anterior. Immobilisation 3 months.

**Ryerson (1923).** Modified triple arthrodesis with smaller wedges and preservation of the talar head; appropriate for younger patients with smaller deformities.

**Nedrigailova and Novachenko-Nikolaev.** Russian variants with different wedge orientations.

**Pan-talar arthrodesis.** Vainio and Djanelidze remove the talus, excise its cartilage and reinsert it as a free graft — reducing consolidation time and AVN risk. Ivanov's method adds talar block sanitation.

**Compression triple arthrodesis of Katsarov.** With external fixator and K-wires providing dosed compression with weight bearing from day 5–6.

**Isolated subtalar arthrodesis.** For painful post-traumatic stasis after calcaneal or talar fracture and some variants of rigid paediatric plano-valgus. An arched lateral incision; opening of the peroneal tendon sheath with posterior-inferior retraction; entry into the sinus tarsi and opening of the subtalar joint; forced supination and cartilage resection to bleeding bone; if needed — wedge resection for varus/valgus correction. Fixation with a screw or K-wires; immobilisation 3 months.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Calcaneal spur and Haglund's disease</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Calcaneal spur (beneath the plantar fascia insertion).** A horseshoe medial incision below the heel apex; detachment of the fascia and short plantar flexors from the calcaneal tuber; exposure of the exostosis and chisel resection. Weight bearing is allowed at 15 days.

**Haglund's disease.** Posterosuperior lateral calcaneal exostosis with retrocalcaneal bursitis and Achilles enthesopathy. Techniques:
- Du Vries — lateral incision along the Achilles contour with depriostiation of the posterosuperior surface and chisel excision of the protruding bony segment; care for the Achilles insertion.
- Keck-Kelly — lateral wedge osteotomy with dorsal rotation.
- Hoffa — horizontal calcaneal osteotomy with dorsal rotation of the posterior part and screw fixation; often with concomitant Achilles shortening.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Hallux valgus correction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-11/hallux-valgus.jpg" data-figure-alt="Hallux valgus — radiographic HVA and IMA angles" aria-label="Enlarge">

    <span class="md-figure-caption">Hallux valgus: anteroposterior weight-bearing radiograph with a dorsomedial exostosis ("bunion"), lateral deviation of the great toe, and medial deviation of M1. Key angles: HVA (normal &lt; 15°), IMA (normal &lt; 9°), DMAA (normal &lt; 10°).   </figcaption>
</figure>

Correction requires elimination of the first metatarsal varus, correction of the hallux valgus angle, potential sesamoid repositioning, and preservation of muscle balance.

**Boychev.** For mild passively correctible deformity. A medial longitudinal incision from the hallux to the navicular, around the dorsal exostosis. The bursa is excised, MTP-1 is opened, the exostosis is resected at the level of the sagittal sulcus separating it from the head. Just below the metatarsal head a medially-based closing wedge is excised, sized to fully correct the valgus. Stabilisation — a single transverse suture between the MTP-1 and MTP-2 capsules, with K-wire or splint fixation.

**Tchokanov (Boychev modification).** Instead of a double metatarsal osteotomy, an opening-wedge is made in the medial cuneiform; a bone block taken from the metatarsal head is placed in the opened wedge.

**McBride (1928).** An arched longitudinal incision over the MTP-1 dorsum; division of the long extensor and the M1 neck; entry into the first interspace, detachment of the adductor hallucis from the base of the proximal phalanx and its redirection (modern analogue — transfer of the adductor hallucis to the M1 neck). Exostosectomy and medial capsulorrhaphy. The sesamoids are released and repositioned beneath the head.

**Lelièvre — five principles.** (1) preserve the length of M1 and its head; (2) preserve MTP-1 mobility; (3) reposition laterally subluxed sesamoids; (4) eliminate M1 varus; (5) eliminate hallux rotation. The technique includes distal chevron or scarf osteotomy with lateral release, medial capsulorrhaphy, and exostosectomy.

**Keller-Brandes (1904).** A dorsomedial arched incision from the distal third of the proximal phalanx to the metatarso-cuneiform joint; a capsular flap; resection of 1/3 to 1/2 of the proximal phalanx; resection of the exostosis and osteophytes. The freed hallux allows passive correction of varus; in very severe deformity — additional transverse osteotomy at the metatarsal base. Used primarily in older patients with advanced MTP-1 arthritis (hallux rigidus).

**Swanson silicone arthroplasty.** Single-stem for rigid moderate hallux valgus with relatively preserved metatarsal head; double-stem for severe rheumatoid deformities with lateral toe drift, hallux valgus senilis, or after failed prior surgery.

**Hoffmann (1912).** For severe rheumatoid deformity with dislocations and lateral toe drift. A transverse plantar or dorsal elliptical incision at the metatarsal head level; resection of M2–M5 heads (or M1–M5 in the most severe cases); resection of the sesamoids and proximal subluxations; closure after excision of redundant plantar flap (Fowler modification).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Hammer toe, claw toe, and quintus varus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Girdlestone-Taylor.** A 2.5 cm dorsolateral incision from the metatarsal neck to the DIP; exposure of the extensor expansion; detachment of the short and long flexor tendons and dorsal rerouting around the lateral phalangeal surface for self-suture — flexor-to-extensor transfer.

**PIP arthroplasty per Du Vries.** Elliptical skin excision over the PIP; resection of the distal end of the proximal phalanx and pegging of the distal end into a recess in the middle phalanx; fixation with a longitudinal K-wire.

**Hallux flexus per Lapidus.** Wide MTP-1 capsulotomy, detachment of the long flexor hallucis, and capsulotomy of the naviculocuneiform and cuneo-metatarsal joints. With overactive tibialis anterior — Meary transfer onto the lateral border.

**Quintus varus per Karchinov.** A lateral incision along the anterior half of the foot and toe; wide medial and dorsal capsulotomy of MTP-5; release of extensor contracture; detachment of the abductor digiti minimi and subcutaneous transfer of the distal portion of the extensor through a bone canal in the proximal phalanx for passive stabilisation. Alternative — Lapidus technique with extensor tenotomy and transfer through a bone canal.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Metatarsus varus and fallen transverse arch</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Gerchev (Bulgarian school) — three-stage for children 3–6 years.** Stage one: medial incision from MTP-1 to the tip of the medial malleolus; Z-lengthening of the adductor hallucis; detachment of tibialis anterior; capsulotomy of the cuneo-metatarsal-1 and naviculo-cuneiform joints. Stage two: lateral incision; detachment and oblique division of peroneus longus, reinsertion more distally under tension. Stage three: K-wire fixation of the correction.

**Heyman-Strong.** A wide dorsal transverse incision or a series of parallel longitudinal incisions in the interspaces; wide capsulotomies of the tarsometatarsal joints of all five metatarsals; passive correction of the adductus and K-wire fixation, one through M1 and one through M5 into the midtarsus.

**Camera (Ottavio Camera) for fallen transverse arch.** A longitudinal dorsal incision over the second interspace; detachment of the long extensor of the second or third toe at the toe base and transfer through a subcutaneous tunnel and a canal in the metatarsal, sutured to itself under tension. Often supplemented with tibialis anterior transfer for construct reinforcement.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Foot amputations (Sharp, Chopart, Lisfranc, Syme, Pirogov)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Classical foot amputations build a functional residual limb with dorsal and plantar skin flaps, the plantar always longer and covering the entire weight-bearing surface.

**Toe amputation.** Two skin flaps, longer plantar; tendon transection of the extensors and flexors with high nerve transection (to avoid neuroma).

**Single metatarsal amputation.** A racquet-shaped flap with longer handle along the dorsum; subperiosteal dissection and tendon transection at distal traction; nerve transection at a higher level.

**Sharp transmetatarsal.** Longer plantar flap covering the stump; metatarsals transected transversely at a level providing support and balance (usually proximal third). Postoperative splint and progressive prosthetic fitting.

**Chopart disarticulation** (between the midtarsal and tarsal bones). Removes the dorsiflexor function while preserving the Achilles insertion, causing the residual foot to drift progressively into equinus deformity. Technical approach — transverse incision along the projection of Chopart's joint; opening of the talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints; division of capsules and plantar ligaments. Concurrent Achilles transfer anteriorly to the talar neck is recommended for neutralisation (Boyd adds talocalcaneal arthrodesis for added stability). Because of the equinus and plantar ulcer risk, Chopart is often abandoned in favour of transmetatarsal or Syme.

**Lisfranc disarticulation** (between the tarsals and metatarsals). On the Hey-Lisfranc principle, with a longer plantar flap; division of Lisfranc's ligament and resection of the M2 base if preserved.

**Syme transankle amputation (1842).** Essential prerequisite — the plantar heel skin must be fully preserved and vascularised. The incision begins at one malleolus, encircles the heel from the plantar surface, and ends at the tip of the other malleolus; from here it turns over the anterior ankle and joins the start. The anterior half of the joint is opened; the foot is forced into maximal plantar flexion and the joint opened panoramically; the talofibular, deltoid and Achilles ligaments are sequentially divided. The foot is removed together with the talus and calcaneus, preserving the cutaneous-fat heel pad with full vascular supply from plantar branches of the posterior tibial artery. Both malleoli are transversely cut at their bases, leaving a small cartilage-bearing tibial articular zone. The skin flap is rotated upward and anteriorly and sutured to the anterior flap. The alternative "high Syme" resects the tibia above its articular surface. Standard for diabetic forefoot gangrene with intact plantar circulation and for severe congenital deformities.

**Pirogov (1854) — the iconic Russian osteoplastic technique.** The foot is amputated at the ankle level, but the heel (with the posterior half of the calcaneus) is preserved and rotated vertically against the resected tibia for bony fusion. Achieves a functional residual limb with natural heel support; rarely used today but remains part of the Russo-Soviet orthopaedic heritage.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Treatment of ingrown toenail (Hadzhistamov)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For unguis incarnatus — total or partial nail plate removal. Total: the nail is undermined with a probe, scissors or other instrument and avulsed with a Kocher; granulations are curetted; sterile ointment dressing. Partial: undermining of the ingrown part; longitudinal nail plate division and removal; granulation curettage; single suture to close the dead space and sterile dressing.

**The Hadzhistamov improvement** creates a triangular skin flap at the nail base, which after curettage is returned to its place and sutured with separate stitches — reducing recurrence.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Anaesthesia for foot surgery</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Ankle block.** Blockade of the posterior tibial nerve behind the medial malleolus, superficial peroneal nerve subcutaneously across the anterior ankle line, deep peroneal nerve deep between the tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus tendons, saphenous nerve subcutaneously medially, and sural nerve subcutaneously posterolaterally. The standard for distal foot surgery (hallux valgus, hammer toes, ingrown toenail) and in elderly patients with high anaesthetic risk.

**Popliteal or spinal/epidural.** Bulgarian tradition prefers spinal or epidural anaesthesia for arthrodesis, triple arthrodesis and Syme; popliteal block for more proximal ankle surgery with prolonged postoperative effect.

## Outcomes and Complications
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Soft-tissue complications.** Wound breakdown (5–25%) in the extensile lateral approach to the calcaneus — reduced to less than 5% with sinus tarsi; flap necrosis in the Holevich bimalleolar approach (1–3%); superficial or deep wound infection (1–4% in elective procedures, up to 10% in open fractures).

**Nerve injuries.** Sural nerve (posterolateral approach and lateral Kocher); superficial peroneal nerve (anterior and anterolateral ankle and dorsal foot approaches); saphenous nerve (medial approach); medial and lateral plantar nerves (plantar incisions, tarsal tunnel release); calcaneal branch of the tibial nerve (Baxter) — a frequently missed source of pain after calcaneal surgery.

**Vascular complications.** Anterior tibial artery and dorsalis pedis (anterior and dorsal approaches); posterior tibial artery (medial approach and tarsal tunnel); artery of the tarsal canal — injury in talar neck fractures → AVN of the corpus tali (20–100% by Hawkins).

**Arthrodesis complications.** Nonunion 5–15% in tibiotalar arthrodesis, higher in smokers and rheumatoid disease; adjacent joint arthritis (subtalar, midtarsal) 5 years after tibiotalar fusion; malalignment (heel varus/valgus, anterior or posterior talar translational offset) → M5 stress fractures, base-of-heel pain, genu recurvatum.

**Arthroplasty complications.** Aseptic periprosthetic loosening in total ankle arthroplasty (20–40% implant survival at 10 years in historical series; > 80% in modern designs); osteolysis, infection, dislocation.

**Deformity correction.** Recurrent clubfoot (5–20% with Ponseti, higher with soft-tissue release); recurrent hallux valgus with inadequate IMA correction; forefoot malalignment after triple arthrodesis (varus, fixed equinus); post-arthrodesis AVN of the talus after triple arthrodesis with neck resection.

**Systemic complications.** Thromboembolic (VTE) — increased risk with immobilisation and with ankle surgery; standard thromboprophylaxis for cast immobilisation over 7 days and for operative intervention with postoperative immobilisation.

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school; the University Hospital for Active Treatment in Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya bears his name. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curriculum reference and contains detailed descriptions of the classical approaches and procedures in the ankle and foot, including the parahallux posterior approach, hallux valgus correction with medial closing wedge, and posterior tenodesis with Achilles shortening.

**Yanaki Holevich <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bimalleolar approach with double osteotomy of both malleoli — wide panoramic exposure of the tibiotalar joint for total arthroplasty, talar tumour resection, and complex arthrodesis. Anterior tenodesis of Holevich — anterior transfer of peroneus brevis, longus, and tibialis anterior sutured in an anterior tibial incision.

**Boychev-Holevich <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Trans-Achilles posterior approach with Z-incision in the frontal plane and entry through Kager's space between flexor hallucis longus and the peronei — the Bulgarian classical maximum posterior exposure.

**Ivan Katsarov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Medial approach to the calcaneus for medialising calcaneal osteotomy; compression triple arthrodesis with external fixator and K-wires with dosed compression and weight bearing from day 5–6; the classical method of correcting recurrent pes equino-varus with an Ilizarov apparatus.

**G. Ikonomov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Technique of osteosynthesis of talar neck fractures with a screw-and-wire combination from medial-distal toward dorsal into the trochlea — spares the medial and plantar nutrient branches of the posterior tibial artery.

**Boychev and Tchokanov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Boychev — closing wedge osteotomy at the base of M1 for hallux valgus correction in mild passively correctible deformity. Tchokanov — opening-wedge medial cuneiform osteotomy with a wedge from the M1 head as interposition; a Boychev modification for moderate to severe IMA.

**Karchinov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian contributions: quintus varus technique with transfer of the distal extensor through a bone canal in the proximal phalanx; Karchinov modification of Young's method for flatfoot — transfer of tibialis anterior beneath tibialis posterior.

**Gerchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Four-stage medial-lateral release for pes equino-varus with division of the posterior lateral ligaments for reduction of the talus into the mortise; three-stage correction of metatarsus varus in children 3–6 years.

**Hadzhistamov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Improved technique for unguis incarnatus with a triangular skin flap at the nail base, returned and sutured after granulation curettage — reduces recurrence.

**Kopchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian technique for chronic lateral instability using an Achilles tendon flap and reconstruction of CFL and ATFL through tunnels in the lateral malleolus and calcaneus.

**V. D. Tchaklin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Extensor tenodesis through a transverse tibial canal (two superimposed canals); combined pes cavus technique (two dorsal incisions over Chopart's joint with a dorsally-based wedge); transfer of both plantar flexors (Tchaklin-Kaplan).

**Zatsepin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Iconic Russian/Soviet standard for congenital pes equino-varus correction through a vertical medial malleolar incision with Z-lengthening of tibialis posterior and FDL and wide opening of the medial joints; posterior tenodesis with a flap from the medial half of the Achilles through a medial malleolar canal.

**Nedrigailova and Novachenko-Nikolaev <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Russian modifications of triple arthrodesis with different wedge orientations.

**Vainio, Djanelidze, Ivanov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Pan-talar arthrodesis with talar removal, complete cartilage excision, and reinsertion as a free graft.

**N. Pirogov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1854).** Osteoplastic transankle amputation with preservation of the heel (posterior half of the calcaneus) and its vertical rotation against the resected tibia — a functional residual limb with natural heel weight-bearing.

</div>
</details>

## Classical Sources

<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical line — ankle and foot surgery</strong>
  <p>The modern surgical anatomy of the ankle and foot rests on Pott (1768) for the ankle mortise and the fracture-dislocation; on Pirogov (1854) for the osteoplastic amputation preserving heel weight-bearing; and on Hoffa (1905) for the standard orthopaedic foot operations of the era. Codivilla (1906) opens the operative correction of clubfoot; Hoke (1921, 1931), Dunn (1922), and Lambrinudi (1927) define the classical triple arthrodesis; Steindler introduces the detachment concept in pes cavus correction; Watson-Jones, Chrisman-Snook, and Brostrom-Gould shape the evolution of lateral instability surgery; Charnley (1951) and Müller (1977) — compression arthrodesis.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Pott 1768</strong> — *Some Few General Remarks on Fractures and Dislocations* — ankle mortise (<a href="https://archive.org/details/b30549188">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Pirogov 1854</strong> — *Klinische Chirurgie* — osteoplastic ankle amputation.</li>
    <li><strong>Hoffa 1905</strong> — *Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie* (<a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderortho00hoff">Facsimile</a>).</li>

    <li><strong>Hoke 1921 / Dunn 1922 / Lambrinudi 1927 / Ryerson 1923</strong> — classical triple arthrodesis and its modifications.</li>
    <li><strong>Charnley 1951</strong> → <strong>Müller 1977</strong> → <strong>Ilizarov 1980s</strong> — compression ankle arthrodesis.</li>
