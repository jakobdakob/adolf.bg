## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The pediatric skeleton differs from the adult skeleton in three fundamental respects that determine the entire pediatric fracture repertoire: the presence of the physis (growth plate) at the ends of the long bones; a thicker, more cellular, more vascular, and biologically more active periosteum; and a more porous, more elastic, less stiff bone tissue. These three features produce the specific fracture patterns (Salter–Harris epiphysiolyses, plastic deformation without a fracture line, greenstick fracture, metaphyseal buckle/torus), the unique healing characteristics (rapid consolidation, powerful remodeling), and a fundamentally different operative strategy (preservation of the physis, use of elastic implants, periosteum-sparing dissection, early mobilization).

This chapter presents the zonal anatomy and biology of the physis, the biomechanics of the growing bone, the Salter–Harris classification (1963) with the Rang modification and the related pediatric fracture patterns, the typical diagnostic imaging pitfalls, the differentiated approach to nonoperative and operative treatment, and finally the classical Bulgarian and Soviet school of limb-length equalization (Dzakhov, Dedov, Ilizarov, Iliev, Vasserstein, Bliskunov), of dosed growth arrest (Phemister 1933 → White–Stubbins → Bowen 1985 → Blount 1949 staples → Stevens 2007 8-plate) and of the classical procedures for specific pediatric conditions (Volkov–Oganesyan, Sofield–Millar, telescoping rods).

### Physis — Zonal Anatomy and Vascular Supply
<details id="physis">
<summary>Physis — Zonal Anatomy and Vascular Supply</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The physis is the cartilaginous plate at the metaphyseal–epiphyseal junction of the growing long bones, responsible for longitudinal skeletal growth. Histologically the physis is organized into characteristic horizontal zones, traversed from the epiphyseal toward the metaphyseal side.

**Reserve (germinal) zone.** Adjacent to the epiphysis; contains a relatively sparse, metabolically inactive population of resting chondrocytes embedded in a uniform proteoglycan-rich matrix. This is the "stem-cell" reservoir that supplies the proliferative zone.

**Proliferative zone.** Chondrocytes divide actively and arrange themselves in characteristic vertical columns like "coins in a stack." This zone is the true source of longitudinal growth — the number and height of the proliferating cells determine the growth rate.

**Hypertrophic zone.** Subdivided into three sub-zones. Zone of maturation — chondrocytes enlarge and produce extracellular matrix. Zone of degeneration — chondrocytes degenerate as the matrix calcifies. Zone of provisional calcification — the most distal portion, where the matrix calcifies and provides the substrate for new bone formation.

**Metaphysis.** The bony region adjacent to the physis, where the calcified cartilage matrix is replaced by woven bone, then by trabecular bone. The first blood vessels invade here from the metaphyseal arteries and form the characteristic metaphyseal sinusoids — the sluggish flow within them is the substrate for the hematogenous localization of acute childhood osteomyelitis.

Physeal failure occurs typically through the zone of provisional calcification and the hypertrophic zone — the biomechanically weakest layers — producing the characteristic Salter–Harris patterns.

**Vascular supply.** The vascular supply to the physis is unique: separate vessels supply the epiphyseal side (epiphyseal arteries entering through the articular cartilage or the periphery of the epiphysis) and the metaphyseal side (metaphyseal and nutrient arteries), with no vessels crossing the physis itself. This vascular pattern explains why displacement at a physeal fracture can disrupt the epiphyseal blood supply (with consequent avascular necrosis of the epiphysis) while the metaphyseal supply remains intact; and conversely, why a metaphyseal infection (hematogenous osteomyelitis) in a child under 1 year of age can leap directly across into the physis and epiphysis through the remaining embryonic vessels, before the "barrier" has been established.

**Perichondrial ring (of Ranvier).** Surrounds the physis peripherally and contributes to circumferential growth — the widening of the physis and metaphysis. Between Ranvier's ring and the cortex lies the bony ring of La Croix, which contributes to the peripheral rigidity of the physis. Injuries to the perichondrial ring (direct trauma, infection, thermal or chemical injury during pinning) can produce peripherally localized growth arrest with progressive angular deformity.

### Periosteum, Cortex, and Metaphysis
<details id="periosteum">
<summary>Periosteum, Cortex, and Metaphysis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Pediatric periosteum.** The periosteum of the immature skeleton is significantly thicker, more cellular, more vascular, and biologically more active than the adult periosteum. The outer fibrous layer is dense and resistant to disruption; the inner cambium layer is multipotent and provides appositional widening growth and rapid callus-driven healing. This thickness and toughness produces the clinical hallmarks: the periosteum often remains intact on one side of the fracture and acts as a hinge facilitating reduction; it limits initial displacement (including under significant angulation) and provides the biological substrate for rapid consolidation. The periosteal "sleeve" also explains the periosteal-sleeve fracture of the lateral clavicle in adolescents (Neer IV) — the bone strips out of the periosteum but the periosteum remains intact, so subsequent bone formation often regenerates the original clavicular anatomy.

**Cortex.** The pediatric cortex is more porous (with greater volume of Haversian canals and intracortical vessels), giving it a lower elastic modulus and a higher capacity to absorb energy before failure. The lower tensile strength per unit area is offset by this elasticity — hence the specific "incomplete fracture" patterns (greenstick, plastic deformation, buckle), in which loading exceeds the elastic limit of one side of the bone but not the other.

**Metaphysis.** The metaphysis is widened, spongiose, less cortical, and more susceptible to compression. The buckle (torus) fracture — typical at the distal radius after a fall on the outstretched hand — is a direct consequence of this metaphyseal spongiosis: the axial force buckles the trabecular bone beneath the cortex in the subcortical zone without producing a complete fracture line.

### Growth, Healing, and Remodeling
<details id="growth-remodeling">
<summary>Growth, Healing, and Remodeling</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Tempo of longitudinal growth.** Longitudinal growth is the product of the proliferative and hypertrophic zones of the physis. The various physes of the long bones contribute differently to the final length of the segment: distal femur contributes approximately 9 mm/year, proximal tibia about 6 mm/year, proximal humerus about 7 mm/year, distal radius about 5 mm/year. The Anderson–Green–Messner "70:30 rule": the distal femur and proximal tibia together contribute approximately 70% of the growth of the lower limb (with the remaining 30% from the proximal femur and distal tibia). This distribution makes the physes about the knee the most critical in pediatric trauma with risk of limb-length discrepancy.

**Tempo of healing.** Pediatric bones consolidate significantly faster than adult bones. Typical consolidation times for a tibial diaphyseal fracture: newborn 2–3 weeks, infant 4 weeks, child 6–8 weeks, adolescent 8–10 weeks, adult 12–16 weeks. This pace is a direct consequence of the richer vascularity, more active periosteum, and powerful callus response. Pseudarthrosis (nonunion) in childhood is rare — the exceptions are congenital tibial pseudarthrosis and established chronic infection.

**Remodeling potential.** The remodeling capacity of the growing bone is a central consideration in choosing treatment. It depends on three principal factors. First — age: more years of remaining growth provide greater potential; infants tolerate substantial malalignment, while adolescents close to skeletal maturity tolerate it poorly. Second — proximity to an active physis: bones close to an active physis remodel more reliably, especially in the plane of the physis (by the Hueter–Volkmann rule compression slows and tension accelerates growth). Third — plane of deformity: angulation in the plane of motion of the adjacent joint (sagittal at knee, elbow, wrist) remodels better than angulation in the coronal plane; rotational malalignment does NOT remodel and requires anatomic correction. The acceptable malalignment under cast immobilization depends on these three parameters and increases dramatically at younger ages.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The distal femur (≈9 mm/year) and proximal tibia (≈6 mm/year) carry ≈70% of lower-limb growth — hence physeal injuries about the knee carry the highest clinical significance when predicting final limb-length discrepancy.</li>
    <li>Three conditions favor remodeling: younger age, proximity to an active physis, and deformity in the plane of motion of the adjacent joint. Rotational malalignment does NOT remodel and must be corrected anatomically, regardless of age.</li>
    <li>A femoral diaphyseal fracture in a 3-year-old tolerates up to 30° of sagittal angulation and 2 cm of shortening (which is compensated by the stimulated post-fracture overgrowth); the same fracture in a 12-year-old does not tolerate more than 10° and 1 cm.</li>

### Growth Arrest — Pathoanatomy
<details id="growth-arrest">
<summary>Growth Arrest — Pathoanatomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Growth arrest after a physeal injury occurs when a bony bridge forms between the metaphysis and the epiphysis across the lumen of the physis. The bridge ("bar") consists of trabecular bone or dense fibro-osseous tissue and anchors the epiphysis to the metaphysis, preventing further growth in that sector. By location three types are distinguished: central bridge (in the centre of the physis — the epiphysis remains as a "chimney" with progressive cupping), peripheral bridge (at the rim of the physis — producing a characteristic angular deformity because growth continues from the contralateral, uninjured side) and linear bridge (along a diameter — producing combined angular deformity and shortening).

The incidence of growth arrest depends on the type of initial injury: Salter–Harris I and II <10%, III about 20–30% (depending on quality of reduction), IV 30–50%, V essentially 100%. The clinical manifestation — limb-length discrepancy, progressive angular deformity — appears typically 6–12 months after injury, so all significant physeal injuries require serial follow-up for at least 12–24 months.

Imaging of an established bridge — plain radiography in two projections with "Harris growth-arrest lines" as an indirect marker; CT with thin slices and/or 3D-mapped physeal MRI (Berry, Bright standard) to measure the size and geometry of the bridge — a bridge under 50% of the physeal area in a patient with at least 2 years of remaining growth is the indication for a Langenskiöld bar resection.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Salter–Harris V is often radiographically silent — diagnosis is retrospective when limb-length discrepancy or angular deformity appears. Maintain suspicion at every axial compression of a physis, especially around the knee and ankle, and follow up serially.</li>
    <li>Periphyseal thermal injury from Kirschner-wire pinning (especially repeated drilling in the same spot) is an established iatrogenic cause of peripheral growth bridge — hence the recommendations to use thin smooth wires of minimum diameter and drill at high speed to minimize the thermal load.</li>
    <li>Metaphyseal hematogenous infection in an infant under 1 year of age can pass through the still-present embryonic trans-physeal vessels and infect the epiphysis with risk of AVN and complete growth arrest — hence the clinical rule of urgent drainage of osteomyelitis in infants.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Biomechanics of Pediatric Bone
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="biomechanics">
<details>
<summary>Biomechanics of Pediatric Bone</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Adult bone behaves as a quasi-brittle material: when the elastic limit is exceeded it fails with little plastic deformation. Pediatric bone is significantly more susceptible to plastic deformation before failure — a stress–strain curve with a long "plastic plateau" before rupture. This biomechanical fact explains the four classical "incomplete" fracture patterns that are almost exclusively pediatric.

**Plastic deformation.** Under bending forces that exceed the elastic limit but do not reach the failure strength, the bone bends permanently without a visible fracture line. Histologically, microfractures of the trabeculae and oblique micro-cracks of the compact cortex are detected, but not a complete dividing line. The most vulnerable bones are the thinner of the paired bones in paired segments — the ulna and the fibula.

**Greenstick.** Under higher bending force the tensile side fractures, while the compressive side remains intact (but usually plastically bowed). The analogy with a green tree branch is exact — the elastic cortex on the "inner" side preserves stability but allows only partial anatomic reduction.

**Buckle (torus).** Under axial compression in a metaphyseal zone, the trabecular bone collapses beneath the cortex, locally buckling the cortex without producing a complete fracture line. The classic location is the distal radius after a fall on the outstretched hand.

**Bowing.** Essentially a severe form of plastic deformation with a substantial angular component.

**Hueter–Volkmann principle.** The fundamental biomechanical law of the physis: compression perpendicular to the physis slows the local growth, while reduced pressure (tension or internal release of compression) accelerates it. This principle, formulated by Hueter <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> and Volkmann <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> in the late 19th century, is the experimental basis of the entire concept of guided growth — from Phemister's permanent epiphysiodesis, through Blount's staples, to the Stevens tension-band plate (8-plate). With compression on one side of the physis (paired tension plates or unilateral 8-plate) the growth on that side is slowed, while the contralateral side continues to grow — producing controlled correction of angular deformity.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The Hueter–Volkmann principle is the conceptual bridge between all classical and modern guided-growth methods: compression → slowing of local growth; off-loading → acceleration. The same principle underlies Ilizarov's distraction epiphysiolysis.</li>
    <li>Paired bones (forearm, leg) — the double "frame" implies that a fracture of one bone is often accompanied by plastic deformation of the other. "Missed" plastic deformation is a common cause of failed reduction of the visible fracture.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Landmarks and Imaging
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="landmarks-imaging">
<details>
<summary>Landmarks and Imaging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Imaging in the pediatric fracture starts from a clear understanding of the normal ossification centres and from systematic comparison with the contralateral side.

**Ossification centres.** The appearance and fusion of the secondary ossification centres follow a predictable sequence that varies by location and sex. The classical reference is the "CRITOL" mnemonic for the elbow — Capitellum (1 yr), Radial head (3 yr), Internal (medial) epicondyle (5 yr), Trochlea (7 yr), Olecranon (9 yr), Lateral epicondyle (11 yr) — with fusion by 14–16 yr. An unfused centre should not be mistaken for an avulsed fragment; the rule is: if "T" (trochlea) is visible, then "I" (medial epicondyle) must be visible — otherwise the medial epicondyle is avulsed and entrapped within the elbow joint dislocation (a classic pediatric pitfall).

**Comparison with the contralateral side.** Standard at every suspicion of a subtle physeal fracture, plastic deformation, buckle, or entheseal injury. The contralateral radiograph (with the same exposure protocol) reveals non-obvious asymmetries in the angle of standard axes, in segment length, or in the position of ossification centres.

**Stress radiographs.** At suspicion of Salter–Harris I (undisplaced fracture) and of purely ligamentous ankle injury in a child — stress radiographs under sedation (varus/valgus stress or instrumented anterior drawer) are routinely performed to distinguish physeal instability from a complete ligamentous injury.

**Imaging pitfalls.**

* Salter–Harris I — in small children with only a widened physis without displacement, especially at the distal tibia or fibula; often missed on plain radiography.
* Salter–Harris III and IV of the distal humerus — the specific "lateral condyle" fracture (Milch I/II) radiographically resembles minimally displaced SH-IV, but it is in fact unstable and requires operative fixation at displacement >2 mm.
* Distal tibia in adolescence — asymmetric physeal closure between ages 12 and 15 (medial half closes before the lateral) produces the classical juvenile Tillaux fracture (SH-III) and the triplane fracture (SH-IV with fragments in three planes).
* Plastic deformation of the fibula in a tibial fracture and of the ulna in a radial fracture — often radiographically illegible without comparison to the contralateral side.

**Imaging of growth arrest.** In an established physeal injury — Harris growth-arrest lines (horizontal radiodense lines in the metaphysis) serve as an indirect marker: if the line is parallel to the physis, growth is symmetric; if the line converges toward an angle, a localized bridge is present. Thin-slice CT and 3D-mapped MRI of the physis (Berry, Bright) are the standards for measuring the size and geometry of an established bridge before a potential Langenskiöld procedure.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>At every suspicion of a subtle pediatric peri-articular fracture — contralateral radiograph; normal ossification variants are much more common than true fractures.</li>
    <li>CRITOL sequence at the elbow: if the trochlea (T) is visible, the medial epicondyle (I) must be visible on a standard projection — its absence signals an avulsed fragment entrapped in the joint after dislocation.</li>
    <li>In adolescence (12–15 yr) the asymmetric physeal closure of the distal tibia (medial half before lateral) produces juvenile Tillaux (SH-III) and triplane (SH-IV in three planes) — CT is routinely required when suspected.</li>

The Salter and Harris classification (Robert B. Salter and W. Robert Harris, 1963, Toronto) divides physeal fractures into five main types by the relationship of the fracture line to the physis and to the adjacent epiphyseal and metaphyseal bony structures. The classification predicts both the immediate operative tactic (anatomic reduction required for types III and IV because of joint-surface involvement) and the risk of long-term growth disturbance.

**Mercer Rang modification (1969).** Rang added four further types to the original Salter–Harris classification to describe rarer patterns:

* Type VI — injury to the perichondrial ring of Ranvier (direct lateral trauma dissecting tangentially across the rim of the physis) → peripheral growth arrest.
* Type VII — isolated intra-articular injury to the epiphysis without physeal involvement (e.g. an osteochondral fracture).
* Type VIII — isolated metaphyseal injury with the potential to disrupt the physeal blood supply (postoperative local ischaemia).
* Type IX — injury to the periosteum with the potential to affect appositional growth.

  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Growth-arrest risk hierarchy: SH-I/II &lt;10% → III ~20–30% → IV 30–50% → V essentially 100%. Types III and IV cross the articular surface and demand anatomic reduction; type V is radiographically subtle and often diagnosed retrospectively.</li>

    <li>The Salter–Harris pattern is described from epiphyseal to metaphyseal: the fracture line "crosses" the physis on one side and exits on the other — hence SH-II is described as "physis + metaphysis," SH-III as "physis + epiphysis," and SH-IV as "all three."</li>

    <li>Salter–Harris V is radiographically silent initially. Growth arrest manifests weeks to months later — diagnosis is often retrospective. Maintain suspicion at any axial compression of a physis (especially around the knee and ankle) and follow up serially for at least 12–24 months with radiographs.</li>
    <li>SH-III and IV of the distal humerus — lateral condyle — are often unstable and at displacement above 2 mm require open anatomic reduction even at apparently modest size; a missed unstable lateral condyle fracture is one of the commonest causes of late cubitus valgus and late ulnar neuropathy in childhood.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Plastic Deformation
<details id="plastic-deformation">
<summary>Plastic Deformation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Plastic deformation is the persistent angular deformity of a bone without a discrete fracture line, produced by bending forces sufficient to exceed the elastic limit but insufficient to produce complete fracture. Histologically, microfractures of the trabeculae and obliquely oriented micro-cracks of the compact cortex are detected, allowing plastic deformation without complete disruption of bony integrity.

**Location.** The most common sites are the thinner of the paired bones in paired segments — the ulna in forearm injuries and the fibula in tibial injuries. The classical clinical setting is the pediatric both-bone forearm fracture with a frank radial fracture and concurrent plastic deformation of the ulna; missed plastic deformation of the ulna obstructs the reduction of the radial fracture. Less commonly, isolated plastic deformation of an entire bone is encountered (for example a femoral diaphysis in a high-energy injury to a young child).

**Recognition.** Requires careful inspection of the radiographs in two perpendicular projections with mandatory comparison to the contralateral side. The bone shows the characteristic bowing without a fracture line, sometimes with discrete metaphyseal buckling (a transitional form to buckle).

**Treatment.** Depends on the degree of angulation and the age.

* Angulation under 10° in a child under 6–8 yr — usually does not require active treatment, given the remodeling potential.
* Angulation of 10–20° in younger children, less in older children — closed reduction under anesthesia. Because the bone is plastically deformed, it does not "snap" into place; reduction requires deliberate over-correction — the bone is forced past the deformity into a slight reverse angulation, which then relaxes to a corrected position. The maneuver may require substantial and sustained force (minutes of steady pressure, not momentary manipulation).
* Persistent angulation that does not respond to closed reduction — may require completion of the deformation to a full fracture through a controlled osteotomy (usually a small tension osteotomy on the convex side), followed by anatomic reduction and fixation.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Missed plastic deformation of the ulna or fibula is a common cause of failed reduction of the visible fracture of the paired bone. In a forearm both-bone injury — compare the supposedly intact paired bone with the contralateral side; if it is bowed, deal with it (over-correct or planned tension osteotomy) BEFORE attempting to reduce the fractured paired bone.</li>
    <li>Over-correction of plastic deformation requires sustained (1–2 minutes) steady pressure with full muscular relaxation under anesthesia, not momentary manipulation — the latter usually ends with recurrence of the deformity on release.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Greenstick Fractures
<details id="greenstick">
<summary>Greenstick Fractures</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Hellerhoff, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gruenholzfraktur_-_Unterarmfraktur.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>

The greenstick fracture is the classical pediatric pattern of incomplete fracture, named by analogy with the bending of a green tree branch. The bone breaks on the tension cortex (the side of tensile loading), while the compression cortex (on the opposite side) remains intact, although usually plastically bowed.

**Mechanism.** A bending force that exceeds the failure strength of one cortical side (tension) but not the other (compression). The intact cortical side provides residual stability and tends to return the fracture toward its initial position when the deforming force is released.

**Location.** Commonest in the forearm (both bones, or isolated distal or middle diaphysis); can affect any bone. The clavicle, tibia, fibula, humerus, and femur are common pediatric sites.

**Treatment.** Minimally angulated fractures (under 10–15°) — cast immobilization without reduction; remodeling of the growing skeleton corrects the residual deformity. Significantly angulated fractures — closed reduction; with a substantial residual tendency to redeformation, controlled completion of the fracture (deliberate breaking of the intact cortical side for complete anatomic reduction) is often necessary. The fracture is then immobilized in a cast in the corrected position with serial radiographic follow-up in the first 1–2 weeks for detection of angulation recurrence.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The risk of angulation recurrence within the cast is a well-known problem, especially when the fracture is not completed. Serial radiographic follow-up (at days 5–7 and 10–14) is mandatory; on redisplacement — repeat reduction or operative treatment.</li>
    <li>The approach of "completing the greenstick" (deliberately breaking the intact cortical side) converts an unstable pattern into a stable one — the technique is standard for greensticks with angulation above 15° and for those with a tendency to redeformation in the cast.</li>
    <li>Greenstick of the radius with an intact ulna requires particular attention to pronation–supination — an "apex-volar" deformity is corrected with the forearm in supination; "apex-dorsal" — in pronation.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Buckle (Torus) and Bowing

<summary>Buckle (Torus) and Bowing</summary>

**Buckle (torus) fracture.** A compression fracture of the metaphyseal cortex without a complete fracture line, producing a characteristic "buckling" of the cortex. The name "torus" derives from the architectural toroidal shape of column bases — the circumferential buckling of the cortex. Most common at the distal radius after a fall on the outstretched hand; possible at other metaphyseal sites as well (distal tibia, proximal humerus).

Treatment is conservative: a low cast splint or removable orthosis for 3–4 weeks, with good consolidation and no risk of deformity. Over the past two decades, multiple randomized trials (the BUCKLE trial and others) have shown equivalence between a removable splint and cast immobilization — hence the standard shift toward a removable splint in isolated torus fractures of the distal radius.

**Bowing deformity.** Essentially a severe form of plastic deformation with a substantial angular component but no fracture line. Treatment is identical to that for plastic deformation (see above).

</details>


### Avulsion and Apophyseal Fractures
<details id="avulsion">
<summary>Avulsion and Apophyseal Fractures</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Avulsion fractures at apophyseal sites (where tendons or ligaments attach to ossifying secondary growth centres) are particularly common in adolescents during the time of apophyseal development, when the apophysis has already ossified enough to be radiographically recognizable as a discrete fragment but the synchondrosis with the parent bone is not yet closed and is weaker than the pulling tendon.

**Classical locations.**

* Anterior superior iliac spine — avulsion of the sartorius in sports.
* Anterior inferior iliac spine — avulsion of the rectus femoris.
* Ischial tuberosity — avulsion of the hamstrings in sprinters and dancers.
* Tibial tuberosity — avulsion of the patellar tendon with the Ogden classification (types I, II, III): type III with intra-articular extension requires anatomic reduction.
* Small hip avulsions — lesser trochanter (iliopsoas) and greater trochanter (gluteus medius).
* Medial epicondyle of the humerus in adolescents — avulsion by the pull of the flexor muscle mass under flexion–valgus stress, often associated with elbow dislocation (the entrapped intra-articular avulsed fragment is a classical pitfall — see the CRITOL section).

**Treatment.** Minimally displaced avulsions (under 1.5–2 cm) — rest, NSAIDs, partial weight bearing with progressive return to sport over 6–12 weeks. Significantly displaced avulsions (over 2 cm) or those with functional consequences (e.g. tibial tuberosity type III with intra-articular extension) — open reduction and fixation with screws or with trans-osseous sutures.

<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Nonoperative
<details id="nonoperative">
<summary>Nonoperative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Closed reduction is the cornerstone of treatment for the vast majority of pediatric fractures. The combination of a robust periosteum (providing a tissue hinge that guides reduction), favorable remodeling, and rapid healing means that many fractures can be treated by closed reduction and cast immobilization with acceptable substantial residual deformity. The "acceptable malalignment" approach in childhood proceeds from the knowledge that remodeling will correct residual angulation in the plane of joint motion, provided that age and physeal potential are sufficient.

**Closed reduction.** In greenstick — often with deliberate completion of the fracture for full anatomic position. In Salter–Harris I and II — gentle, without coarse manipulation, with the minimum number of reduction attempts (repeated coarse manipulation damages the germinal cells of the physis, with risk of secondary bridging). In plastic deformation — sustained steady pressure for over-correction, not momentary manipulation.

**Cast immobilization.** Requires attention to growth (avoiding circulatory compromise of an actively growing limb, monitoring for cast bivalving on swelling), to the mechanical principles of three-point fixation, and to the activity of children (with appropriate material strength, protection of vulnerable zones, and the surgeon educating the family about signs of compartment syndrome).

**Serial follow-up.** Radiographic follow-up at days 5–7 and 10–14 to detect angulation recurrence in greenstick and minimally stable patterns; on recurrence — repeat reduction or escalation to operative treatment.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>In Salter–Harris I and II — as few reduction attempts as possible. Coarse or repeated manipulation damages the proliferative and reserve zones, with risk of secondary growth bridging, even with a subsequently perfect position.</li>
    <li>A cast on a growing child is not "forget-and-cast" — it requires serial monitoring, checking of circulation and sensation, and readiness for cast change on swelling or repeat reduction on recurrence of deformity.</li>
    <li>In pediatric upper-limb fractures — long-arm cast for unstable, short-arm cast for stable patterns; forearm in neutral position or in specific supination/pronation according to the fracture mechanism.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Operative
<details id="operative">
<summary>Operative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<details>
<summary>Indications for Operative Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Specific indications for operative treatment of pediatric fractures include:

1. Open fractures — urgent debridement and stabilization (usually with external fixator for severe injury, flexible intramedullary nail for milder open type I–II).
2. Irreducible fractures or those that cannot be held in acceptable position by closed means.
3. Articular fractures Salter–Harris III and IV — anatomic reduction to avoid post-traumatic arthritis and bony bridging.
4. Fractures with associated neurovascular injury requiring direct surgical intervention.
5. Polytrauma in adolescents — stable fixation for nursing and early mobilization.
6. Pathological fractures in bone cysts, fibrous dysplasia, or other lesions.
7. Selected fractures in older adolescents near skeletal maturity — with limited remodeling potential and preference for adult-style anatomic reduction.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Flexible Intramedullary Nailing (TENS, Métaizeau)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The principal operative technique in pediatric diaphyseal long-bone fractures — forearm, femur, humerus. The Métaizeau titanium elastic nailing system (TENS) uses two flexible titanium nails of diameter 2.0–4.0 mm, introduced retrograde or antegrade through small metaphyseal incisions. The two nails bend in opposite directions and meet at the fracture site in a "C–C" configuration providing three-point fixation: proximal contact of one nail with the proximal metaphysis, distal contact of the other nail with the distal metaphysis, and contact of both at the fracture site. The construct avoids the physes and the principal blood supply, providing reliable union and excellent functional results.

Classical indications:

* Femoral diaphyseal fracture in a 5–12 yr child (standard; in younger children — cast or Pavlik harness in infants; in older — submuscular plate or adult intramedullary nail with sparing of the greater trochanter).
* Both-bone forearm fracture with inability to hold reduction in a cast.
* Humerus — selected diaphyseal patterns.
* Tibia — selected polytrauma or open patterns.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>K-wire Fixation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For smaller fractures — especially hand and foot fractures, distal radius, and supracondylar humerus. Wires are 1.0–2.0 mm in diameter and are removed at 4–8 weeks. Principles:

* Smooth wires when crossing of the physis is required — the smoothness allows sliding with growth without bridge formation.
* A minimum of 2 wires (to prevent rotation).
* In complex patterns — three wires (Lambrinos technique: latero-lateral + medial crossing), taking care of the ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel at the medial pin.
* High drill speed to reduce the thermal load on the physis.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Management of Growth Arrest</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Treatment of established growth arrest depends on location, size of the bony bridge, and remaining growth potential. The classical approaches:

**Observation with serial follow-up.** At least during the first year after injury, with attention to limb-length discrepancy and angular deformity — radiographs at standard intervals (3, 6, 12, 24 months).

**Bar resection (Langenskiöld procedure, 1967).** Removal of the bony bridge between the epiphysis and the metaphysis with interposition of fat (autologous), polymethylmethacrylate, or silicone to prevent reformation. Indications: small bridges (under 50% of the physeal area) in patients with at least 2 years of remaining growth potential. Success rate is about 50–70% — on success, the physis resumes normal function and the original deformity is corrected with continued growth.

**Lengthening, shortening, or corrective osteotomy.** For established limb-length discrepancy or angular deformity — the appropriate reconstructive approach (see the next subsections).

**Contralateral epiphysiodesis.** To equalize limb lengths by arresting growth on the unaffected side at the appropriate time — calculated by Anderson–Green–Messner, Moseley, or the Paley multiplier method.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The distal femur (≈9 mm/year) and proximal tibia (≈6 mm/year) carry the greatest clinical significance for growth arrest because of their disproportionate contribution to lower-limb length.</li>
    <li>Bar resection (Langenskiöld) works on bridges under 50% of the physeal area with at least 2 years of remaining growth potential, with 50–70% success. Larger bridges or proximity to skeletal maturity → length equalization and/or corrective osteotomy as the direct strategy.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Limb-Length Equalization — Bulgarian and Soviet Classical School</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Limb-length inequality in the lower extremities is a central topic of pediatric orthopaedics, uniting an etiologically diverse spectrum of congenital malformations (hypo- and aplasia of individual segments, congenital pseudarthroses), disturbances of longitudinal growth (chondrodystrophy, local hypertrophy, coxa vara, post-traumatic physeal injuries), and acquired diseases (osteoarticular tuberculosis, hematogenous osteomyelitis, the sequelae of poliomyelitis). While in the upper limb a discrepancy of 2–3 cm usually does not impair function and is only a cosmetic concern, in the lower limb, as Dzakhov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> noted, the same discrepancy forces the patient to lift the heel of the shorter limb or tilt the pelvis, leading to progressive compensatory scoliosis and to wear of the hip of the longer limb. During active growth the discrepancy increases, so equalization must be planned in good time.

Dedov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> systematized the management of length inequality into two principal groups: (1) methods acting on the growth rate of the longer limb (arrest, epiphysiodesis) or of the shorter limb (stimulation, distraction epiphysiolysis); and (2) surgical reconstructive measures — operative shortening of the longer limb or operative (single-stage or gradual) lengthening of the shorter limb. The choice of method is determined by etiology, magnitude of the discrepancy, remaining growth potential, and patient age; at a discrepancy below 2 cm at the end of growth intervention is usually unjustified, at 2–5 cm selective epiphysiodesis or shortening suffice, and beyond 5 cm lengthening of the shorter limb becomes the preferred approach. Prediction of the final discrepancy is performed with the classical methods of Anderson–Green–Messner (nomograms of growth of the distal femur and proximal tibia), Moseley (straight-line graph), and Paley (multiplier method) — the last being especially applicable in congenital malformations with proportional growth of the discrepancy.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Growth Stimulation and Distraction Epiphysiolysis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Methods of growth stimulation at the physeal zone — biological stimulation by Zatsepin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (S. T. Zatsepin), periosteal stripping (decortication), various metaphyseal osteotomies, interruption of the nutrient arteries in the medullary canal, blockade of the canal with bone grafts or metal pins, and regional sympathectomies — arise from the observation that local hyperemia and impaired venous outflow accelerate endochondral growth. In modern practice these classical methods are rarely applied because of the unpredictable and limited effect (typically 0.5–1.5 cm additional growth), but they retain historical and conceptual significance and remain in the curriculum of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school.

Distraction epiphysiolysis, originally proposed in experimental form by Dzakhov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> and colleagues (1950s), is based on the principle that reducing pressure on the physeal zone accelerates growth (an application of the Hueter–Volkmann principle in reverse). The force applied (about 16 kp in their original experiment) was not sufficient for a clinical result, and the method remained experimental until its revival by Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (G. A. Ilizarov, Kurgan, USSR). Ilizarov and his school developed the technique: with transverse Kirschner wires through the epiphysis and metaphysis, connected to a four-ring frame, a gradual and controlled distraction of about 1 mm/24 h in four stages per day is applied, producing between the epiphysis and metaphysis a characteristic diastasis that fills with newly formed bone (regenerate). Lengthening continues until limb equalization or to a tolerable biological limit; the frame remains in place on average 60 days after distraction stops to allow consolidation and shaping of the regenerate. Radiographically a homogeneous shadow of new bone appears by the end of the third week.

The method is applicable only when the physis is open and therefore at relatively young age. Ilizarov maintained that the younger the child, the better tolerance for distraction; other authors warned that accelerated physeal growth depletes the reserve of the physis and leads to earlier closure of the elongated epiphysis — hence lengthening should be performed not before 10–12 yr of age, close to the end of active growth. Currently distraction epiphysiolysis has been replaced in most indications by guided growth with tension-band plates (see below) because of the substantially lower morbidity and the smaller risk of iatrogenic physeal blockade.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Ilizarov distraction epiphysiolysis requires an open physis and provides lengthening through a regenerate that gradually fills the diastasis. It is applied between 10 and 14 years of age — before this age the growth potential may be iatrogenically depleted, beyond it the physis is closed and the method does not work. The distraction rate of 1 mm/24 h in 4 stages is universal both in classical Ilizarov and in modern systems.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Epiphysiodesis — Growth Arrest</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Operative arrest of the growth of the longer limb (epiphysiodesis) is the principal method for 2–5 cm length inequality in patients with sufficient remaining growth. Phemister <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (D. B. Phemister, 1933) gave the first detailed description of the classical technique: through a lateral and a medial incision over the distal femur or proximal tibia, rectangular or cylindrical bone autografts crossing the physis perpendicular are excised, opened with an osteotome through cortical windows, in which the grafts are rotated 180° before reimplantation — the reversed orientation interrupts physeal trophism and creates a local bony bridge. The White and Stubbins <sup class="natflag">US</sup> modifications use grafts with a square base, and the Bowen <sup class="natflag">US</sup> modification uses round grafts rotated 90°. Phemister epiphysiodesis is reliably permanent, but accurate dosing of the final effect requires strict preoperative planning by the Moseley diagram or Paley multiplier method.

Bowen epiphysiodesis <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (Bowen, 1985) simplifies the technique with a percutaneous procedure using a high-speed drill: through a small skin incision the drill perforates the physis in several directions and the physeal cartilage is curetted with a small instrument; local destruction of the proliferative zone and rapid bony bridging are achieved. The percutaneous epiphysiodesis subsequently developed by Métaizeau and further refined by Canale remains the modern standard for permanent epiphysiodesis in selected cases.

Between permanent epiphysiodesis and observation stands the concept of temporary growth restraint, drawing on the experimental work of Müller, who showed that sustained compression in the physeal area disrupts the normal architecture of the cartilage and reversibly slows growth. Haas (1922) proposed transverse metal wires across the physis; Blount <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (W. P. Blount, 1949) developed this idea with so-called "staples" — paired U-shaped clips placed percutaneously or through a small incision across the physis on both sides of the limb, bridging the epiphysis and metaphysis. The Blount system allowed theoretically reversible epiphysiodesis by removal of the staples; in practice, because of risks of dislocation, inflammation, and unpredictable rebound, Blount staples are rarely used today.

Plate removal restores normal growth within weeks. The method has nearly displaced classical Phemister and Bowen epiphysiodesis for indications of 2–4 cm inequality with concurrent angular deformity.

  <strong>Pearl</strong>

Blount staples are historical.</li>

<details>
<summary>Operative Shortening of the Longer Limb</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

When the patient has passed the age of active growth, or the discrepancy is substantial (4–5 cm and more), epiphysiodesis is not applicable and one proceeds to operative shortening of the longer limb. The first classical techniques included excision of a cylindrical bone segment with a transverse or step-cut osteotomy and restoration of the axis by lateral displacement of the fragments; subsequently the technique was standardized to segmental shortening by the principles of long-bone osteotomies. Times to consolidation are the same as for standard osteotomies (12–16 weeks for the femoral diaphysis). A classical limitation is that shortening produces muscular slack and loss of tone — hence the recommendation not to shorten more than 5–7 cm per segment or 8 cm in total, to avoid permanent muscular insufficiency (especially of quadriceps femoris in femoral shortening).

The modern standard for femoral shortening is the closed intramedullary technique of Winquist (1978–1986): through an antegradely introduced nail with a saw-blade reamer, a double osteotomy is made 5–7 cm below the lesser trochanter and at the desired distance distally; the central cylindrical segment is removed through the nail entry portal; the distal fragment is pulled proximally to an end-to-end joining and statically locked with transverse screws. The technique is fully closed, provides reliable union, and permits full weight bearing early; its main limitation is technical complexity and the need for specialized instrumentation. For tibial shortening (with a rigid tibia and a proximal diaphyseal osteotomy) segmental resection with intramedullary nail or plate fixation is applied. For combined deformity (shortening + angular correction) at the supracondylar level a wedge osteotomy or cylindrical osteotomy with an interposition plate is performed.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Operative Lengthening of the Shorter Limb</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

When the age for distraction epiphysiolysis has passed or there are contraindications (closed physis, infection of the epiphysis), lengthening is performed in the diaphyseal or metaphyseal area of the affected bone. We distinguish in principle single-stage (acute) and gradual lengthening. Single-stage lengthening, developed by Putti, Magnuson, Merle d'Aubigné, Coucholx, Pol le Coeur, and in the Bulgarian school by Iliev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (the Iliev apparatuses for single-stage lengthening illustrated in Boychev), allows an immediate gain of up to 4–5 cm by resection, displacement, and compensation with a bone graft. The disadvantages are substantial load on the neurovascular bundle with risk of ischemia and neural traction, the limited magnitude of lengthening, and the heavy postoperative immobilization. For these reasons, in modern practice single-stage lengthening has been almost entirely replaced by gradual lengthening.

Gradual lengthening — based on the principle of distraction osteogenesis (callotasis) — provides plastic elongation of the vessels, nerves, and the tendon–muscle apparatus and allows substantially larger lengthening. The classical technique of Wagner <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> (H. Wagner, 1971) uses a uniaxial threaded distractor after a diaphyseal osteotomy; lengthening at 1.5 mm/24 h continues to target, after which a second stage is performed for bone grafting and plate fixation of the defect. The technique has been displaced by the Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> method (G. A. Ilizarov, Kurgan): a circular external fixator with transverse Kirschner wires, corticotomy sparing the medullary blood supply and periosteum, a 5–7 day latency before starting distraction, and gradual lengthening at 1 mm/24 h in 4 stages. The regenerate consolidates in the apparatus for a period equal to about 2–3 times the duration of the distraction phase (the "consolidation index"). Ilizarov demonstrated the biological law of tension-driven stimulation of osteogenesis, and to this day remains the gold standard for complex lengthening, especially when simultaneous correction of angular deformity, rotation, and translation is required (so-called six-axis correction, implemented in modern hexapod systems Taylor Spatial Frame, TrueLok Hex).

The classical Bulgarian school describes a variant of gradual lengthening by Iliev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> with heavy Steinmann pins providing greater axial stability, and the technique of Vasserstein <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> with an intermediate bone graft: during distraction a cortico-cancellous segment is inserted between the fragments, which after reaching the required length is compressed for bony union; compensates for the shortening due to osteolytic processes around the graft. Bliskunov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (A. I. Bliskunov) developed an internal (intramedullary) distractor activated by an external magnet — the conceptual precursor of the modern internal lengthening nails. Albizzia (Guichet <sup class="natflag">FR</sup>, 1991) and the modern PRECICE and STRYDE systems (non-coupling magnet-driven intramedullary distractor) implement the same idea with an external electromagnetic drive and inductive transmission, allowing fully internal lengthening without external pins and without the associated risk of pin-track infection.

The general view is that bones tolerate lengthening up to about 10% (sometimes 15%) of the initial length without delay of reparative processes; beyond this limit the risk of impaired consolidation, neurological events, and regenerate fracture rises progressively. The experiments of Karatiga show that lengthening up to 10% does not disturb the physeal cartilage, while lengthening beyond 15% causes earlier closure of the physis (mainly the distal one). Contraindications are fixed contractures, trophic disturbances, active infection, osteomyelitis, and osteoarthritis; muscular paralysis is not an absolute contraindication if the limb is functional at least for static balance.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Lengthening beyond 10–15% of the initial length of the segment carries progressively rising risk of delayed consolidation, regenerate fracture, neural traction (especially the peroneal nerve in tibial lengthening), and contractures. The classical recommendation from the Bulgarian and Soviet schools — staged planning, simultaneous lengthening of both segments rather than one to a larger magnitude, monitoring of circulation and sensation at every stage.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<summary>Volkov–Oganesyan Hinged Elbow Distractor</summary>

The Volkov–Oganesyan elbow distractor <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (M. V. Volkov, O. V. Oganesyan) — a combined hinged external-fixator frame providing simultaneous distraction, early mobilization, and controlled restoration of joint congruity — is the historical precursor of modern hinged elbow external fixators for complex flexion–extension contractures and for recovery after severe elbow fracture–dislocations (especially complex patterns in adolescence). The concept combines joint distraction (unloading the articular cartilage and facilitating remodeling) with passive and active mobilization across the axes of the joint, and survives in modern practice as the Compass or Mayo external fixators.

The classical Sofield–Millar technique <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (H. A. Sofield, E. A. <details>
<summary>Hematogenous Osteomyelitis — Pediatric Decompression Principles</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In the treatment of pediatric hematogenous osteomyelitis the Bulgarian wartime and postwar school championed the principle of early urgent decompression: a burr hole in the metaphysis with evacuation of the intramedullary pus, copious lavage, local antibiotic application, and subsequent systemic antibiotic therapy by sensitivity, with the aim of preventing spread to the joint (septic arthritis) and to the physis (post-infectious partial or total growth arrest). The classical technique of "soft" (without regard to the physis) drilling has been replaced by the modern, physis-sparing decompression with image-based navigation. Chronic osteomyelitis with a sequestrum requires sequestrectomy with bone-substitute osteosynthesis or the Masquelet induced membrane — the latter, although modern, implements the principle of two-stage (debridement → reconstruction) treatment laid down in the classical Soviet school.

## Outcomes and Complications
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The complications of pediatric fractures fall into four categories that differ from those in adults: growth-related (specifically pediatric), healing-related (general but with lower incidence), functional (with lower incidence of joint stiffness), and systemic.

**Growth-related complications.** Growth arrest after a physeal injury is specific to pediatric trauma; realized limb-length discrepancy, progressive angular deformity, or a combination of the two. Incidence by Salter–Harris type is discussed above. Active assessment with serial radiographs, "Harris growth-arrest lines," and on indication MRI/CT for evaluation of the size of an established bridge. The clinical manifestation appears 6–24 months after the injury and demands active follow-up.

**Malalignment at rest.** Residual angular or rotational deformity after consolidation — remodeling corrects a substantial part of the angular deformity in the plane of joint motion, but rotational malalignment does not remodel. Residual translation (mediolateral or anteroposterior) usually remodels with sufficient growth potential.

**Avascular necrosis.** Particularly after physeal injuries of the proximal femur, the lateral condyle of the humerus, and the proximal radius. Progressive, manifests 6–24 months after injury.

**Chronic pain and stiffness.** Less common than in adults because of the better regenerative properties of pediatric tissue; joint stiffness often resolves spontaneously with active mobilization. Immobilization beyond 6 weeks for a pediatric fracture must be specifically justified — the risks of muscle atrophy, psychosocial dis-adaptation, and school absence outweigh the usual benefits of prolonged immobilization.

**Compartment syndrome.** In pediatric fractures it is often masked, with the "pediatric 3 A's" — Anxiety, Agitation, increasing Analgesic requirement — replacing the classical "5 P's" triad. Progression of pain despite adequate analgesia is an early and often the only signal. On suspicion — urgent compartment-pressure measurement and fasciotomy.

**Psychosocial complications.** Especially significant in repeated fractures (OI, pathological cysts) or in prolonged immobilization (school absence, social isolation). One of the reasons for the modern shift toward operative treatment of selected patterns — return to full activity in days to weeks instead of months.

## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the orthopaedic school in Bulgaria; the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curriculum reference and contains detailed descriptions of the classical Bulgarian school of limb-length equalization, of management of pediatric fracture pathology, and of reconstructive procedures for the sequelae of poliomyelitis, rickets, and infectious spondylitis.

**Iliev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian variant of single-stage limb lengthening with the "Iliev apparatuses" described in Boychev — resection, displacement, and bone-graft compensation for an immediate gain of length up to 4–5 cm. Historical significance; largely replaced by gradual Ilizarov lengthening and the modern internal magnetic distractors.

**Vasserstein <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Gradual lengthening with an intermediate bone graft — a cortico-cancellous segment is inserted between the fragments and, after reaching the required length, is compressed for bony union; compensates for the shortening due to osteolytic processes around the graft.

**Karchinov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1974/1975).** Two-chamber bone bridges for large defects in congenital tibial pseudarthrosis — a Bulgarian classical modification of tibial reconstruction in NF1-associated pseudarthrosis.

**Boychev Hospital, Gorna Banya <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** The classical orthopaedic teams at the Boychev Hospital developed the technique of pedicled cortico-periosteal fibular transfer (a modification of the Hahn–Huntington–Codivilla method) for severe segmental defects of the tibia in childhood.

From the classical Soviet school, deeply integrated into the Bulgarian curriculum:

**S. T. Zatsepin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Biological stimulation of growth at the physeal zones through periosteal stripping (decortication), various metaphyseal osteotomies, and interruption of the nutrient arteries in the medullary canal. Historical significance; the effect is limited and unpredictable (0.5–1.5 cm additional growth), but the method remains in the curriculum.

**Dzakhov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Classical systematization of the problem of limb-length inequality in childhood; introduced distraction epiphysiolysis in experiment (1950s, approximately 16 kp force) — the conceptual precursor of the Ilizarov system.

**Dedov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Systematization of the methods of limb-length equalization into two principal groups (action on growth rate; reconstructive measures); a graphical approach to planning the final correction, paralleling Anderson–Green–Messner and Moseley.

**G. A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1921–1992).** Creator of the principle of distraction osteogenesis (callotasis); circular external fixator with transverse Kirschner wires, corticotomy sparing the medullary blood supply, a 5–7 day latency, and gradual distraction at 1 mm/24 h in 4 stages. Distraction epiphysiolysis at an open physis (10–14 yr); segmental lengthening in the diaphyseal or metaphyseal area at a closed physis; bone transport for defects. The conceptual foundation of modern hexapod systems (Taylor Spatial Frame, TrueLok Hex) and of intramedullary magnetic distractors (PRECICE, STRYDE).

**A. I. Bliskunov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Internal (intramedullary) distractor for limb lengthening activated by an external magnet — conceptual precursor of modern internal magnet-driven systems (Albizzia, PRECICE, STRYDE).

**M. V. Volkov, O. V. Oganesyan <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Hinged elbow distractor — a combined hinged external-fixator frame providing simultaneous distraction, early mobilization, and controlled restoration of joint congruity. The historical precursor of modern hinged elbow external fixators (Compass, Mayo).

From the North American and Western European orthopaedic traditions, the constructive basis of the entire concept of growth management:

**Robert B. Salter <sup class="natflag">CA</sup> and W. Robert Harris <sup class="natflag">CA</sup> (1963).** The classification of physeal fractures (Toronto) — five main types by the relationship of the fracture line to the physis, with standard prognostic and operative implications. Salter (Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto) is also the author of the innominate osteotomy for developmental hip dysplasia.

**D. B. Phemister <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1933).** The first detailed description of classical epiphysiodesis — excision, 180° rotation, and reimplantation of rectangular or cylindrical physeal blocks through a lateral and a medial incision over the distal femur or proximal tibia for reliable permanent growth arrest.

**White and Stubbins <sup class="natflag">US</sup>.** Modification of Phemister epiphysiodesis with square-based grafts — facilitates rotation and reimplantation.

**S. P. Bowen <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1985).** Percutaneous epiphysiodesis with a high-speed drill through a small skin incision — the modern standard for permanent epiphysiodesis in pure length inequality, further developed by Métaizeau and Canale.

**W. P. Blount <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1949).** Metal U-shaped "staples" for theoretically reversible growth restraint — placed percutaneously or through a small incision across the physis on both sides of the limb. The conceptual precursor of the modern tension-band plate; in modern practice replaced by the 8-plate.

**P. M. Stevens <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (2007).** The "8-plate" tension-band plate — a small figure-of-eight plate with one screw in the epiphysis and one in the metaphysis, placed extra-periosteally. Reversible, guidable, implements the Hueter–Volkmann principle for guided growth. The modern standard for graduated growth restraint at 2–4 cm inequality and/or concurrent angular deformity.

**H. A. Sofield and E. A. Millar <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1959).** The "shish-kebab" technique — multiple segmental osteotomy of a long bone with axial threading of the fragments onto an intramedullary rod — the standard for stabilization of long bones in severe osteogenesis imperfecta.

**Bailey–Dubow <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1963)** and **Fassier–Duval <sup class="natflag">CA</sup> (2000s).** Telescoping rods for osteogenesis imperfecta that lengthen with the child's growth and do not require replacement — the modern standard for treatment of severe OI.

**Wagner <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> (1971).** Uniaxial threaded distractor for two-stage lengthening (distraction + second stage with bone grafting and plate fixation) — the historical precursor of modern external lengthening fixators.

**Hueter–Volkmann principle <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>.** The experimental rule of the two German authors at the end of the 19th century: compression perpendicular to the physis slows local growth, off-loading accelerates it. The conceptual foundation of the entire guided-growth concept and of distraction epiphysiolysis.

**H. Langenskiöld <sup class="natflag">FI</sup> (1967).** Resection of the physeal bony bridge with fat or silicone interposition — the standard for treatment of established localized growth arrest at bridges under 50% of the physeal area with at least 2 years of remaining growth potential.

**J. Métaizeau <sup class="natflag">FR</sup>.** Flexible intramedullary nailing with titanium elastic nails (TENS) — the principal operative technique for pediatric diaphyseal long-bone fractures. Further refined (with Canale, US) for percutaneous epiphysiodesis.

  /* V4 anatomy-12: open the target <details> when the page loads with a hash
     or when the reader clicks an in-page anchor. Native <details> isn't
     auto-expanded by :target alone, so we wire a tiny progressive enhancement. */
  (function () {
    function openByHash(hash) {
      if (!hash) return;
      var id = hash.replace(/^#/, "");
      if (!id) return;
      var el = document.getElementById(id);
      if (!el) return;
      if (el.tagName === "DETAILS") el.open = true;
      var inner = el.querySelector(":scope > details");
      if (inner) inner.open = true;
      var node = el.parentElement;
      while (node && node !== document.body) {
        if (node.tagName === "DETAILS") node.open = true;
        node = node.parentElement;
      }
      requestAnimationFrame(function () {
        el.scrollIntoView({ behavior: "smooth", block: "start" });
      });
    }
    function wire() {
      openByHash(window.location.hash);
      document.addEventListener("click", function (e) {
        var t = e.target;
        while (t && t !== document) {
          if (t.tagName === "A" && t.getAttribute && t.getAttribute("href") && t.getAttribute("href").charAt(0) === "#") {
            openByHash(t.getAttribute("href"));
            break;
          }
          t = t.parentNode;
        }
      });
      window.addEventListener("hashchange", function () { openByHash(window.location.hash); });
    }
    if (document.readyState === "loading") {
      document.addEventListener("DOMContentLoaded", wire);
    } else {
      wire();
    }
  })();
