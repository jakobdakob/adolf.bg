## Overview
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">


The shoulder girdle offers the greatest arc of motion of any joint in the body — a compromise reached through a radical sacrifice of bony stability in favour of soft-tissue balance. The glenohumeral articular surface of the humerus is roughly three times the area of the glenoid, the contact patch at any instant covers only 25–30% of the head, and only the coordinated action of the rotator cuff, the labrum, the capsule, and the periscapular muscles allows stability and mobility to coexist.

This chapter follows that logic: regional anatomy and the biomechanics that flow from it, the clinical examination and imaging that drive approach selection, the classifications that determine operative tactics, and the full register of approaches and procedures — from the workhorse deltopectoral to Hadzhistamov's trans-spinal osteotomy, from Bankart–Putti-Platt to Boychev, and from the Neer prosthesis to the full spectrum of arthrodesis techniques. The Bulgarian contributions — the topographic concept of the clavipectoral / pectoral / subpectoral triangles, the Boychev procedure, the Holevich–Paneva–Popova reconstruction, the Balchev, Moskov, Imamaliev and Portugalov techniques — remain the didactic backbone of the Bulgarian orthopaedic curriculum.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Shoulder surgery is navigation around three neurovascular limits: the <code>axillary nerve</code> 5–7 cm distal to the acromion on the deep surface of the deltoid, the <code>musculocutaneous nerve</code> 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid in coracobrachialis, and the neurovascular bundle in the pectoral triangle 2 cm anterior to subscapularis.</li>
    <li>Every approach is plotted around these three limits — the 5 cm limit distal to the acromion in deltoid-splitting incisions is a direct consequence of the axillary nerve wrapping the surgical neck.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</section>

### Bones and Joints
<section class="topic-subsection" id="bones-joints">

The glenohumeral joint is a ball-and-socket articulation between the relatively small, slightly retroverted glenoid (retroversion 5–7°, inferior tilt 4–5°) and the substantially larger humeral head, covered by approximately 120° of articular cartilage. The size mismatch produces the characteristic mobility–stability trade-off — only 25–30% of the head is in contact with the glenoid at any instant. The glenoid labrum is a fibrocartilaginous rim that deepens the socket by approximately 50% and provides attachment for the capsular ligaments and the long head of the biceps tendon.

The acromion forms the lateral roof of the joint; its morphological variants — flat (Bigliani I), curved (II) and hooked (III) — carry direct surgical significance, especially type III, associated with rotator cuff impingement. *Os acromiale* (1–8% of the population, mesoacromion most common) is a failure of fusion of the acromial ossification centres and a recognised substrate for impingement. The coracoid projects anteriorly and provides attachment for the conjoint tendon (short head of biceps and *m. coracobrachialis*), the coracoacromial ligament, the coracoclavicular ligaments (conoid and trapezoid) and the coracohumeral ligament; the coracoid is the bone-graft source for the Latarjet procedure. The clavicle completes the girdle, articulating with the acromion laterally and the sternum medially.

### Capsule and Ligaments
<section class="topic-subsection" id="capsule-ligaments">
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-2/shoulder-capsule.png" data-figure-alt="Capsule of the glenohumeral joint" aria-label="Enlarge">

    <span class="md-figure-caption">Capsule of the glenohumeral joint (distended for demonstration), anterior view.     <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray327.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>
  </figcaption>

The capsule of the glenohumeral joint is generous — the synovial volume is roughly twice that required for bony contact. This explains both its capacity for an enormous arc of motion and its dependence on three fibrous condensations: the middle glenohumeral ligament (MGHL) — primary anterior restraint in mid-abduction with external rotation; the inferior glenohumeral ligament complex (IGHL), with its anterior and posterior bands — primary restraint at full abduction; and the superior (SGHL) — stabiliser of the rotator interval. The rotator interval, a triangular field between the supraspinatus tendon and the superior border of subscapularis, contains the coracohumeral and superior glenohumeral ligaments and is the route for arthroscopic establishment of the anterior portal.

</section>


### Infraclavicular and Axillary Topography
<section class="topic-subsection" id="infraclavicular-topography">
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian anatomical school describes the infraclavicular region (regio infraclavicularis), bounded by the inferior clavicular border, the lateral edge of the sternum, the third rib, and the anterior border of the deltoid, as the key topographic zone for anterior shoulder approaches. The skin is thin and mobile; the subcutaneous tissue carries branches of the anterior supraclavicular nerves and sensory rami of the intercostal nerves. Beneath the superficial fascia lies *pectoralis major* with its own fascia; between it and the deltoid forms the deltopectoral sulcus, expanding proximally into the deltopectoral triangle — a fatty-lymphatic space in which the cephalic vein drains into the axillary vein at the level of the superior border of *pectoralis minor*.

The infraclavicular space is divided into three triangles. The *clavipectoral triangle* lies between the clavicle and the upper border of pectoralis minor; the clavipectoral fascia here is pierced by branches of *a. thoracoacromialis* (pectoral, deltoid, acromial, and clavicular rami), which must be identified and controlled in extended deltopectoral exposures. The *pectoral triangle* lies behind pectoralis minor; here the entire neurovascular bundle of the upper limb runs, about 2 cm anterior to subscapularis. The *subpectoral triangle* lies below the inferior border of pectoralis minor.

In the pectoral triangle the musculocutaneous nerve leaves the lateral cord at the level of the inferior border of pectoralis minor and adheres to the axillary artery before entering coracobrachialis; the median nerve forms from its two roots 3–5 cm below the inferior border of pectoralis minor; the ulnar nerve separates 3.5–4 cm above the latissimus dorsi tendon and runs on the inner side of the artery. This arrangement is critical in extended anterior exposures to the humeral neck.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The axillary neurovascular bundle lies approximately 2 cm anterior to subscapularis within the pectoral triangle.</li>
    <li>Dissection medial to the short head of biceps beneath the coracoid directly risks the axillary artery, median nerve and ulnar nerve — especially in an extended deltopectoral approach with coracoid osteotomy.</li>
    <li>The three triangles (clavipectoral, pectoral, subpectoral) define the logic of safe dissection: the thoracoacromial branches in the clavipectoral triangle are identified and ligated before work reaches the pectoral triangle.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>

**Rotator cuff.** Four muscles forming a cuff around the humeral head and functioning principally as dynamic compressors of the head into the glenoid. *Supraspinatus* (supraspinous fossa → superior facet of greater tuberosity) carries the initial abduction to 30° and works with the deltoid through the full arc; the most frequently torn cuff muscle. *Infraspinatus* (infraspinous fossa → middle facet) — main external rotator. *Teres minor* (lateral border of the scapula → inferior facet) — assists external rotation. *Subscapularis* (subscapular fossa → lesser tuberosity) — main internal rotator and anterior dynamic stabiliser.

**Deltoid.** *Musculus deltoideus* is the principal abductor beyond the initial supraspinatus range, with three heads: anterior (clavicular) — flexion and internal rotation; middle (acromial) — abduction; posterior (spinal) — extension and external rotation. It inserts on the deltoid tuberosity. Innervation by the axillary nerve, wrapping the surgical neck 5–7 cm distal to the acromion on the deep surface of the muscle, limits every deltoid-splitting incision.

**Periscapular muscles.** Trapezius (occiput and spinous processes → scapular spine, acromion, clavicle) — elevation, retraction, and upward rotation of the scapula. Rhomboid major and minor — retraction and downward rotation. Serratus anterior (ribs → medial border of the scapula anteriorly) — protraction and upward rotation; scapular winging from injury to the long thoracic nerve. Levator scapulae — elevation. Latissimus dorsi and teres major — internal rotators and adductors, together forming the posterior axillary fold.

**The conjoint bundle.** Coracobrachialis with the short head of biceps detaches from the coracoid and forms the working landmark in anterior exposures; medial retraction of this bundle uncovers subscapularis.

### Nerves
<section class="topic-subsection" id="nerves">
<div class="ts-body">

**Axillary nerve (C5–C6).** Branches from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus, passes posteriorly through the quadrilateral space, and wraps the surgical neck of the humerus deep to the deltoid; branches to deltoid and teres minor; its sensory branch (*n. cutaneus brachii lateralis superior*) supplies the skin over the lateral deltoid.

**Musculocutaneous nerve (C5–C7).** Branches from the lateral cord, pierces *coracobrachialis* 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid, and supplies biceps brachii, brachialis, and coracobrachialis before continuing as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve.

**Suprascapular nerve (C5–C6).** Passes through the suprascapular notch (beneath the superior transverse scapular ligament), supplies supraspinatus, continues round the spinoglenoid notch, and supplies infraspinatus. Compression or traction at either site produces neuropathy with weakness of supraspinatus and/or infraspinatus — an additional risk in deep posterior glenoid dissection and in wide scapular approaches.

**Long thoracic nerve (C5–C7).** Runs along the lateral chest wall on the surface of *serratus anterior*; injury produces scapular winging with medial-border prominence on forward elevation.

**Brachial plexus.** Roots C5–T1, trunks (superior, middle, inferior), divisions (anterior and posterior), cords (lateral, medial, posterior) and terminal branches; lies medial to the coracoid and behind *pectoralis minor*, together with the axillary vessels.

  <strong>Critical</strong>

### Vessels
<section class="topic-subsection" id="vessels">
<div class="ts-body">

The axillary artery and vein cross the axilla as continuations of the subclavian vessels below the clavicle and become the brachial vessels distal to the inferior border of teres major. Of the six branches of the axillary artery, three carry direct surgical significance: *a. thoracoacromialis* (with pectoral, deltoid, acromial, and clavicular rami piercing the clavipectoral fascia in the clavipectoral triangle); the *anterior humeral circumflex artery* — one of the "three sisters" along the inferior border of subscapularis, requiring ligation prior to detachment; and the *posterior humeral circumflex artery*, which accompanies the axillary nerve through the quadrilateral space and anastomoses with its anterior counterpart. The humeral head is supplied principally by the ascending branch of the anterior humeral circumflex (Laing's arcuate artery); its disruption explains the high rate of AVN in displaced surgical-neck fractures.

</div>
</section>

## Pathophysiology and Mechanism
<section class="topic-section" id="pathophysiology">


**Biomechanics of stability.** The glenohumeral joint exists in a continuous dynamic equilibrium between four stabilising mechanisms. The *static* set includes articular congruity (minimal because of the caput/glenoid mismatch), cohesive-adhesive forces within the synovial space, negative intra-capsular pressure at rest, and the capsuloligamentous complex (MGHL for mid-abduction in external rotation, IGHL for full abduction). The *dynamic* set — the principal stabilising mechanism during motion — relies on the concavity-compression principle: the rotator cuff compresses the head into the glenoid perpendicular to the articular surface while the deltoid generates the tangential force for motion; effectiveness depends on preserved glenoid depth (labrum!) and an intact cuff.

**Scapulohumeral rhythm.** Full 180° abduction is the product of ~120° glenohumeral motion plus ~60° scapulothoracic rotation (a 2:1 ratio after the first 30° of pure glenohumeral motion). The periscapular muscles (upper and lower trapezius, serratus anterior) are the "forgotten muscles" — scapular winging, serratus dysfunction or AC pain immediately disrupt the rhythm and produce pseudo-impingement.

**Pathomechanical themes that operative surgery addresses.** *(1)* Anterior instability — traumatic first dislocation in patients under 25 carries a high recurrence rate because of a Bankart-equivalent substrate (detached labrum + capsular plastic deformation) ± Hill-Sachs impression and/or bony defect of the antero-inferior glenoid rim; the basis for capsulolabral reconstruction (Bankart, Boychev, Putti-Platt) or bony augmentation (Latarjet, Eden, Lange, Oudar, Judet). *(2)* Impingement and cuff degeneration — between the greater tuberosity and the coracoacromial arch, exacerbated by Bigliani III, os acromiale and calcifications; the basis for acromioplasty, bursectomy and Debeyre-style wide exposure for retracted tears. *(3)* AC separation — sequential disruption of the AC capsule, then conoid and trapezoid ligaments in a high-energy adduction injury; Rockwood I–VI dictates operative choice. *(4)* Brachial plexus traction — Erb-Duchenne in birth or in high-energy adult shoulder dislocation. *(5)* Thoracic outlet involvement of the brachial bundle — TOS from a cervical rib, a congenitally short clavicle (Sprengel + Ombredanne), or after over-correction.

**Avascular necrosis.** The ascending branch of the anterior humeral circumflex (arcuate artery) is the end-vessel for the humeral head; its disruption in a displaced surgical-neck fracture (especially the 4-part Neer pattern) precisely cuts off the blood supply and explains the high rate of AVN — one of the indications for hemiarthroplasty or reverse arthroplasty in the elderly.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Concavity-compression means that pathology which removes or flattens the glenoid concavity (Bankart, bony glenoid loss) disrupts the very mechanism of stability, not merely the passive static capsule.</li>
    <li>This explains why pure capsulorrhaphies (Putti-Platt, Magnuson-Stack) show higher recurrence rates with bone loss — they restore passive, not active, stability.</li>
    <li>The implication: operative tactics must restore the concavity (Bankart repair, Latarjet, bone block), not merely tighten the capsule.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis
<section class="topic-section" id="clinical-diagnosis">


**The surgically relevant clinical examination** is organised around the structure the approach will engage.

*Deltoid and axillary nerve*: sensory testing over the lateral deltoid and a test of isometric abduction after every anterior dislocation and proximal humerus fracture. *Rotator cuff*: Jobe (empty can) for supraspinatus; external rotation in neutral and the Hornblower sign for infraspinatus / teres minor; lift-off (Gerber) and belly-press for subscapularis; Hawkins-Kennedy and Neer for impingement. *Labrum and instability*: apprehension in abduction–external rotation plus relocation (Jobe), load-and-shift in neutral, sulcus sign for inferior laxity, O'Brien and dynamic labral shear for SLAP pathology. *AC joint*: tenderness on palpation, cross-body adduction, O'Brien with stabilised clavicle. *SC joint*: palpation, anterior or posterior step on supination. *Brachial plexus*: motor-sensory C5–T1 screen, Tinel and compression tests, Roos and Adson for TOS.

**Imaging that drives approach selection.** A standard AP in the scapular plane plus a transaxillary or Velpeau-axillary view excludes posterior dislocation (a 50% miss rate is reported on standard AP alone). The *true AP* and *outlet (Y)* views show Bigliani acromial morphology and define acromioplasty indications. The *Zanca view* (10–15° cephalic tilt) shows the AC joint without the overlying humerus. The *Stryker notch view* demonstrates Hill-Sachs impressions. The *Garth view* (45° apical oblique) shows the antero-inferior glenoid rim in bone-defect cases. CT with 3D reconstruction is the standard for glenoid bone loss (precise Latarjet vs. Bankart planning), for surgical-neck fracture planning, and for identification of osteolytic tumours. MR-arthrography or high-field MRI shows labrum, cuff, biceps and capsule with high sensitivity. Ultrasound in trained hands is a dynamic and effective assessment of cuff tears and biceps long-head pathology.

## Classification
<section class="topic-section" id="classification">


**Bigliani — acromial morphology (outlet view).**

| Type  | Morphology  | Correlation                                          |
|-------|-------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| I     | Flat        | No clear link to impingement                         |
| II    | Curved      | Mild-to-moderate impingement                         |
| III   | Hooked      | Full-thickness supraspinatus tears                   |

**Os acromiale** (1–8% of the population): pre-acromion (tip segment), meso-acromion (middle; most common), meta-acromion (posterolateral), basi-acromion. The mesoacromion is the substrate for impingement and for failure of classical acromioplasty.

**Rockwood — acromioclavicular separation.**

| Type  | AC capsule  | CC ligaments  | Displacement              | Treatment        |
|-------|-------------|---------------|---------------------------|------------------|
| I     | sprain      | intact        | none                      | conservative     |
| II    | ruptured    | partial       | <25%                      | conservative     |
| III   | ruptured    | complete      | 25–100%                   | individualised   |
| IV    | ruptured    | complete      | posterior through trapezius | operative      |
| V     | ruptured    | complete      | 100–300% sup.–inf.        | operative        |
| VI    | ruptured    | complete      | inferior below coracoid   | operative        |

**Neer — proximal humerus fractures** (4-part anatomical principle): a fragment is defined by >1 cm displacement or >45° angulation; 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-part fractures, with the 4-part pattern carrying the highest risk of AVN.

**Capsulolabral lesions in anterior instability.** *Bankart* — avulsion of the capsulolabral complex from the bony glenoid rim; *bony Bankart* — with a bone fragment; *ALPSA* (Neviaser) — medial avulsion and rolling of the labrum-periosteum-capsule sleeve along the anterior scapular neck with pseudo-healing and chronic deficiency; *HAGL* — humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament; *GLAD* — glenolabral articular disruption (chondral avulsion with intact labrum); *Perthes* — a non-displaced Bankart with intact periosteum.

**Hill-Sachs (Itoi's concept).** "Glenoid track" — separates posterolateral impressions into *on-track* (engaging the glenoid track in the ABER position; safe for pure labral repair) and *off-track* (extending beyond the track in ABER; require remplissage or bony augmentation).

**Snyder — SLAP (superior labrum anterior to posterior).** I — frayed, degenerative; II — avulsion of the biceps-labral anchor; III — bucket-handle with intact biceps; IV — bucket-handle with the tear extending into the biceps. Types V–X (added later) combine SLAP with Bankart extension etc.

**Cavendish — Sprengel.** I very mild (invisible beneath clothing), II mild (visible asymmetry on motion), III moderate (visible anterior groove with ~2 cm elevation), IV severe (>5 cm elevation, restricted motion). The Hamner-Capen radiographic classification is an alternative.

**Jonasch — clavicular pseudarthrosis** (congenital and traumatic): ranked by defect length and location, guiding the choice between a tibial cortico-periosteal interposition (small), a fibular segment (medium), or an iliac-crest or rib graft (large).

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>An overlooked os acromiale at acromioplasty produces persistent pain and iatrogenic instability of the acromial fragment; outlet and axillary views are mandatory, with CT in doubt.</li>
    <li>Off-track Hill-Sachs requires remplissage or bony augmentation in addition to labral repair; a plain Bankart for an off-track substrate is almost certain to recur.</li>
    <li>The off-track criterion is especially important with bony glenoid loss &gt;25% (inverted-pear glenoid) — an indication for Latarjet.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</section>



## Treatment
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">



### Nonoperative
<section class="topic-subsection" id="nonoperative">
<div class="ts-body">

Velpeau-style immobilisation (3 weeks for first traumatic dislocations in patients under 25; individualised in older patients — a balance between recurrence and stiffness), physiotherapy with a focus on scapular setting and dynamic cuff stabilisation, infiltrations (subacromial for impingement, glenohumeral for adhesive capsulitis, AC for arthropathy). Hydrodilatation is a valid adjunct in adhesive capsulitis. Arthrodesis or arthroplasty when 6–12 months of systematic conservative treatment have failed.

</div>
</section>


### Operative
<section class="topic-subsection" id="operative">
<div class="ts-body">

<details>
<summary>Deltopectoral Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The workhorse for the anterior shoulder. *Indications:* open reduction, proximal humerus fixation, anterior glenoid procedures (Bankart, Latarjet), arthroplasty, tumour resection. *Position:* beach-chair at 45–60°. *Landmarks:* coracoid, cephalic vein in the deltopectoral groove. *Steps:* incision along the groove from the coracoid 10–15 cm distally; cephalic vein retracted laterally with the deltoid (vein preservation); clavipectoral fascia divided lateral to the conjoint tendon; conjoint bundle retracted medially; identification and ligation of the "three sisters" along the inferior subscapularis border; subscapularis detached (vertical tenotomy, lesser-tuberosity osteotomy, or peel). *Structures at risk:* musculocutaneous nerve (5–8 cm distal to coracoid), axillary artery in deep medial dissection, cephalic vein.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The "three sisters" (anterior humeral circumflex artery + venae comitantes) along the inferior subscapularis border must be ligated <em>before</em> subscapularis detachment — uncontrolled bleeding obscures the field and forces quick, imprecise moves near the axillary nerve.</li>
    <li>Subscapularis dysfunction after a classical vertical tenotomy — lesser-tuberosity osteotomy or peel with a bone block delivers better functional outcomes.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Posterior Shoulder Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Indications:* posterior glenoid procedures, posterior instability, posterior dislocations, scapular fixation. *Position:* lateral or prone. *Landmarks:* posterolateral corner of the acromion, lateral border of the deltoid. *Steps:* 6–10 cm incision from the posterolateral corner along the deltoid border; deltoid split between middle and posterior heads ≤5 cm distal to the acromion (axillary nerve!); infraspinatus / teres-minor interval developed; posterior capsule exposed. Alternative: infraspinatus fibre-split or detachment from the greater tuberosity with reinsertion. *Structures at risk:* axillary nerve in the quadrilateral space, suprascapular nerve at the spinoglenoid notch.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Anterior Axillary Approach (Henry)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

A cosmetic alternative to the deltopectoral. *Landmark:* anterior axillary fold below the coracoid; the dissection then turns up to the deltopectoral groove. *Advantage:* less visible scar. *Disadvantage:* more restricted proximal exposure.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Superior (Saber-Cut) Approach and Transacromial Variants</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Indications:* distal clavicle resection, AC procedures, open cuff repair, selected proximal humerus fractures. *Steps:* 4–6 cm incision over the AC joint or greater tuberosity; deltoid split ≤5 cm distal to the acromion; supraspinatus tendon and AC zone exposed. *Classical Bulgarian variants.* The *economical transacromial* — a partial acromial osteotomy along a pre-drawn line with a hole pre-drilled beneath the acromion to protect the cuff; osteosynthesis with wire or a small screw. The *saber-cut* — the extended form with full release of the acromion and the laterally directed clavicular portion lateral to the coracoclavicular ligament; AC complex osteosynthesised. Both give complete visualisation in massive retracted tears, in classical operations for calcified periarthritis, and in selected complex anterior dislocations with avulsion of the greater tuberosity.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>In a deltoid-splitting approach the separation must not exceed 3–4 cm from the muscle origin — branches of the axillary nerve to pars acromialis enter at the midpoint between the lateral acromial edge and the deltoid tuberosity.</li>
    <li>A split &gt;5 cm is acceptable only in a paralysed deltoid.</li>
    <li>The safest splitting routes are along the natural borders between pars clavicularis ↔ pars acromialis and pars acromialis ↔ pars spinalis.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Wide Scapular Approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*L-shaped* — vertical component along the vertebral border + horizontal component 2 cm above the superior angle; patient prone; the deep branch of the transverse cervical artery (0.5–1 cm medial to the border) is ligated; identification of the suprascapular notch (omohyoid as landmark) protects the suprascapular nerve. *T-shaped (the Hejian)* — combines the vertical incision with an oblique arm along the scapular spine to the AC joint; the widest field for subtotal scapulectomy and release of the lateral angle. *Posterior trans-spinal (Hadzhistamov)* — osteotomy across the middle of the scapular spine (without disrupting the AC joint); the bone fragment is hinged down like a page, exposing the upper cuff, the greater tuberosity facet, the long head of biceps, and the posterior capsule; osteosynthesis with wire or screw. A conceptual precursor of modern transacromial and "cobra" approaches.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Hadzhistamov's trans-spinal approach is the conceptual precursor of modern transacromial and "cobra" exposures: a scapular-spine osteotomy (without disrupting the AC joint) from a posterior skin incision delivers a wide superior view of the rotator cuff without disturbing deltoid function.</li>
    <li>Re-osteosynthesis of the osteotomised fragment with wire or screw is critical for postoperative stability.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Clavicular Approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Standard longitudinal, slightly curved incision, *not* directly over the bone (reduces scar tension at the muscular insertions); S-shaped incision for simultaneous supra- and infra-clavicular access. *Anatomical landmarks beneath the bone:* the external jugular vein lies 0.5–1 cm lateral to the clavicular head of sternocleidomastoid; the subclavian vessels beneath subclavius; the brachial plexus and subclavian vessels cross the clavicle posteriorly an average of 2 cm lateral to the SCM insertion; the suprascapular artery runs along the posterior surface from the midshaft to the acromial end. *SC-joint approach* — skin incision over the medial quarter, with the jugular notch and the sternal clavicular end as landmarks. *AC-joint approach* — curved incision over the lateral quarter plus the anterosuperior acromial border.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Arthroscopic Portals</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Posterior portal* — 2–3 cm inferior and 1–2 cm medial to the posterolateral corner of the acromion; the "soft spot" between infraspinatus and teres minor; the main viewing portal for the glenohumeral joint. *Anterior portal* — established under direct visualisation through the rotator interval; the main working portal. *Anterolateral* — inferior to the anterior acromial edge; for subacromial procedures and cuff repairs. *Posterolateral* — for subacromial procedures. *Lateral* — through the deltoid, 2–3 cm lateral to the acromial edge; for subacromial procedures. *Accessory portals* — the 5-o'clock portal, Neviaser portal, trans-cuff portal.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>AC and SC Instability Stabilisation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Over 30 techniques have been described for acute AC dislocations; the classical variants:

- *Bosworth* — vertical channel through the clavicle above the coracoid with a clavicle-to-coracoid screw + percutaneous K-wire across the AC (removed at 3–4 weeks).
- *AO (Weber)* — cancellous screw + Weber tension band; CC ligaments reconstructed with half the coracobrachialis tendon.
- *Heyzlauer* — transposition of the CC ligament to form a new AC ligament; avoids any rigid fixation that would limit clavicular rotation.
- *Dewar-Barrington* — osteotomy of the coracoid tip with the conjoint muscles and fixation to the clavicle by screw — dynamic depression of the clavicle through the pulling muscles; preserving 1 cm and pre-drilling are technical critical points.
- *Bunnell* — passive fascial stabilisation with a fascia-lata strip through bone tunnels using Deschamps' needle.
- *Rowe* — longitudinal incision + oblique distal-clavicle osteotomy + ligament repair in overlap.
- *Weaver-Dunn* — distal clavicle resection + transposition of the coracoacromial ligament (the modern historical standard).

In SC dislocation (anterior or posterior) — transverse incision; posterior dislocation may compress the trachea and great vessels and demand emergency treatment; preoperative tomography is essential (Kennedy 1949, Holmdahl 1954). *Lowman* — a fascial graft through two central tunnels. SC-end resection alone is not recommended; reconstruction of the interclavicular ligament and fixation of the intra-articular disc are critical.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>A percutaneous K-wire across the SC joint or into the manubrium: real risk of intrathoracic migration into the pericardium or great vessels (fatal cases reported).</li>
    <li>Bend the ends over the skin, remove at 3–4 weeks, and reconstruct the interclavicular ligament with a fascial strip.</li>
    <li>Posterior SC dislocation may compress the trachea and great vessels — an emergency; preoperative CT mandatory.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Correction of Congenital High-Riding Scapula (Sprengel)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Goals: excise the omovertebral link, lower the scapula, correct the functional and cosmetic deficit. Optimal age >10 years.

*Omovertebral excision* — patient prone; incision along the C1–T9 spinous processes; the rhomboids are divided; the bony bridge is resected.

*Excision of the supero-medial portion* — transverse incision along the scapular spine; excision of the supero-medial angle scaled to the prominence.

*Green* — incision along the vertebral border; trapezius is carefully detached from the scapular spine (preserved for reinsertion); supraspinatus and infraspinatus stripped strictly subperiosteally with attention to the suprascapular nerve and transverse scapular artery; serratus anterior tagged; a steel wire is passed through a hole at the medial/middle-third junction of the scapular spine for downward traction; step-wise anchoring to a rib.

*Woodward* — relocation of the trapezius and rhomboid origins more caudally (instead of moving the bone itself).

*Schrock* — 15–20 cm incision along the vertebral border; subperiosteal trapezius release; supraspinatus and infraspinatus released near the vertebral border and lifted ~1 cm laterally; step-wise reinsertion with wire suture.

</section>

<details>
<summary>Muscle Transpositions for Deltoid Paralysis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Prerequisites: intact rotator cuff, good wrist and hand function.

*Léo Mayer* — detachment of trapezius from the acromion + extension with fascia lata; distal end to proximal brachialis at 90° abduction; shoulder-spica cast 2 months. The effect in complete paralysis is largely tenodesis — clinically a working-position anchor.

*Bateman* — osteoplastic detachment of trapezius + resection of the lateral clavicle; reinsertion of an osteoplastic fragment to the humerus (more secure bony union).

*Ober* — saber incision; the short head of biceps taken off the coracoid osteoplastically + the long head of triceps off the infraglenoid tubercle → transposition to the acromion at 90° abduction; cast 4 weeks.

*Hildebrand* — pectoralis major detached from its rib origins (not the humeral insertion); pars sternocostalis + pars sternoclavicularis with care for the anterior thoracic nerves and the feeding thoracoacromial and axillary branches; rotated 90° about the humeral insertion and fixed to the acromion and lateral clavicle. Active abduction from a robust donor.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Obstetric Brachial Palsy — Anterior Tenotomy and Transpositions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Fairbank-Sever* — deltopectoral route with deltoid retraction; Z-lengthening of pectoralis major (the proximal stump remains attached to reduce adductor pull); coracobrachialis retracted; transverse division of subscapularis. Cast in external rotation and 90° abduction → at 2 weeks an abduction brace + rehabilitation. Sever modified Fairbank by omitting the capsulotomy (stiffness risk).

*Wickstrom* — anterior deltopectoral incision with lengthening of subscapularis and pectoralis major; the latissimus dorsi and teres major tendons divided close to bone; a second posterior incision — both tendons routed through the proximal deltoid and fixed in a prepared bed on the lateral head of triceps at full external rotation and 90° abduction; immobilisation 3 weeks.

*Merle d'Aubigné* — a conceptually similar technique with concurrent posterior capsulorrhaphy (to prevent posterior subluxation).

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Long Head of Biceps Tenodesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Deltopectoral route to the bicipital groove (opened along its entire length to the supraglenoid tubercle); the degenerated proximal stump is resected; the distal stump is fixed extra-articularly to the soft tissues around the groove. Traumatic ruptures — a small anterior arm incision to retrieve the distal stump. A long-arm cast in 130° elbow flexion for 25 days.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Rotator Cuff Reconstruction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Conservative treatment succeeds in 85–98% of cases when applied systematically for over a year. After failure — classical open techniques.

*Debeyre* — a saber incision over the acromion; the periosteum is divided longitudinally between the trapezius and deltoid origins; the deltoid is fully detached, from the trapezius side a periosteo-tendinous flap is raised; with a chisel a portion or the whole acromion plus the AC and lateral clavicle to the coracoclavicular ligament is resected. </section>

<details>
<summary>Resection and Reconstruction of the Proximal Humerus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Lawrence-Jones muscle-plastic resection* — an extended deltopectoral with detachment of the deltoid from the clavicle, acromion, and scapular spine; three capsulomuscular flaps (anterior — subscapularis 1–3 o'clock, superior — supraspinatus 11–1 o'clock, posterior — infraspinatus + teres minor 9–11 o'clock); economical resection of the humeral head; flaps anchored with sutures or wire in longitudinal bony grooves. Preserved active function via the preserved muscular cuff.

*Tumour resection* — supraperiosteal, en-bloc ablastic resection of the involved muscles with the bone.

*Reconstructions:* *Portugalov* — proximal portion of the ipsilateral fibula placed in the humeral medullary canal + 1–2 screws; the fibular head functions as the new caput. *Moskov* — lateral half of the clavicle with the AC joint preserved (cosmetic shoulder contour preserved). *Imamaliev* — frozen allograft, inserted step-wise with reinsertion of muscle attachments; allograft complications (fracture, fragmentation, resorption) have shifted the standard to custom endoprostheses.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Shoulder Arthroplasty</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Neer (1953)* — a unipolar vitallium prosthesis simulating the humeral head with holes for cuff reinsertion. Deltopectoral route; subscapularis tendon and long head of biceps are tagged and detached; the conjoint is taken off the coracoid osteoplastically. The head is removed and the medullary canal is reamed; three small cancellous grafts are placed in the triangular spaces between bone and stem.

*Engelhardt (1975)* — a cementless model with ceramic surface; primary fixation by bone plastic deformation + cone teeth; secondary fixation by bone ingrowth into surface microroughness.

*Modern standard* — anatomic hemiarthroplasty or total shoulder replacement for degenerative GH arthritis with an intact cuff; reverse total shoulder (Grammont) for cuff-tear arthropathy — inverting the ball-and-socket (ball on the glenoid, socket on the humerus) makes the deltoid the principal abductor in a cuff-deficient shoulder.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The version of the humeral component is critical for stability: insufficient retroversion (head set in neutral) predisposes to anterior subluxation.</li>
    <li>Excessive retroversion &gt;40° — to posterior instability.</li>
    <li>Classical Neer recommends 30–40° relative to the forearm, measured intra-operatively.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Stabilisation in Recurrent Anterior Instability</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Classical goals: *(1)* capsulorrhaphy / tenodesis limiting external rotation; *(2)* bony reconstruction in glenoid defect; *(3)* reinsertion of labrum and capsule.

*Putti-Platt (V. Putti, H. Platt)* — deltopectoral; subscapularis and the anterior capsule are cut in parallel, 1 cm lateral to the glenoid rim; at maximum external rotation the capsular sutures are tied with overlap; subscapularis is then sutured with shortening. Outcomes are unreliable in Bankart lesions or bony defects.

*Magnuson-Stack* — 8 cm anteromedial incision; subscapularis is taken off the lesser tuberosity osteoplastically → transposition to the crest of the greater tuberosity; external rotation permanently reduced by 40–50%. Velpeau 3 weeks.

*Bankart* — deltopectoral; conjoint osteoplastic; subscapularis tenotomy 1–1.5 cm from insertion (the capsule may be adherent); the anterior glenoid rim is exposed; freshening with curette; the labrum and antero-inferior capsule are reinserted through bone tunnels with transosseous sutures (today suture anchors). External rotation is preserved — the advantage over Putti-Platt and Magnuson-Stack.

*du Toit-Roux* — 8–10 cm deltopectoral incision; the conjoint is retracted; subscapularis is split semi-bluntly along its fibres to the glenoid rim; the capsule is divided along the same line; 1–3 metal staples are driven through capsule and glenoid neck intra- or extra-articularly under image control; the capsule is repaired; the split subscapularis is not sutured.

*Bone blocks ("blocs")* — historical predecessors of Latarjet. *Eden* — a corticocancellous graft from tibia or iliac crest placed vertically in the antero-inferior glenoid neck (intra-articular block). *Max Lange* — the graft placed extracapsularly as an external buttress against anterior displacement. *Oudar* — coracoid lengthening by oblique cut and translation of the distal fragment with the conjoint muscles in front of subscapularis with 1–2 sutures — the conceptual ancestor of Latarjet. *Judet* — a free rib graft wedged into freshened beds in the coracoid and the antero-inferior glenoid neck with a K-wire. *Latarjet (1954)* — the modern standard in bony defects >25%: the whole coracoid with the conjoint muscles → transposition to the antero-inferior glenoid rim with screws; the triple effect (bony augmentation, conjoint sling in ABER, dynamic hammock configuration of subscapularis).

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>A Bankart without freshening of the glenoid rim with a curette before reinsertion → non-union and recurrence; the bony bed must be bleeding.</li>
    <li>Putti-Platt and Magnuson-Stack — permanent reduction of external rotation (40–50%) and post-operative degenerative GH arthritis (capsulorrhaphy arthropathy on long-term follow-up).</li>
    <li>Off-track Hill-Sachs or bony defect &gt;25% — Latarjet/bone block, not a plain capsulolabral repair.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<summary>Stabilisation in Posterior Instability</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Scott* — posterior approach with deltoid detachment and resection of the prominent portion of the acromion as a free graft for posterior glenoid augmentation.

*Adams* — a posterior bone block + capsulorrhaphy in congenital posterior habitual subluxation and rare traumatic cases.

</div>

<details>
<summary>Open Reduction of Fresh and Old Fracture-Dislocations</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

When closed reduction fails (impression fracture of the antero-superior humeral head with the posterior glenoid rim locked into it — reverse Hill-Sachs in posterior dislocation). Posterior approach; internal rotation of the arm → progressive release of adhesions → articular surfaces. The impression groove is filled with a contoured iliac-crest autograft and fixed with 2–3 screws; alternatively, subscapularis + capsule are reinserted onto the new anterior rim Bankart-style. In children with open physes — K-wires. 70° abduction and neutral rotation for 45 days; with stable fixation — early active exercise (Müller).

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Shoulder Arthrodesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Indications: deltoid paralysis with intact trapezius and serratus anterior, severe destruction by infection (tuberculosis, septic arthritis), neglected traumatic injury, multiple failed procedures, unsuitability for arthroplasty. *Optimal position:* **45° abduction, 45° external rotation, 45° forward flexion** — Müller recommends intra-operative measurement of the angle between the lateral scapular border and the humerus (~75° corresponds to 45° abduction relative to the trunk). Three groups:

- *Extra-articular.* Albee — a compact tibial graft driven through the acromion into the head without opening the joint. Watson-Jones — a freshened acromion driven into a slot beneath the humeral cortex. *Inferior extra-articular* (biomechanically more sound): Vreden-Putti — internal scapular border as graft; Brittain/Britton — a compact tibial graft shaped like an arrow with a bifid base, wedged between scapula and humerus.
- *Intra-articular.* Lexer — antero-external approach with deltoid detachment; freshening and fixation. Matz — addition of metal osteosynthesis and compression. *Müller compression arthrodesis* (modern standard) — incision along the scapular spine distally over the humeral midline; articular surfaces freshened; primary fixation with 2 K-wires (horizontally through the humeral metaphysis + obliquely through the head); a compression screw along one wire, a second along the other; a compression plate with 2 short screws into the scapular spine + 2 cancellous screws through the head into the scapular neck. Long-arm cast 2–3 months.
- *Combined.* Merle d'Aubigné — a pedicled scapular-spine graft with the attached posterior acromion, hinged down to bridge the joint (pedicled on trapezius for blood supply). Gill — posterolateral incision; intra-articular fusion with the acromion seated in a slot in the head. Holevich-Paneva — saber incision across the scapula, acromion and lateral third of the clavicle; the space between caput humeri and the inferior surface of the acromion is filled with a free iliac-crest autograft; temporary K-wires → screws.
- *External-fixator compression arthrodesis (Charnley, Ilizarov)* — anterior incision extended posteriorly; 3 pins in the head/shaft; after 4 weeks the fixator is removed; cast continues for another 30 days.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The 45/45/45 position (abduction / external rotation / forward flexion) lets the hand reach the mouth, face, and contralateral ear with elbow flexion, and the perineum with extension.</li>
    <li>Excessive abduction yields permanent restriction of adduction and chronic discomfort.</li>
    <li>Müller compression arthrodesis is the modern standard among intra-articular variants; the classical extra-articular constructs (Albee, Vreden-Putti) remain useful when the articular surface is destroyed and an intra-articular approach is impractical.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Scapulectomy and Tumour Resections</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Tikhoff-Linberg* — total or subtotal scapulectomy with interscapulothoracic resection without limb sacrifice in malignant tumours with an intact humerus. The economical resections (acromion, body, coracoid) are reserved for post-traumatic states, osteomyelitis, tuberculosis, exostoses, and low-grade tumours. Kocher's access. For superior-portion resection — protect the suprascapular nerve and artery. When limb salvage is not possible — Berger's interscapulothoracic disarticulation; if possible, high humeral amputation with resection of the proximal third (the scapula and clavicle preserve the shoulder contour). Acromial-process fractures (differential with persistent os acromiale) — a malleolar screw or a Weber-style tension band.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Arthroscopic Procedures</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Arthroscopy has supplanted open surgery for many shoulder conditions. The arthroscopic survey systematically visualises the glenohumeral joint (labrum — Bankart, SLAP; cuff from the articular side; biceps tendon; cartilage; capsule), then the subacromial space (bursal-side cuff, coracoacromial arch, AC joint). Standard procedures: arthroscopic Bankart with suture anchors, cuff repair (single- or double-row), subacromial decompression and acromioplasty, biceps tenotomy or tenodesis, distal clavicle resection.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Postoperative Care</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Principles of postoperative care after shoulder surgery are organised around the tissue that has been repaired.

**Capsulolabral procedures** (Bankart, Boychev, Putti-Platt, Holevich-Paneva-Popova) — Velpeau immobilisation for 3 weeks in neutral rotation and gentle forward lift; active elbow and wrist-hand exercises from day 1; passive forward elevation and external rotation in neutral from week 4; active elevation from week 6; return-to-sport at 4–6 months.

**Bone blocks and Latarjet** — sling 4 weeks; passive and active-assisted elevation from week 4; active elevation from week 6; CT confirmation of coracoid union at week 12 before return-to-sport.

**Arthroplasty (anatomic)** — sling 4–6 weeks; passive exercises from day 1 (forward elevation 90° and external rotation 30° to week 6 to protect the subscapularis repair); active elevation and internal rotation above L5 after week 6; strengthening and resistance after 3 months.

**Reverse arthroplasty** — sling 2–3 weeks; early active elevation within the painless arc; internal rotation remains weak long-term (an anatomical deficit, not a repair failure).

**Cuff repair** — abduction sling 4–6 weeks depending on tear size and retraction; passive forward elevation and external rotation in neutral from day 1; active-assisted from week 6; active and resistance over 12 weeks; re-tear rates 20–40% — functional outcome often exceeds structural integrity.

**Arthrodesis** — long-arm cast 2–3 months until radiographic union; gradual weaning with protection from heavy loads to 6 months.

**Sprengel correction and muscle transpositions** — cast or abduction brace 6–8 weeks in the position of maximum correction; active-assisted work in the new vectors from week 8.

## Outcomes and Complications
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">


**Neurological.** Axillary nerve — injured in ~10–20% of anterior dislocations and many proximal humerus fractures; protection in deltoid-splitting incisions — the 5 cm limit. Musculocutaneous nerve — neurapraxia from vigorous retraction of the conjoint bundle; limit to 5 cm distal to the coracoid. Suprascapular nerve — at the spinoglenoid notch in deep posterior dissection; at the suprascapular notch in wide scapular approaches. Brachial plexus — compression in Sprengel correction without Ombredanne osteotomy; traction in high-energy dislocations.

**Vascular.** Bleeding from the anterior humeral circumflex ("three sisters") — ligation before subscapularis detachment. Axillary artery — at risk in deep medial dissection beneath the coracoid. Circumflex scapular artery in wide scapular exposures — retraction beneath the lateral border makes control difficult.

**Hardware.** K-wire migration (fatal cases reported in SC and AC fixation) — bend the ends, early removal (3–4 weeks). Tension-band wire on the clavicle — breakage by week 4. Inadequate retroversion in arthroplasty (neutral → anterior subluxation; >40° → posterior instability).

**Soft tissue.** Subscapularis dysfunction after a classical vertical tenotomy — lesser-tuberosity osteotomy or peel with a bone block delivers better functional outcomes. Tight capsulorrhaphy (Putti-Platt, Magnuson-Stack) — permanent restriction of external rotation (40–50%) and post-operative degenerative GH arthritis (capsulorrhaphy arthropathy on long-term follow-up).

**Union and healing.** Clavicular fracture non-union rates <5% with plate fixation and 10–15% with cerclage; freshening with curette before Bankart reinsertion is critical to union; re-osteosynthesis of the osteotomised acromion in the Debeyre or Hadzhistamov approach is critical to stability.

**Functional outcomes.** Classical long-term series (Boychev, Holevich-Paneva): instability recurrence 5–15% in correctly selected patients without bone loss. Modern meta-analyses: Bankart with anchor repair — recurrence 4–10%, Latarjet 1–5% in bony defect. Cuff repair: re-tear rates 20–40% depending on tear size, retraction, tendon quality and age; good-to-excellent Constant and UCLA scores in 75–85%. Anatomic TSA — implant survival 90% at 10 years, 85% at 15 years in degenerative GH arthritis. Reverse TSA — survival 85–90% at 10 years; effect on pain and active elevation dramatic, but internal rotation remains a weakness.

## Local Contributions
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">


The Bulgarian orthopaedic school has contributed to shoulder surgery at the topographic, reconstructive and prosthetic levels at once. Systematised in *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* of Boycho Boychev (with Ya. Holevich and A. Konforti; later editions with Chokanov), these contributions remain part of the national curriculum and are taught today at the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya.

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school; the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (with Ya. Holevich and A. Konforti; later editions with Chokanov) remains a curricular reference and houses the topographic concept of the clavipectoral / pectoral / subpectoral triangles, which explains why dissection medial to the short head of biceps beneath the coracoid directly risks the axillary artery, median nerve and ulnar nerve.

**Boychev's procedure <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** The iconic Bulgarian operation for recurrent anterior dislocation without significant glenoid bone loss. The conjoint bundle (*coracobrachialis* + the short head of biceps) is taken off the coracoid osteoplastically, routed *behind subscapularis* (between the muscle and the anterior capsule), and reinserted to the coracoid in its new position. The transferred bundle creates an active anterior "hammock" over the antero-inferior head in abduction–external rotation and simultaneously shortens subscapularis with limitation of external rotation. The anatomical risk is the musculocutaneous nerve in coracobrachialis — the nerve enters 1–6 cm below the coracoid tip and is at risk during routing.

**Holevich–Paneva–Popova <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** A Bulgarian modification of the Bankart principle. Deltopectoral route; conjoint osteoplastic; subscapularis tenotomy 1–1.5 cm from insertion, the belly retracted medially; in full external rotation the glenoid rim is freshened with a curette; through pre-drilled holes small corticocancellous autografts ("transplants") are driven — 4–5 mm tall, ~1 cm long, with reverse-direction barbs to prevent pull-out. A strong silk suture is tied to the base of each transplant; the capsule is passed over it and sutured in overlap — simultaneous bony reconstruction of the antero-inferior rim and capsulorrhaphy. Soft immobilisation in moderate abduction and internal rotation for 20–30 days. The same authors describe a *posterior variant* for posterior habitual dislocation and a *combined arthrodesis* with a free iliac-crest autograft.

**Hadzhistamov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** The posterior trans-spinal approach — the conceptual precursor of modern transacromial and "cobra" approaches. An osteotomy across the middle of the scapular spine (without opening the AC joint); a cutaneo-muscular flap is hinged downward like a page, exposing the entire upper rotator cuff, the greater tuberosity facet, the long head of biceps, and the posterior segment of the glenohumeral capsule. Indicated in classical reconstructions of massive postero-superior tears in which the standard posterior interval between infraspinatus and teres minor is inadequate. Re-osteosynthesis of the osteotomised fragment is critical for postoperative stability.

**Balchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** An independent Bulgarian modification in the Magnuson-Stack spirit: lateral transposition of the subscapularis tendon from the lesser to the greater tuberosity, with permanent reduction of external rotation and limitation of anterior instability. Described in parallel with the American technique and retained as a national variant in the Bulgarian curriculum.

**Portugalov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** For tumoural or post-traumatic loss of the proximal third of the humerus — replacement with the proximal part of the ipsilateral fibula, whose head acts as the new caput humeri, fixed in the medullary canal with 1–2 screws.

**Moskov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Replacement of the proximal humerus with the lateral half of the clavicle with the AC joint preserved (preserves the cosmetic shoulder contour).

**Imamaliev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Replacement with a frozen allograft, step-wise inserted with reinsertion of the muscle attachments. The historical standard, displaced by custom endoprostheses because of high rates of allograft fracture, fragmentation, and resorption.

**Ombredanne <sup class="natflag">FR</sup>** (a French contribution adopted into the Bulgarian curriculum). Prophylactic clavicular osteotomy for planned descents in Sprengel correction >2 cm or pre-operative paraesthesia: pre-emptive osteotomy of the congenitally short clavicle protects the brachial plexus from compression in the thoracic outlet.

From the classical Russian and Soviet schools, tightly woven into the Bulgarian curriculum:

**R. R. Vreden <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Extra-articular shoulder arthrodesis with the internal scapular border as graft (Vreden-Putti) — a biomechanically sound inferior extra-articular construct.

**Ya. L. Tsivyan, V. D. Chaklin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Major contributors in spinal surgery (vertebrotomy, Chaklin's spondylodesis); their contributions to general operative tactics are taught in the integrated Bulgarian curriculum.

**A. A. Korzh <sup class="natflag">UA</sup>.** Complex revision procedures from the Kharkov school.

**G. A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Compression arthrodesis of the shoulder with external fixator — anterior incision extended posteriorly; 3 pins in the head/shaft; after 4 weeks the fixator is removed; cast continues for another 30 days.

</section>



## Classical Sources
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">


The surgical anatomy of the shoulder was systematised at the beginning of the 19th century by Velpeau, whose *Nouveaux éléments de médecine opératoire* (1832) gave the founding description of the shoulder bandage that still bears his name. Dupuytren's *Leçons orales* (1832) contributed the Hôtel-Dieu clinical series on shoulder dislocations. Astley Cooper's *A Treatise on Dislocations* (1822) gave the founding English-language description of recurrent anterior dislocation. Heinrich Braun (1907) introduced the supraclavicular brachial plexus block — the antecedent of modern ultrasound-guided ISB / SCB — and Hoffa (1905) consolidated the orthopaedic operative tradition.

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical lineage — Brachial plexus anaesthesia</strong>
  <p>Regional anaesthesia of the upper limb has a well-documented genealogy whose textbook source remains *Die Lokalanästhesie* by Heinrich Braun (Leipzig, 1907). Braun synthesised the American experiments of Crile (1887) and Cushing with endoneural injections into the exposed plexus, and introduced the terms "endoneurale" and "perineurale" injection (attributed to Matas). The line continues with Kulenkampff (1911) — the first truly percutaneous supraclavicular brachial plexus block — and with Winnie in the 1970s, whose work underpins the modern ultrasound-guided blocks at interscalene (ISB), supraclavicular (SCB), infraclavicular (ICB) and axillary (AXB) levels.</p>

    <li><strong>Velpeau 1832</strong> — *Nouveaux éléments de médecine opératoire*, volume 1 — bandages and reduction of the shoulder (<a href="https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_43901x01">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Astley Cooper 1822 / 1832 ed.</strong> — *A Treatise on Dislocations and Fractures of the Joints* — recurrent dislocation (<a href="https://archive.org/details/treatiseondisloc00coop_0">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Heinrich Braun 1907</strong> — *Die Lokalanästhesie* — supraclavicular brachial plexus block (<a href="https://archive.org/details/dielokalansthe00brau">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Hoffa 1905</strong> — *Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie* — shoulder operations (<a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderortho00hoff">facsimile</a>).</li>
