## Introduction

The shoulder girdle, comprising the glenohumeral joint, the acromioclavicular joint, the sternoclavicular joint, and the scapulothoracic articulation, provides the greatest range of motion of any joint in the body — a functional capacity achieved through a substantial sacrifice of bony stability in favor of soft-tissue stabilization. The surgical anatomy of the shoulder reflects this design: a relatively shallow glenoid, a generous capsule with reinforcing ligaments, the rotator cuff providing dynamic stability, and the deltoid and periscapular muscles powering motion. The orthopedic surgeon’s approaches to the shoulder must navigate this complex muscular envelope while protecting the axillary nerve (the most vulnerable nerve in shoulder surgery), the musculocutaneous nerve, and the brachial plexus and axillary vessels in the deeper structures. This chapter, drawing on Orthopaedic Surgical Approaches, Netter's Concise Orthopaedic Anatomy, Gray's Anatomy, and the classical Bulgarian Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology of Boycho Boychev — the namesake of the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya — addresses the surgical anatomy and principal approaches to the shoulder, incorporating the classical Bulgarian and Soviet (Holevich-Paneva, Balchev, Hadzhistamov, Moskov, Imamaliev) techniques which, together with the Boychev procedure for recurrent anterior dislocation, remain part of the Bulgarian orthopedic curriculum.

The glenohumeral joint is a ball-and-socket articulation between the relatively small, slightly retroverted glenoid (typical retroversion 5 to 7 degrees, with inferior tilt of about 4 to 5 degrees) and the substantially larger humeral head (covered by approximately 120 degrees of articular surface). The size mismatch produces the characteristic mobility-stability trade-off; only 25 to 30 percent of the humeral head is in contact with the glenoid at any time. The glenoid labrum is a fibrocartilaginous ring around the glenoid that deepens the socket by approximately 50 percent and provides attachment for the capsular ligaments and the long head of the biceps tendon.

The acromion forms the lateral roof of the joint, with anatomical variants relevant to surgery — the acromial morphology classified as type I flat, type II curved, type III hooked (Bigliani) is associated with rotator cuff impingement, particularly in type III. The os acromiale is a failure of fusion of the acromial ossification center (occurring in 1 to 8 percent of the population), with the meso-acromion (between the middle and posterior acromial ossification centers) being the most common type and a recognized cause of impingement and rotator cuff pathology. The coracoid process projects anteriorly and provides attachment for the conjoint tendon (short head of biceps and coracobrachialis), the coracoacromial ligament, the coracoclavicular ligaments (conoid and trapezoid), and the coracohumeral ligament. The coracoid is the source of the bony graft in the Latarjet procedure for glenoid bone loss ([Topic Trauma-12](/en/trauma/12/)). The clavicle completes the shoulder girdle, articulating with the acromion laterally (AC joint) and the sternum medially (SC joint). Its anatomy and pathology were addressed in [Topic Trauma-11](/en/trauma/11/).

## Infraclavicular and Axillary Topography

The classical Bulgarian anatomical school describes the infraclavicular region (regio infraclavicularis) — bounded by the inferior border of the clavicle, the lateral edge of the sternum, the third rib, and the anterior border of deltoid — as the key topographic zone for anterior shoulder approaches. The platysma covers fine cutaneous nerves (anterior supraclavicular branches and intercostal twigs); deep to the superficial fascia lies pectoralis major with its own fascia. Between pectoralis major and deltoid the deltopectoral groove forms, expanding proximally into the deltopectoral triangle — a fatty-lymphatic space in which the cephalic vein joins the axillary vein at the upper border of pectoralis minor. The infraclavicular space subdivides into three triangles: the clavipectoral triangle (between clavicle and the upper border of pectoralis minor), the pectoral triangle (behind pectoralis minor) and the subpectoral triangle (below its inferior border). In the clavipectoral triangle the clavipectoral fascia is pierced by branches of the thoracoacromial artery (pectoral, deltoid, acromial, clavicular branches), all of which must be identified and controlled during extended deltopectoral exposures.

In the pectoral triangle the neurovascular bundle sits between pectoralis minor anteriorly, subclavius posteriorly, the coracoid laterally, and serratus anterior medially; the bundle runs approximately 2 cm anterior to subscapularis. Laterally the musculocutaneous nerve leaves the lateral cord at the inferior border of pectoralis minor and hugs the axillary artery before entering coracobrachialis; the median nerve forms from its two roots 3-5 cm below the inferior border of pectoralis minor; the ulnar nerve detaches 3.5-4 cm above the latissimus dorsi tendon and travels along the medial side of the artery. This arrangement is critical for extended anterior approaches to the humeral neck and for brachial plexus dissection — the concentration of all neurovascular structures between the coracoid and the axillary fold explains why proceeding medial to the short head of biceps is not advisable.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The axillary neurovascular bundle lies approximately 2 cm anterior to subscapularis in the pectoral triangle. This distance defines the safe working field when mobilizing the conjoint tendon — dissection deep medial to the short head of biceps, especially with coracoid osteotomy during an extended deltopectoral exposure, directly risks axillary artery, median nerve and ulnar nerve injury.</p>
</aside>

The rotator cuff comprises four muscles forming a cuff around the humeral head: Supraspinatus: Originates from the supraspinous fossa of the scapula, passes under the acromion, and inserts on the superior facet of the greater tuberosity. Function: initial 30 degrees of abduction; participates with deltoid throughout abduction range. Most commonly torn of the rotator cuff muscles. Infraspinatus: Originates from the infraspinous fossa, inserts on the middle facet of the greater tuberosity. Function: external rotation of the shoulder. Teres minor: Originates from the lateral border of the scapula, inserts on the inferior facet of the greater tuberosity. Function: external rotation, assists infraspinatus. Subscapularis: Originates from the subscapular fossa (anterior surface of the scapula), inserts on the lesser tuberosity. Function: internal rotation; the principal anterior stabilizer of the shoulder. The rotator cuff muscles function principally to compress the humeral head into the glenoid, providing dynamic stability, while permitting the deltoid to generate the principal abduction force.

## Deltoid

The deltoid is the principal motor of shoulder abduction beyond the initial range provided by the supraspinatus. It has three heads: Anterior (clavicular) deltoid: From the lateral third of the clavicle, producing flexion and internal rotation. Middle (acromial) deltoid: From the acromion, producing abduction. Posterior (spinous) deltoid: From the spine of the scapula, producing extension and external rotation. The deltoid inserts on the deltoid tuberosity of the humerus. The axillary nerve innervates the deltoid (and teres minor) and runs through the quadrilateral space (bounded by teres minor superiorly, teres major inferiorly, long head of triceps medially, and humeral shaft laterally) — passing approximately 5 to 7 cm distal to the acromion along the deltoid undersurface, the critical anatomical relationship that limits the size of safe deltoid-splitting incisions.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The axillary nerve passes around the surgical neck of the humerus approximately 5-7 cm distal to the acromion along the deltoid undersurface, after traversing the quadrilateral space (teres minor, teres major, long head of triceps, humeral shaft). This limits any deltoid-splitting incision to about 5 cm distal to the acromion.</p>
</aside>

## Periscapular Muscles

The periscapular muscles stabilize the scapula on the chest wall: Trapezius: From the occiput and spinous processes, inserting on the scapular spine, acromion, and clavicle. Provides scapular elevation, retraction, and upward rotation.

Rhomboids major and minor: From the thoracic spinous processes to the medial border of the scapula. Retract and downwardly rotate the scapula. Serratus anterior: From the ribs to the medial border of the scapula (anterior surface). Protracts and upwardly rotates the scapula; scapular winging results from serratus anterior weakness (typically from long thoracic nerve injury). Levator scapulae: From cervical transverse processes to superior medial border of scapula. Elevates the scapula.

## Neurovascular Structures


## Axillary Nerve (C5-C6)

The axillary nerve is the principal neurological structure at risk in shoulder surgery. It branches from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus, passes posteriorly through the quadrilateral space, and then winds around the surgical neck of the humerus deep to the deltoid, dividing into branches to the deltoid and to the teres minor, with a sensory branch (superior lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm) supplying the skin over the lateral deltoid. The proximity of the axillary nerve to the surgical neck of the humerus (approximately 5 to 7 cm distal to the acromion along the deltoid undersurface) is the critical surgical anatomical fact that limits the extent of deltoid-splitting incisions. The axillary nerve is injured in approximately 10 to 20 percent of anterior shoulder dislocations and in many proximal humerus fractures ([Topic Trauma-13](/en/trauma/13/)).

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The axillary nerve carries the sensory branch (superior lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm) to the skin over the lateral deltoid; this is the patch to test for sensation after every anterior shoulder dislocation or proximal humerus fracture (10-20% have axillary nerve injury).</p>
</aside>


## Musculocutaneous Nerve (C5-C7)

The musculocutaneous nerve branches from the lateral cord of the brachial plexus, pierces the coracobrachialis muscle (typically about 5 to 8 cm distal to the coracoid, the “safe zone” for retraction during anterior shoulder surgery is approximately 5 cm distal to the coracoid), and supplies the biceps brachii, brachialis, and coracobrachialis. It then continues as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The musculocutaneous nerve pierces coracobrachialis approximately 5-8 cm distal to the coracoid; vigorous retraction of the conjoint tendon beyond about 5 cm distal to the coracoid risks neuropraxia. The 'safe zone' is the proximal 5 cm below the coracoid tip.</p>
</aside>

The brachial plexus lies medial to the coracoid and posterior to the pectoralis minor. The axillary artery and vein travel with the plexus. These structures are at risk in deeper dissection medial to the coracoid and in high-energy shoulder dislocations.

## Suprascapular Nerve (C5-C6)

The suprascapular nerve passes through the suprascapular notch (deep to the transverse scapular ligament) and supplies the supraspinatus, then continues around the spinoglenoid notch to supply the infraspinatus. Compression or traction at these notches produces suprascapular neuropathy with weakness of the supraspinatus and/or infraspinatus.


## Long Thoracic Nerve (C5-C7)

The long thoracic nerve runs along the lateral chest wall on the surface of serratus anterior, supplying the muscle. Injury produces scapular winging with prominence of the medial border of the scapula on forward elevation of the arm.

## The Deltopectoral Approach

The deltopectoral approach is the most commonly used approach to the anterior shoulder, providing access for anterior open reduction of dislocations, open reduction and internal fixation of proximal humerus fractures, anterior glenoid procedures (Bankart, Latarjet, bone block), shoulder arthroplasty (anatomic and reverse), and tumor resections.

## Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned in beach chair (45 to 60 degrees of trunk elevation) with the head supported in a specialized head holder; the affected arm is draped freely to permit movement during the procedure. Alternatively, the lateral decubitus position can be used for shoulder arthroscopy with the affected side up and traction applied to the arm.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

The incision is along the deltopectoral groove, beginning just inferior to the coracoid process and extending distally toward the deltoid insertion for approximately 10 to 15 cm. The anterior axillary fold marks the lower extent of the deltoid.

## Approach

The deltopectoral interval between the deltoid (axillary nerve) and the pectoralis major (medial and lateral pectoral nerves) is identified — the classical landmark is the cephalic vein running in the interval. The vein is typically taken either medially (with the pectoralis) or laterally (with the deltoid); the lateral retraction may be safer with respect to vein preservation. The deltoid is then retracted laterally and the pectoralis medially. The clavipectoral fascia is incised lateral to the conjoint tendon. The conjoint tendon (coracobrachialis and short head of biceps) is identified and retracted medially with attention to the musculocutaneous nerve entering coracobrachialis approximately 5 to 8 cm distal to the coracoid (this defines the safe zone for retraction). The subscapularis is now visualized, with the anterior humeral circumflex vessels (the “three sisters”) at its inferior border. These vessels are typically ligated or coagulated. The subscapularis is then incised (with various techniques, vertical incision, lesser tuberosity osteotomy, peel from the lesser tuberosity for shoulder arthroplasty) to expose the underlying capsule and joint.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>On the deltopectoral approach, the anterior humeral circumflex vessels (the 'three sisters') at the inferior border of subscapularis must be controlled before subscapularis takedown — uncontrolled bleeding obscures the field. Subscapularis disruption is a common postoperative concern; lesser tuberosity osteotomy or subscapularis peel with bone block may outperform the historical vertical tenotomy.</p>
</aside>


## Variations

The extended deltopectoral approach carries the incision more proximally and distally for extended exposure (e.g., for tumors or revision procedures). The minimally invasive deltopectoral approach uses a smaller skin incision and limited dissection for percutaneous and arthroscopically-assisted procedures.


## Complications

The complications of the deltopectoral approach include musculocutaneous nerve injury (rare, prevented by gentle retraction of the conjoint tendon and avoidance of vigorous retraction more than 5 cm distal to the coracoid), axillary nerve injury (rare in this approach but possible with extended distal dissection), vascular injury (rare), and subscapularis dysfunction (common after various subscapularis takedown techniques; subscapularis peel with bone block or lesser tuberosity osteotomy may produce better outcomes than the historical vertical incision).

## The Posterior Shoulder Approach

The posterior approach provides access for posterior glenoid procedures (posterior Bankart, posterior bone block), posterior shoulder instability surgery, posterior reduction of locked posterior dislocations, scapular body fixation, and tumor resection of the posterior scapula.

## Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned in lateral decubitus with the affected side up, or prone. The arm is draped freely.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

The scapular spine and the acromion are the principal landmarks. The incision extends from the posterolateral corner of the acromion distally over the lateral border of the deltoid for 6 to 10 cm. An alternative incision parallels the scapular spine.

## Approach

The deltoid is split in line with its fibers between the middle and posterior heads, with the split limited to approximately 5 cm distal to the acromion to protect the axillary nerve (which runs around the surgical neck of the humerus). The posterior deltoid is reflected inferiorly. The infraspinatus and teres minor interval is identified (often by palpation of the fibers, since the interval is not as visible as the deltopectoral interval). The interval is developed, with the infraspinatus retracted superiorly and the teres minor retracted inferiorly. The posterior capsule is then exposed. Alternative techniques include the infraspinatus split approach (splitting the infraspinatus in line with its fibers, which provides direct access to the posterior capsule with preservation of the muscle but with potential for some loss of function), and the detachment of infraspinatus from the greater tuberosity (with later repair, providing the most extensive exposure but with concern for repair healing).

## Complications

The complications of the posterior approach include axillary nerve injury (the principal concern, prevented by limiting the deltoid split to 5 cm distal to the acromion), suprascapular nerve injury (in the spinoglenoid notch, at risk with deep dissection), and rotator cuff dysfunction (from infraspinatus disruption).

## Wide Scapular Approaches

For extensive scapular pathology (tumours, osteomyelitis, post-traumatic deformities, partial or total scapulectomy) the limited posterior approaches are insufficient; the classical Bulgarian school describes two extended variants. The L-shaped approach has a vertical limb along the medial (vertebral) border and a horizontal limb parallel to the upper angle, running approximately 2 cm above it. The patient is prone; the medial cutaneous branches of the dorsal rami are divided through subcutaneous tissue. The skin-fascial flap is reflected laterally, the ascending portion of trapezius is freed from the vertebral border, and the rhomboids major and minor are divided near their attachment. The entire posterior surface of the scapula is then exposed. Anatomical hazards are: the deep branch of the transverse cervical artery lies parallel to the medial border about 0.5-1 cm medial and must be ligated; when releasing supraspinatus in its supero-medial corner, the anastomosis between the transverse cervical and suprascapular arteries; when releasing infraspinatus, the circumflex scapular artery, which on bleeding retracts beneath the lateral border. Identifying the scapular notch with omohyoideus as a guide is critical for protecting the suprascapular nerve (beneath the superior transverse scapular ligament) and the suprascapular vessels (above the ligament).

The T-shaped (hocky-stick) extended approach combines the vertical incision along the medial border with an oblique limb along the scapular spine up to the AC joint; the skin-muscle flaps are reflected superiorly and inferiorly and provide the widest exposure — required for subtotal scapulectomy and for releasing the lateral angle with the glenoid. For complete scapulectomy, after freeing the medial border the inferior angle is identified, teres major is divided close to its insertion, and the scapular circumflex vessels are ligated; access to the lateral angle requires releasing deltoid from the acromion and scapular spine (except its most medial third) and dividing the joint capsule and rotator cuff tendons.

## Posterior Trans-Spinal Approach (Hadzhistamov)

Described by the Bulgarian orthopaedist Hadzhistamov, this is a postero-superior exposure with osteotomy of the scapular spine through its midpoint, without opening the AC joint. The patient is lateral on the healthy side. The skin incision runs 1 cm above the scapular spine for 7-9 cm, curves around the AC joint, and descends towards the lateral humeral epicondyle for 9-11 cm. The skin-subcutaneous flap is reflected medially and inferiorly, the scapular spine is divided longitudinally along its midline (Gigli saw or oscillating saw); the resulting bone fragment is rotated downward and outward like a page, with two transmuscular incisions descending from its ends — a lateral one approximately 4-5 cm and a medial one of similar length. The transverse portion of trapezius is divided, exposing supraspinatus and infraspinatus and the full width of the rotator cuff. The approach affords wide visualization of the superior cuff, the greater tuberosity, the long head of biceps and the posterior glenohumeral capsule for procedures in which the standard posterior infraspinatus-teres minor approach is inadequate — chiefly classical repairs of massive postero-superior cuff tears and combined reconstructions for degenerative lesions. Closure involves osteosynthesis of the scapular spine with wire or a small screw and reinsertion of trapezius.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Hadzhistamov's trans-spinal approach is a conceptual ancestor of modern transacromial and superior "cobra" approaches: osteotomizing the scapular spine (without disturbing the AC joint) from a posterior skin incision delivers wide exposure of the rotator cuff from above while preserving deltoid function. Osteosynthesis of the osteotomized fragment is critical for postoperative stability.</p>
</aside>

## Anterior Axillary (Henry’s) Approach

The anterior axillary approach uses a more inferior anterior incision that avoids the visible anterior shoulder scar and provides access for anterior shoulder procedures with cosmetic priority (selected female patients, athletes), as well as access to certain pathologies of the anterior shoulder.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

The incision is in the anterior axillary skin fold, beginning approximately 1 to 2 cm distal to the coracoid and extending downward into the axilla. The incision is cosmetic when healed.

## Approach

The dissection proceeds upward to the deltopectoral groove and proceeds in the standard deltopectoral approach. The advantage is the smaller, less visible scar; the disadvantage is the more limited proximal exposure and the somewhat more demanding orientation.

## The Superior (Saber-Cut) Approach

The superior approach (also called the saber-cut approach or the superior arthroscopic-assisted approach) provides access to the superior aspect of the shoulder for distal clavicle excision, AC joint procedures, superior rotator cuff repair (open), and proximal humerus fracture access in selected cases.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

A small superior incision is made over the AC joint or over the greater tuberosity, depending on the desired exposure. The incision is typically 4 to 6 cm in length.

## Approach

The deltoid is split in line with its fibers (the deltoid-splitting approach) for a maximum of approximately 5 cm distal to the acromion to protect the axillary nerve. The supraspinatus tendon and its insertion on the greater tuberosity are exposed. For AC joint procedures, the trapezoid fascia is incised over the joint and the joint capsule is opened.

For rotator cuff procedures, the tendinous insertion is identified and the repair is performed. The superior approach is largely supplanted by arthroscopic techniques for most rotator cuff and AC joint procedures, but retains a role in selected open procedures.

## Classical Transacromial Approaches

The Bulgarian school describes two transacromial exposures of didactic value for understanding the evolution of superior shoulder approaches. The economical transacromial approach performs partial osteotomy of the acromion along a pre-marked line (frontal or lateral), with a hole pre-drilled beneath the acromion to protect the rotator cuff from the instrument (osteotome, Gigli or oscillating saw). A small portion of deltoid is detached at the AC joint, the flap is reflected distally, and the cuff is exposed. On closure the resected acromial fragment is fixed with wire or a small screw, and deltoid is sutured to the periosteum of the remaining acromion and to the overlying fascia. The saber-cut approach is its extended form with complete detachment of the acromion plus a portion of the clavicle lateral to the coracoclavicular ligament; on closure the acromio-clavicular complex is reconstructed. Both variants offer full visualization of the rotator cuff for massive retracted tears, for classical periarthritis with ossifications, and for selected complex anterior dislocations with avulsion of the greater tuberosity.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>When splitting deltoid in line with its fibres, the split must not exceed 3-4 cm from its origin — branches of the axillary nerve to pars acromialis enter the muscle at the midpoint between the lateral acromial edge and the deltoid tuberosity. Longer splitting (more than 5 cm) is acceptable only with a paralysed deltoid. The safest sites for deltoid-splitting are the boundary lines between pars clavicularis and pars acromialis, and between pars acromialis and pars spinalis.</p>
</aside>

## Shoulder Arthroscopy

Shoulder arthroscopy has largely supplanted open surgery for many shoulder pathologies and merits brief consideration in the context of surgical anatomy. The principal portals include: Posterior portal: Approximately 2 to 3 cm inferior and 1 to 2 cm medial to the posterolateral corner of the acromion. The “soft spot” between the infraspinatus and teres minor. Provides the principal viewing portal for the glenohumeral joint. Anterior portal: Established under direct vision through the rotator interval (between the supraspinatus and subscapularis). Provides the principal working portal for anterior shoulder procedures. Anterolateral portal: Inferior to the anterior acromial edge. Used for subacromial procedures and for some rotator cuff repairs. Posterolateral portal: Inferior to the posterior acromial edge. Used for subacromial procedures. Lateral portal: Through the deltoid 2 to 3 cm lateral to the acromial edge. Used for subacromial procedures and rotator cuff repair. Multiple accessory portals (e.g., 5 o’clock portal, Nevasier portal, trans-rotator cuff portal) are added as needed for specific procedures. The arthroscopic examination systematically visualizes the glenohumeral joint, with attention to the labrum (Bankart and SLAP lesions), the rotator cuff (articular-sided tears), the long head of biceps, the cartilage surfaces, and the capsule. The subacromial space is examined separately for bursal-sided cuff tears, the coracoacromial arch, and the AC joint.

## Approaches and Operations on the Clavicle

Because of its subcutaneous position the entire clavicle and its muscular attachments (sternocleidomastoid and trapezius superiorly, pectoralis major and deltoid inferiorly) are easily palpable. The standard approach to the clavicular shaft is a longitudinal slightly curved incision — convex superiorly or inferiorly — that does not lie directly over the bone (to minimize scar along the line of muscular attachments). For wider operations involving the supraclavicular and infraclavicular regions an S-shaped incision provides access to both zones. The platysma is divided transversely, inevitably cutting supraclavicular nerves. Subperiosteal release must respect that the external jugular vein lies 0.5-1 cm lateral to the clavicular head of sternocleidomastoid; the subclavian artery and vein lie beneath subclavius; the brachial plexus and subclavian vessels cross the clavicle posteriorly on average 2 cm lateral to the sternocleidomastoid attachment; and the suprascapular artery runs along the posterior surface of the clavicle from the midpoint to its acromial end.

The approach to the sternoclavicular joint uses a skin incision along the medial quarter of the clavicle crossing the joint to the midline; the jugular fossa and the sternal end of the clavicle are the landmarks. The approach to the acromioclavicular joint uses a curved incision over the lateral quarter of the clavicle and the antero-superior border of the acromion; the deltoid and trapezius attachments are released subperiosteally to expose the joint capsule.

## Osteosynthesis of Clavicle Fractures

For displaced fractures a 10 cm slightly curved incision is made along the inferior border of the clavicle; after platysma division the fragments are exposed strictly subperiosteally, protecting the subclavian vessels and brachial plexus. The classical fixation variants are: intramedullary Kirschner wire (inserted antegrade into the lateral fragment to the fracture site, then retrograde into the medial fragment after reduction — suitable for paediatric or simple transverse patterns and acromial-end fractures, with bending of both ends to prevent migration); cerclage wiring (a historic technique with significant risk of wire breakage in the first 3-4 weeks); and plate osteosynthesis (the contemporary standard for diaphyseal fractures in adults, restoring length and providing stability for early functional rehabilitation).

For clavicular pseudarthrosis (congenital or traumatic) the principles are preservation of clavicular length, sufficient osteoplastic material (tibial graft with periosteum, fibular segment, iliac crest or rib for large defects) and stable osteosynthesis. Jonasch's classification organizes the variants according to defect size and location. Resection of the lateral end of the clavicle (approximately 1-1.5 cm) is a standard procedure for chronic post-traumatic AC symptomatology; postoperative motion is allowed on day 7-10. Exarticulating resection is indicated for vascular and plexus operations, bone tumours, osteoarticular tuberculosis, osteomyelitis and lymphatic procedures — subperiosteal for benign tumours, with periosteum and adjacent musculature for malignant disease. The resulting defect can be reconstructed with a fibular segment or iliac crest graft; postoperatively a chest-arm cast is applied. Total claviculectomy follows the same indications when destructive changes involve the entire structure; principles include accurate incision over the clavicular projection, simultaneous release of all attached muscles with periosteum, packing of the periosteal bed with fibrin sponge to prevent haematoma, and piecemeal osteotomy with sequential extraction of the clavicle to reduce vascular injury.

Over 30 techniques have been described for acute acromioclavicular dislocations. The classical Bulgarian-systematized variants include: **Bosworth technique** — the displaced acromial end is reduced and a drill makes a vertical channel through the clavicle above the coracoid; a screw is then passed through the channel into the coracoid; supplementary percutaneous Kirschner wire through the acromion is recommended and removed at 3-4 weeks. **AO/Weber technique** — cancellous screw plus Weber tension-band, with reconstruction of the disrupted coracoclavicular ligaments using half the coracobrachialis tendon passed around the clavicle and sutured back to itself. **Heyzlauer technique** — transposition of the coracoclavicular ligament to form a new acromioclavicular ligament, avoiding screw or wire fixation that limits clavicular rotation. **Dewar-Barrington technique** — osteotomy of the coracoid tip with the attached conjoint muscles (coracobrachialis and short head of biceps) and screw fixation to the clavicle — dynamic depression of the lateral clavicle through the pull of the conjoint muscles. Preservation of 1 cm of coracoid length and pre-drilling before osteotomy are technically critical to prevent fragmentation. **Bunnell technique** — passive fascia lata reconstruction passed through bone holes in the clavicle and beneath the coracoid using a Deschamps needle; supplementary short K-wires across the AC joint until graft healing. **Rowe technique** — longitudinal incision over the AC ligament; oblique osteotomy of the distal clavicle so that the inferior cut ends at the insertion of the AC ligament; after fragment removal the two ligament ends are sutured with overlap.

For SC joint resection (chronic infection and tumour) the most important step is restoration of the interclavicular ligament along with fixation of the intra-articular disc; resection of the sternal end of the clavicle alone is not recommended by most authors. For acute anterior or posterior SC dislocation a transverse incision over the joint is used; posterior dislocation may compress the trachea and great vessels and requires emergent surgery (the literature describes approximately 41 cases, with Beckman 1923 describing bilateral). Pre-operative tomography is mandatory (Kennedy 1949, Holmdahl 1954). Stabilization with a Kirschner wire across the joint carries a risk of intrathoracic migration and is not recommended by many authors; the Lownan variant uses a fascia lata graft through two central holes in the joint.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>Percutaneous Kirschner wire across the sternoclavicular joint or within the manubrium carries a real risk of intrathoracic migration (including into pericardium and great vessels) — fatal cases have been described. Bending both ends above skin, early removal (3-4 weeks), and reconstruction of the interclavicular ligament with a fascial strip are mandatory precautions.</p>
</aside>

Sprengel deformity results from failure of scapular descent during embryonic development, often associated with an omovertebral bone — a fibrous, cartilaginous or osseous bridge between the supero-medial angle of the scapula and the cervical spine. The surgical aims are: 1) release of the omovertebral attachment; 2) descent of the scapula; 3) functional and cosmetic correction. The optimal age for operation is over 10 years — in adults the procedure is extensive and the results poor because of established cervical changes. Operations fall into four groups: excision of the omovertebral bone (as a stand-alone procedure); excision of the supero-medial corner of the scapula; subtotal scapulectomy; and scapular translocation.

**Excision of the omovertebral bone** — Prone with slight neck flexion. Incision along the spinous processes from C1 to T9. The lateral border of trapezius is identified and beneath it the rhomboids; after their division the omovertebral bridge is exposed and resected, freeing the upper angle of the scapula. **Excision of the supero-medial scapula** — Transverse incision along the scapular spine; trapezius is osteoplastically detached, levator scapulae and rhomboids are released, and the supero-medial corner is resected. **Green technique** — Vertebral border incision 2-3 cm below the inferior angle. Trapezius is carefully released from the scapular spine (preserving its integrity for reinsertion), then supraspinatus and infraspinatus are stripped strictly subperiosteally, protecting the suprascapular nerve and transverse scapular artery. Serratus anterior is released and tagged. A hole is drilled in the scapular spine at the medial-middle third junction; a steel wire is passed through it for downward traction. The scapula is descended into the desired position and fixed in stepwise fashion to the ribs with wire sutures. **Woodward technique** — Subperiosteal release of trapezius and rhomboids from their spinal origins, with re-insertion of these origins more caudally — conceptually different from Green in working "top-down" by relocating muscle origins rather than pulling the scapula. **Schrock technique** — 15-20 cm vertebral-border incision; trapezius is subperiosteally released and retracted medially; supraspinatus and infraspinatus are divided near the vertebral border and stripped laterally about 1 cm; after scapular descent they are reinserted stepwise to the new medial position.

When marked descent is planned, brachial plexus compression from the congenitally short clavicle becomes a real hazard; Ombredanne recommends prophylactic clavicular osteotomy. The descended scapula is then fixed with wire to rib VII and the medial border to the ribs with wire and sutures; Redon drainage for 24-48 hours. After two months the wire is removed and rehabilitation begins.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Descent of a high-set scapula carries a real risk of brachial plexus compression at the cervico-thoracic outlet, because the clavicle is congenitally short and the descending scapula tugs the plexus over the first rib. Prophylactic clavicular osteotomy (Ombredanne) is indicated when planned descent exceeds approximately 2 cm or when preoperative paraesthesias are documented.</p>
</aside>

## Muscle Transpositions for Deltoid Paralysis

When shoulder abduction is impossible due to partial or complete deltoid paralysis (polio, permanent axillary nerve injury) a series of muscle transpositions reconstruct the abductor function. Prerequisites are a relatively preserved rotator cuff and satisfactory hand and wrist function. **Léo Mayer trapezius transposition** — Detachment of trapezius from the acromion; the muscle is mobilized medially and extended with a fascia lata strip whose distal end is fixed to the proximal brachialis at the humerus with moderate tension and 90° abduction. Immobilization in chest-arm cast at 90° abduction for 2 months. The effect in complete paralysis is more tenodesing than dynamic — functionally useful for fixing the limb in a working position. **Bateman trapezius transposition** — Osteoplastic detachment of trapezius from the acromion with resection of the lateral end of the clavicle, then mobilization and reinsertion of the osteoplastic fragment to the humerus, the bone-to-bone interface providing more stable union than Léo Mayer's soft-tissue extension. **Ober biceps-triceps transposition** — Saber-shaped incision over the acromion; the short head of biceps is osteoplastically detached from the coracoid and together with the long head of triceps (from the infraglenoid tubercle) is transposed proximally and fixed to the acromion at 90° abduction; chest-arm cast for 4 weeks. **Hildebrand pectoralis major transposition** — The muscle is detached not from its humeral insertion but from its costal origins (from rib IV along the sternal border); the incision curves along the upper border of the clavicle to the acromion. Pars sternocostalis and pars sternoclavicularis are mobilized with careful preservation of the anterior thoracic nerves and the feeding thoracoacromial and axillary branches. The muscle is then rotated 90° around its humeral insertion and fixed to the acromion and the lateral clavicle — delivering active abduction from a robust donor.

## Obstetric Brachial Plexus Palsy — Anterior Tenotomy and Transpositions

In obstetric palsy (Erb-Duchenne) with internal rotation contracture refractory to conservative care, the anterior tenotomy of Fairbank and Sever's modification release the internal-rotator yoke. Sever modified Fairbank's original technique by omitting the capsulotomy because of the risk of joint stiffening. The anterior tenotomy is often performed simultaneously with muscle transpositions when the contracture persists despite active treatment. **Operative technique** — Deltopectoral exposure with deltoid retraction; the pectoralis major tendon is divided in Z-fashion to lengthen it (the proximal portion remains attached to the humerus, reducing its adductor action). Coracobrachialis is retracted; subscapularis is divided transversely close to its insertion. Postoperative cast in external rotation and 90° abduction; after 2 weeks an abduction apparatus with active rehabilitation.

**Muscle transpositions in obstetric palsy** aim to restore the balance between preserved internal rotators and paralysed external rotators (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, posterior deltoid). Indicated above 2-4 years of age with preserved articular congruity. **Wickstrom technique** — Deltopectoral incision with cephalic vein retraction; pectoralis major and subscapularis tendons are lengthened; if needed anterior capsulotomy is added. With maximal external rotation the latissimus dorsi and teres major tendons are identified, divided close to bone and tagged. A second incision is made along the posterior border of deltoid curving over the axilla. The latissimus dorsi and teres major tendons are pulled through the proximal deltoid fibres and at 90° abduction with full external rotation are fixed in a pre-prepared bed over the lateral head of triceps. Immobilization at 70° abduction and full external rotation for 3 weeks. **Merle d'Aubigné method** — Conceptually similar with emphasis on simultaneous posterior capsulorrhaphy to prevent postoperative posterior subluxation.

## Long Head of Biceps Tenosynovitis and Rupture

For tenosynovitis with degeneration of the long head of biceps a tenodesis is performed. Deltopectoral exposure to the bicipital groove, which is opened along its entire length to the supraglenoid tubercle. The degenerated proximal stump is excised and the distal stump is fixed extra-articularly to the soft tissues around the groove. For traumatic ruptures a small anterior arm incision suffices to identify the distal stump. The elbow is splinted at 130° flexion for 25 days.

After failed conservative care for shoulder periarthritis with movement blockade (over 1 year of systematic conservative treatment, which is successful in 85-98% of cases), classical open techniques emphasize wide exposure and resection of impinging structures. **Debeyre approach** — Saber-shaped incision over the acromion; the periosteum is split longitudinally between the trapezius and deltoid attachments. The bone is subperiosteally exposed on both sides: deltoid is fully detached while a periosteal-tendinous flap is raised from the trapezius side. Using an osteotome, a portion of the acromion or the entire acromion plus the AC joint and lateral clavicle up to the coracoclavicular ligament is resected. After cuff repair the acromion is reconstructed by osteosynthesis or periosteal suture. The technique provides maximal visualization for massive retracted tears, irreducible cuff fragmentation, and removal of calcifications and ossifications in periarthritis. Where adequate resection without total acromionectomy is feasible, the approach preserves deltoid function. Supraspinatus repair classically uses temporary acromial osteotomy as described by Debeyre for wide exposure; the cuff is sutured into a bone-periosteal bed and the acromion is then re-osteosynthesised. Immobilization in chest-arm cast at 70° abduction and 20-30° antepulsion for 4 weeks.

## Resection and Reconstruction of the Proximal Humerus

Typical shoulder resection was formerly indicated for severe pyogenic or tuberculous arthritis — indications now largely obsolete. Modern indications include severely comminuted fractures with cuff avulsion when arthroplasty is not feasible, and tumour resection. The approach is posterior or combined; the joint surfaces are resected with a saw, preserving a "muscle cuff" around the bone for active function of the resected joint. **Muscle-plastic resection (Lawrence-Jones)** — Extended deltopectoral approach with deltoid detachment from clavicle, acromion and scapular spine. Three capsulo-muscular flaps are fashioned — anterior (with subscapularis, capsule between 1 and 3 o'clock), superior (with supraspinatus, 11-1 o'clock) and posterior (with infraspinatus and teres minor, 9-11 o'clock). The humeral head resection is economical; the flaps are tagged with strong sutures or wire and fixed into pre-cut longitudinal bone grooves. The technique preserves active function because the cuff is partly retained.

For tumour resection the work is supraperiosteal and en bloc with the involved musculature. Classical reconstructions are: 1) substitution with the proximal end of the ipsilateral fibula (Portugalov) fixed into the humeral medullary canal with 1-2 supplementary screws — the fibular head functioning as a new humeral head; 2) substitution with the lateral half of the clavicle preserving the AC joint (Moskov); 3) substitution with a frozen allograft (Imamaliev) cut step-wise and reinserted with muscle reattachment. After fibular arthroplasty: chest-arm cast at 70° abduction until bony union, then abduction splint. The complications of allograft replacement are significant — fracture, fragmentation and resorption — for which reason the modern standard is an individual endoprosthesis.

## Classical Shoulder Arthroplasty

**Neer hemiarthroplasty** — Described by C. S. Neer (1953). The Neer prosthesis is fashioned from vitallium, imitating the humeral head with proximal holes for cuff reinsertion. Deltopectoral approach; subscapularis and the long head of biceps tendons are tagged and released; the short head of biceps and coracobrachialis are osteoplastically detached from the coracoid. The humeral head is removed and the medullary canal is broached to fit the prosthetic stem. Three small cancellous grafts are placed in the triangular spaces between the humerus and the proximal stem for press-fit fixation. At head retroversion the stem is inserted halfway and then impacted to seating. Setting the head in neutral position (without retroversion) risks postoperative instability with anterior subluxation. Immobilization in chest-arm cast for 4 weeks in the "salute" position (abduction and forward flexion).

**Engelhardt ceramic-coated endoprosthesis (1975)** — A cementless design combining a metallic stem with a bio-inert ceramic articular surface and proximal conical zone. Primary fixation is achieved by plastic bone deformation and engagement of teeth on the inner conical surface; secondary fixation by bone in-growth into the coating micro-roughness and around the prosthesis ("buttress" effect). Indications are extensive humeral head destruction by aggressive benign giant cell tumour or low-grade malignancy requiring wide resection, and avascular necrosis after fracture. Postoperative olecranon traction at 3-4 kg with 60° abduction and 20° flexion for 3 weeks, then chest-arm cast in the same position for 2 months.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>Humeral component version at shoulder arthroplasty is critical for stability: lack of retroversion (head in neutral) predisposes to anterior subluxation, while excessive retroversion (>40°) predisposes to posterior instability. Neer classically recommends approximately 30-40° retroversion relative to the forearm, measured intraoperatively.</p>
</aside>

Chronic anterior instability after the first traumatic dislocation manifests as recurrent or habitual dislocations; the pathoanatomical substrate is most commonly Bankart (avulsion of labrum and periosteum from the anterior glenoid rim), often combined with a Hill-Sachs groove on the postero-lateral humeral head. Less frequently a habitual dislocation occurs from anterior capsular over-stretching without other pathology — a finding in congenital habitual dislocations, sometimes with anterior glenoid hypoplasia. Classical procedures aim at: 1) capsulorrhaphy/tenodesis to limit external rotation; 2) bone reconstruction/block for glenoid defect; 3) labrum-capsule reattachment to bone.

**Putti-Platt technique** — Deltopectoral incision curving proximally. Subscapularis and the anterior capsule are divided in parallel about 1 cm lateral to the glenoid rim; at maximal external rotation the capsular sutures are tied with overlap — the medial capsular leaflet reinforces the anterior wall. Subscapularis is then sutured with corresponding shortening. The outcome is unreliable with Bankart lesion avulsion or bony glenoid defect. **Magnuson-Stack technique** — Approximately 8 cm antero-medial incision through the most medial deltoid fibres (protecting the cephalic vein). At strong external rotation subscapularis is exposed; the conjoint muscles are retracted. Two parallel incisions are made along the superior and inferior subscapularis borders through muscle fibres and capsule. The tendon is osteoplastically detached and transposed laterally onto the greater tuberosity crest (from lesser to greater tuberosity). External rotation remains permanently limited by approximately 40-50%. An analogous method has been proposed in Bulgaria by G. Balchev. Velpeau immobilization for 3 weeks.

**Boychev procedure (Метод на Бойчев)** — The iconic Bulgarian operation for habitual anterior dislocation without significant anterior glenoid bone defect and without significant posterior humeral head impression. The patient is supine with a sandbag under the scapula. The incision begins at the junction of the middle and lateral thirds of the clavicle, follows the bone close to the AC joint and descends vertically. The deltopectoral groove is entered, with pectoralis major, the cephalic vein and the most anterior deltoid fibres retracted medially. In muscular patients the anterior deltoid is detached from the clavicle. The conjoint tendon (coracobrachialis and short head of biceps) is osteoplastically detached from the coracoid.

The essence of the procedure is that the conjoint bundle, rather than being simply reinserted, is **passed behind subscapularis** (between muscle and anterior capsule) and then returned and re-fixed to the coracoid in its new position. The transposed conjoint bundle then forms an active anterior "hammock" over the antero-inferior humeral head in external rotation and abduction — a dynamic functional check against anterior dislocation. The new route also passively shortens subscapularis, limiting external rotation. The hazards of the Boychev operation are musculocutaneous nerve injury with rough dissection and inadequate anatomic orientation (the nerve enters coracobrachialis 1-6 cm below its origin, at risk while routing the conjoint behind subscapularis), and recurrence in patients with concomitant anterior glenoid bone defect — for whom the procedure is not appropriate. Velpeau immobilization for 3 weeks with progressive mobilization.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>

**Bankart technique** — Deltopectoral incision. Osteoplastic detachment of the conjoint muscles. Subscapularis is divided transversely 1-1.5 cm from its insertion; care is needed not to enter the anterior capsule, which at this site is adherent to the tendon. At maximal external rotation the anterior glenoid rim is reached. The avulsed labrum with the antero-inferior capsule is reattached to the bony rim through holes in the bone with trans-osseous sutures. Curettage of the bony rim is critical for healing. With complete anatomic restoration external rotation is preserved — a substantial advantage over Putti-Platt and Magnuson-Stack. **Holevich-Paneva-Popova technique** — Bulgarian modification of the Bankart principle. Deltopectoral approach; conjoint detachment; subscapularis is divided transversely 1-1.5 cm from its insertion and retracted medially. At full external rotation the bony glenoid rim is curetted; small cortico-cancellous autograft "transplant slips" (4-5 mm high, about 1 cm long) with reverse-direction nicks to prevent dislodgement are driven into corresponding holes. A strong silk suture is tied to the base of each slip; the suture is then passed through the capsule and tied with overlap, achieving simultaneous bony reconstruction of the antero-inferior rim ("bouto") and capsulorrhaphy. Immobilization in a soft dressing at moderate abduction and internal rotation for 20-30 days. **Toit-Roux technique** — 8-10 cm deltopectoral incision. Conjoint retraction; subscapularis is split bluntly longitudinally to the glenoid rim; the capsule is divided along the same line. One to three metal staples are driven through capsule and scapular neck with intra- or extra-articular position under fluoroscopic control. Capsule sutured. A relatively sparing operation; the longitudinally split subscapularis is not sutured.

**Classical anterior bone block techniques** — Historical predecessors of Latarjet: **Eden** (cortico-cancellous tibial or iliac crest graft vertically wedged into the antero-inferior scapular neck); **Max Lange** (graft placed extracapsularly rather than intra-articular as in Eden, providing an external check against anterior head displacement); **Oudar** (coracoid elongation by oblique osteotomy with displacement of the distal fragment with attached conjoint muscles in front of subscapularis, fixed by 1-2 sutures to the proximal fragment — conceptual predecessor of modern Latarjet); **Judet** (free rib graft wedged into refreshed beds of the coracoid and antero-inferior scapular neck, fixed with a Kirschner wire crossing coracoid, rib and scapula).

## Posterior Recurrent Dislocation — Glenoplasties

Posterior habitual dislocations are rarer than anterior and may be congenital, idiopathic or due to external rotator paralysis. The classical procedures use posterior approaches and techniques analogous in principle to the anterior Bankart-Putti-Platt set. **Holevich-Paneva technique for posterior habitual dislocation** — Lateral position with draped limb. Posterior approach along the scapular spine curving distally around the acromion. Deltoid is detached from the scapular spine and retracted inferiorly, protecting the axillary nerve. After dividing the fascia over infraspinatus and teres minor, both muscles are tagged with a Terrier clamp; infraspinatus is divided 2 cm from its insertion (protecting the suprascapular nerve branch); the posterior capsule is divided 2 cm from the glenoid rim. The capsule is sutured with overlap (shortening), and infraspinatus is reinserted slightly more lateral as an active posterior tenodesis. Immobilization in neutral position or mild external rotation for 3 weeks. **Scott posterior glenoplasty** — Posterior approach with deltoid release and resection of the prominent posterior acromion preserved as a free graft for posterior glenoid augmentation. **Adams technique** — For congenital posterior habitual subluxation and the rare case of posterior traumatic habitual dislocation: aim is a posterior bone check plus capsulorrhaphy.

## Open Reduction of Fresh and Old Fracture-Dislocations

For irreducible cases (impression fracture of the antero-superior humeral head locked against the posterior glenoid rim — reverse Hill-Sachs in posterior dislocation) open reduction is performed. Standard posterior approach; internal humeral rotation progressively divides adhesions and exposes joint surfaces. The impression groove on the anterior head may be filled with a moulded iliac crest autograft fixed with 2-3 screws; alternatively subscapularis with capsule is reinserted to the new anterior rim per Bankart (with T-shaped allograft or cancellous autograft for defect). In children with open epiphyseal plates Kirschner wires are the preferred fixation. Immobilization at 70° abduction and neutral rotation for 45 days; with stable osteosynthesis Müller recommends early active exercises.

## Shoulder Arthrodesis

Arthrodesis is indicated for severe conditions where arthroplasty is unfeasible: deltoid paralysis with intact trapezius and serratus anterior, severe destruction from infection (tuberculosis, pyogenic arthritis), old traumatic injuries, and failed previous procedures. The optimal position for arthrodesis is 45° abduction, 45° external rotation (relative to the horizontal forearm) and 45° antepulsion. Müller recommends intra-operative measurement of the angle between the lateral border of the scapula and the humerus (approximately 75° corresponds to 45° from the trunk). The classical techniques fall into three groups: extra-articular, intra-articular and combined.

**Extra-articular arthrodesis** — The Albee technique uses a compact tibial graft driven through the acromion into the humeral head without opening the joint. Watson-Jones refreshes the acromion and drives it into a slot beneath the cortical humerus. More biomechanically sound are the inferior extra-articular arthrodeses: a graft bridging the lateral scapular border to the humerus is loaded in compression by limb weight — the material may be the medial border of the scapula (Vreden, Putti) or a compact tibial graft (Brittain/Britton). **Brittain technique** — Prone with hanging arm; incision 2-3 cm proximal to the scapular angle continuing along the posterior deltoid border. A tibial graft (arrow-shaped with bifid base) is wedged between scapula and humerus; prolonged cast immobilization is a significant drawback. **Intra-articular arthrodesis** — Lexer's historical method uses an antero-lateral approach with deltoid detachment; the joint surfaces are refreshed and fixed. Matz advocates supplementary metal osteosynthesis and compression. **Müller compression arthrodesis** is the modern standard in this group: incision along the scapular spine extending distally along the humeral midline; the humeral head and glenoid surfaces are refreshed; primary fixation with 2 Kirschner wires (one horizontally through humeral metaphysis into scapular neck, one obliquely through humeral head to medial scapular border); fluoroscopy; one compression screw along each K-wire track; supplementary compression plate with 2 short screws through scapular spine and 2 cancellous screws through humeral head into scapular neck, delivering additional compression. Cast immobilization for 2-3 months until union.

**Combined arthrodesis** — **Merle d'Aubigné** uses a pedicled graft of the scapular spine with the attached posterior acromion, displaced inferiorly to bridge the humeral-glenoid articulation, pedicled on trapezius which provides vascularity. **Gill** uses a postero-lateral incision; the acromion is exposed from both sides, the joint is opened and surfaces refreshed, and the denuded acromion is wedged into a slot in the humeral head, providing simultaneous bone compression and intra-articular fusion. **Holevich-Paneva** uses a saber incision around the lateral scapula, acromion and lateral third of clavicle; deltoid is subperiosteally detached from the acromion; the joint capsule is widely opened; surfaces are refreshed; the space between humeral head and inferior acromial surface is filled with iliac crest autograft; temporary K-wire fixation is later converted to screws. **Compression external-fixator arthrodesis** — External fixators (Charnley, Ilizarov) opened the possibility of compression without prolonged cast immobilization. The Ilizarov compression arthrodesis is approached anteriorly with extension posteriorly; the joint surfaces and inferior acromial surface are refreshed. The external fixator consists of 3 pins in humeral head/shaft, fixed with nuts; after fluoroscopic check the temporary K-wires are removed and compression is delivered by tightening the nuts. Abduction angle is fine-tuned by selectively tightening upper or lower nuts. After 4 weeks the external fixator is removed; the cast is maintained for another 30 days.

## Scapulectomy and Tumour Resections

For malignant scapular tumours with an intact humerus the classical Tikhoff-Linberg procedure is total or subtotal scapulectomy with interscapulothoracic resection sparing the limb. Smaller partial resections of the scapula (acromion, body, coracoid) are indicated for post-traumatic states, osteomyelitis, tuberculosis, exostoses and low-grade tumours; resection of the inferior angle may also be a stage of thoracoplasty. The approach is Kocher's (slightly curved incision along the vertebral border). For resection of the superior portion the suprascapular nerve and artery must be exposed and protected. Redon drainage for 24-48 hours is mandatory. When the pathological lesion does not allow limb preservation, interscapulothoracic disarticulation (Berger) is performed; when feasible a high humeral amputation with resection of the upper third (preserving scapula and clavicle for the cosmetic shoulder silhouette) is preferred. For osteosynthesis of acromial process fractures (typically transverse — to be differentiated from a persistent apophysis, os acromiale, occurring in about 15% of the population) a malleolar screw or a Weber tension band is used.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

The shoulder offers a substantial set of surgical approaches reflecting its mobility, complex muscular envelope, and proximity to neurovascular structures. The deltopectoral approach is the workhorse for the anterior shoulder, using the avascular plane between the deltoid (axillary nerve) and the pectoralis (pectoral nerves), with the cephalic vein as the surface landmark; the musculocutaneous nerve entering coracobrachialis approximately 5 to 8 cm distal to the coracoid limits medial retraction; the subscapularis management (vertical incision, lesser tuberosity osteotomy, or peel) is a key surgical decision in many anterior procedures. The posterior approach between infraspinatus and teres minor provides access to the posterior glenoid and capsule, with the axillary nerve running below the deltoid limiting deltoid split to approximately 5 cm distal to the acromion. The anterior axillary approach is a cosmetic alternative to deltopectoral. The superior (saber-cut) approach with deltoid-splitting incision provides access to the superior rotator cuff and AC joint. The rotator cuff comprises four muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) functioning principally as dynamic stabilizers of the humeral head in the glenoid; the deltoid is the principal abductor with three heads (anterior, middle, posterior). The axillary nerve in the quadrilateral space is the critical neurological structure at risk in shoulder surgery, supplying the deltoid and teres minor and the lateral skin of the upper arm. The musculocutaneous nerve entering coracobrachialis and the suprascapular nerve in the suprascapular and spinoglenoid notches are additional neurological structures requiring protection. The long thoracic nerve along the chest wall surface of serratus anterior is at risk in lateral chest wall procedures and produces scapular winging when injured. Shoulder arthroscopy has supplanted many open procedures and uses standard posterior, anterior, anterolateral, lateral, and accessory portals to access the glenohumeral joint and subacromial space.

From the classical Bulgarian and Russian schools of shoulder surgery the following should be remembered: the topographic concept of the clavipectoral, pectoral and subpectoral triangles with the neurovascular bundle 2 cm anterior to subscapularis; the wide scapular approaches (L-shaped, T-shaped, Kocher) and Hadzhistamov's posterior trans-spinal approach with scapular-spine osteotomy for classical cuff reconstructions; the AC dislocation procedures (Bosworth screw, AO-Weber tension band, Heyzlauer, Dewar-Barrington with dynamic clavicular depression by coracoid transfer, Bunnell fascial reconstruction, Rowe oblique distal-clavicle resection); the four Sprengel groups (omovertebral excision, Green with traction osteosynthesis, Woodward with origin relocation of trapezius/rhomboids, Schrock, and the prophylactic Ombredanne clavicular osteotomy for plexus protection); the deltoid-paralysis transpositions (Léo Mayer and Bateman trapezius transfers, Ober biceps-triceps, Hildebrand pectoralis major) and obstetric palsy reconstructions (Fairbank-Sever anterior tenotomy, Wickstrom, Merle d'Aubigné); Debeyre's wide approach with temporary acromial osteotomy for massive cuff tears; Lawrence-Jones muscle-plastic resection and the proximal humeral reconstructions with fibula (Portugalov), lateral clavicle (Moskov) or allograft (Imamaliev); Neer and Engelhardt arthroplasties; and especially the iconic Bulgarian recurrent-anterior-dislocation procedures — the Boychev technique (routing the conjoint bundle behind subscapularis for active anterior stabilization) and the Holevich-Paneva-Popova technique (anterior-rim bone-slip "transplants" with simultaneous capsulorrhaphy), alongside the classical Putti-Platt, Magnuson-Stack/Balchev, Bankart, Toit-Roux, and the historical bone blocks (Eden, Max Lange, Oudar as Latarjet predecessor, Judet); Holevich-Paneva for posterior habitual dislocation; and the full spectrum of arthrodesis techniques — extra-articular (Albee, Watson-Jones, Brittain, Vreden-Putti), intra-articular compression (Müller with paired screws and plate), and combined (Merle d'Aubigné with pedicled spinoacromial graft, Gill, Holevich-Paneva), including compression arthrodesis with Charnley and Ilizarov external fixators. This classical foundation remains relevant for understanding the evolution of shoulder surgery and for routinely solving revision and non-standard clinical scenarios. The chapter that follows turns to the arm and humerus, continuing the systematic regional anatomy through the upper extremity.
