*Anatomia chirurgica humeri et scapulae · Aditus operatorii ad articulationem humeri*

## I. Conspectus · Overview

The shoulder girdle offers the greatest range of motion of any joint in the body — a compromise reached through a radical sacrifice of bony stability in favour of soft-tissue balance. The glenohumeral articular surface of the humerus is roughly three times the area of the glenoid, the contact patch at any instant covers only 25–30% of the head, and only the coordinated action of the rotator cuff, the labrum, the capsule, and the periscapular muscles allows stability and mobility to coexist.

The surgical anatomy of the shoulder is organised around three practical rules that govern approach selection: *(1)* anterior field — the `axillary nerve` limits any deltoid-splitting incision to 5 cm below the acromion, and the `musculocutaneous nerve` limits medial retraction of the conjoint bundle to 5–8 cm below the coracoid; *(2)* posterior field — the spinoglenoid notch contains the `suprascapular nerve` on its way to infraspinatus; *(3)* medial and deep — the entire neurovascular bundle of the upper limb crosses 2 cm anterior to `subscapularis` within the pectoral triangle.

This chapter systematises shoulder surgical anatomy in six anatomical layers (II), the biomechanical consequences that flow from them (III), the clinical examination and imaging that drive approach selection (IV), and the classifications that determine operative tactics (V), and then proceeds through the full register of operative approaches and classical procedures — from the workhorse deltopectoral to the Hadzhistamov trans-spinal, from Bankart–Putti-Platt–Magnuson-Stack–Balchev to Boychev, and from Neer arthroplasty to the full spectrum of arthrodesis techniques (VI). Complications (VII) are described systematically through the anatomical substrate. Section VIII gathers the Bulgarian contributions — Boychev, Holevich–Paneva–Popova, Hadzhistamov, Balchev, Moskov, Imamaliev, Portugalov — which, together with the topographic concept of the clavipectoral / pectoral / subpectoral triangles, remain the didactic backbone of the Bulgarian orthopaedic curriculum. NB and Cave (IX, X) consolidate fifteen clinical rules and fifteen pitfalls. The classical sources and contemporary literature close the chapter (XI, XII).

### A. Bones and Joints · *Ossium et articulationum*

The glenohumeral joint is a ball-and-socket articulation between the relatively small, slightly retroverted glenoid (retroversion 5–7°, inferior tilt 4–5°) and the substantially larger humeral head, covered by approximately 120° of articular cartilage. The size mismatch produces the characteristic mobility–stability trade-off — only 25–30% of the head is in contact with the glenoid at any instant. The glenoid labrum is a fibrocartilaginous rim that deepens the socket by ~50% and provides attachment for the capsular ligaments and the long head of biceps tendon.

The acromion forms the lateral roof of the joint; its morphological variants — flat (Bigliani I), curved (II), and hooked (III) — carry direct surgical significance, especially type III, associated with rotator cuff impingement. *Os acromiale* (1–8% of the population, mesoacromion most common) is a failure of fusion of the acromial ossification centres and a recognised substrate for impingement. The coracoid process projects anteriorly and provides attachment for the conjoint tendon (short head of biceps and *m. coracobrachialis*), the *lig. coracoacromiale*, the coracoclavicular ligaments (conoid and trapezoid) and the *lig. coracohumerale*; the coracoid is the bone-graft source for the Latarjet procedure (see Trauma-12). The clavicle completes the shoulder girdle, articulating with the acromion laterally (AC joint) and the sternum medially (SC joint); its anatomy and pathology are covered in Trauma-11.

The glenohumeral capsule is generous (synovial volume twice that required for bony contact), which explains both its capacity for an enormous arc of motion and its dependence on three fibrous condensations — superior, middle, and inferior glenohumeral ligaments. The middle glenohumeral ligament (MGHL) is the primary anterior restraint in mid-abduction with external rotation — the pathognomonic position of the apprehension test and the operative substrate of the classical Putti-Platt capsulorrhaphy. The inferior glenohumeral ligament complex (IGHL) with its anterior and posterior bands is the principal restraint at full abduction; avulsion of its anterior band from the glenoid rim is the Bankart lesion. The rotator interval — a triangular field between the supraspinatus tendon and the superior border of subscapularis — contains the coracohumeral and superior glenohumeral ligaments and is the route for arthroscopic establishment of the anterior portal.

### C. Infraclavicular and Axillary Topography · *Topographia infraclavicularis et axillaris*

The classical Bulgarian anatomical school describes the infraclavicular region (regio infraclavicularis), bounded by the inferior clavicular border, the lateral edge of the sternum, the third rib, and the anterior border of the deltoid, as the key topographic zone for anterior shoulder approaches. The skin is thin and mobile; the subcutaneous tissue contains branches of the anterior supraclavicular nerves and sensory rami of the intercostal nerves. Beneath the superficial fascia lies *pectoralis major* with its own fascia; between it and the deltoid forms the deltopectoral sulcus, expanding proximally into the deltopectoral triangle — a fatty-lymphatic space in which the cephalic vein drains into the axillary vein at the level of the superior border of *pectoralis minor*. The infraclavicular space is divided into three triangles — *trigonum clavipectorale* (between the clavicle and the upper border of pectoralis minor), *trigonum pectorale* (behind pectoralis minor), and *trigonum subpectorale* (below its inferior border). Within the clavipectoral triangle the clavipectoral fascia is pierced by branches of *a. thoracoacromialis* (pectoral, deltoid, acromial and clavicular rami), which must be identified and controlled in extended deltopectoral exposures.

In the pectoral triangle the neurovascular bundle lies between *pectoralis minor* (anterior), *subclavius* (posterior), coracoid (lateral), and *serratus anterior* (medial); the bundle runs about 2 cm anterior to *subscapularis*. Laterally, the musculocutaneous nerve leaves the lateral cord at the level of the inferior border of pectoralis minor and adheres to the axillary artery before entering coracobrachialis; the median nerve forms from its two roots 3–5 cm below the inferior border of pectoralis minor; the ulnar nerve separates 3.5–4 cm above the latissimus dorsi tendon and runs on the inner side of the artery. This arrangement is critical in extended anterior exposures to the humeral neck and in dissections toward the brachial plexus — the convergence of the entire neurovascular structure between coracoid and the axillary fold explains why medial dissection beyond the short head of biceps is not recommended.

**Rotator cuff** — four muscles forming a cuff around the humeral head and functioning principally as dynamic compressors of the head into the glenoid. *Supraspinatus* (supraspinous fossa → superior facet of greater tuberosity) carries the initial abduction to 30° and works with the deltoid through the full arc; the most frequently torn cuff muscle. *Infraspinatus* (infraspinous fossa → middle facet) — main external rotator. *Teres minor* (lateral border of the scapula → inferior facet) — assists external rotation. *Subscapularis* (subscapular fossa → lesser tuberosity) — main internal rotator and anterior dynamic stabiliser.

**Deltoid.** *Musculus deltoideus* is the principal abductor beyond the initial supraspinatus range, with three heads: anterior (clavicular) — flexion and internal rotation; middle (acromial) — abduction; posterior (spinal) — extension and external rotation. It inserts on the deltoid tuberosity. Its innervation by the axillary nerve, which wraps the surgical neck 5–7 cm distal to the acromion on the deep surface of the muscle, limits every deltoid-splitting incision.

This limits every deltoid-splitting incision to about 5 cm distal to the acromion.</p>

**Periscapular muscles.** *Trapezius* (occiput and spinous processes → scapular spine, acromion, clavicle) — elevation, retraction, and upward rotation of the scapula. *Rhomboid major and minor* — retraction and downward rotation. *Serratus anterior* (ribs → medial border of the scapula anteriorly) — protraction and upward rotation; scapular winging from injury to the long thoracic nerve. *Levator scapulae* — elevation. *Latissimus dorsi* and *teres major* — internal rotators and adductors, together forming the posterior axillary fold.

**The conjoint bundle** — *coracobrachialis* together with the short head of biceps — peels off the coracoid and forms the working landmark in anterior exposures; medial retraction of this bundle uncovers subscapularis. The musculocutaneous nerve enters coracobrachialis 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid and defines the "safe zone" for retraction.

### E. Nerves · *Nervi*

**Axillary nerve (C5–C6).** Branches from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus, passes posteriorly through the quadrilateral space, and wraps the surgical neck of the humerus deep to the deltoid; branches to deltoid and teres minor; its sensory branch (*n. cutaneus brachii lateralis superior*) supplies the skin over the lateral deltoid. Its proximity to the surgical neck (5–7 cm distal to the acromion) is the critical surgical-anatomical fact limiting the reach of deltoid-splitting incisions. Injured in approximately 10–20% of anterior shoulder dislocations and in many proximal humerus fractures (Trauma-13).

</aside>

**Musculocutaneous nerve (C5–C7).** Branches from the lateral cord, pierces *coracobrachialis* 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid, and supplies biceps brachii, brachialis, and coracobrachialis before continuing as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The musculocutaneous nerve pierces coracobrachialis approximately 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid; vigorous retraction of the conjoint tendon beyond about 5 cm distal to the coracoid risks neuropraxia. The "safe zone" is the proximal 5 cm below the coracoid tip.</p>
</aside>

**Suprascapular nerve (C5–C6).** Passes through the suprascapular notch (beneath the superior transverse scapular ligament), supplies supraspinatus, continues round the spinoglenoid notch, and supplies infraspinatus. Compression or traction at either site produces neuropathy with weakness of supraspinatus and/or infraspinatus — an additional risk in deep posterior glenoid dissection and in wide scapular approaches.

**Long thoracic nerve (C5–C7).** Runs along the lateral chest wall on the surface of *serratus anterior*; injury produces scapular winging with medial-border prominence on forward elevation.

**Brachial plexus.** Roots C5–T1, trunks (superior, middle, inferior), divisions (anterior and posterior), cords (lateral, medial, posterior) and terminal branches; lies medial to the coracoid and behind *pectoralis minor*, together with the axillary vessels. These structures are at risk in deep dissection medial to the coracoid and in high-energy dislocations.

### F. Vessels · *Vasa*

The axillary artery and vein cross the axilla as continuations of the subclavian vessels below the clavicle and become the brachial vessels distal to the inferior border of teres major. Of their six branches, three carry direct surgical significance: *a. thoracoacromialis* (with pectoral, deltoid, acromial and clavicular rami piercing the clavipectoral fascia in the clavipectoral triangle); the *anterior humeral circumflex artery* — one of the "three sisters" along the inferior border of subscapularis, requiring ligation prior to detachment; and the *posterior humeral circumflex artery*, which accompanies the axillary nerve through the quadrilateral space and anastomoses with its anterior counterpart. The humeral head is supplied principally by the ascending branch of the anterior humeral circumflex (Laing's arcuate artery); its disruption explains the high rate of AVN in displaced surgical-neck fractures.

## III. Pathophysiologia · Pathophysiology

**Biomechanics of stability.** The glenohumeral joint exists in a continuous dynamic equilibrium between four stabilising mechanisms. The *static* set includes articular congruity (minimal because of the caput/glenoid mismatch), cohesive-adhesive forces within the synovial space, negative intra-capsular pressure at rest, and the capsuloligamentous complex (MGHL for mid-abduction in external rotation, IGHL for full abduction). The *dynamic* set — the principal stabilising mechanism during motion — relies on the concavity-compression principle: the rotator cuff compresses the head into the glenoid perpendicular to the articular surface while the deltoid generates the tangential force for motion; effectiveness depends on preserved glenoid depth (labrum!) and an intact cuff.

**Scapulohumeral rhythm.** Full 180° abduction is the product of ~120° glenohumeral motion plus ~60° scapulothoracic rotation (a 2:1 ratio after the first 30° of pure glenohumeral motion). The periscapular muscles (upper and lower trapezius, serratus anterior) are the "forgotten muscles" — scapular winging, serratus dysfunction or AC pain immediately disrupt the rhythm and produce pseudo-impingement.

**Pathomechanical themes that operative surgery addresses.** *(1)* Anterior instability — traumatic first dislocation in patients under 25 carries a high recurrence rate because of a Bankart-equivalent substrate (detached labrum + capsular plastic deformation) ± Hill-Sachs impression and/or bony defect of the antero-inferior glenoid rim; the basis for capsulolabral reconstruction (Bankart, Boychev, Putti-Platt) or bony augmentation (Latarjet, Eden, Lange, Oudar, Judet). *(2)* Impingement and cuff degeneration — between the greater tuberosity and the coracoacromial arch, exacerbated by Bigliani III, os acromiale and calcifications; the basis for acromioplasty, bursectomy and Debeyre-style wide exposure for retracted tears. *(3)* AC separation — sequential disruption of the AC capsule, then conoid and trapezoid ligaments in a high-energy adduction injury; the Rockwood I–VI classification dictates operative choice (Bosworth, Heyzlauer, Dewar-Barrington, Weaver-Dunn). *(4)* Brachial plexus traction — Erb-Duchenne in birth or in high-energy adult shoulder dislocation. *(5)* Thoracic outlet involvement of the brachial bundle — TOS from a cervical rib, a congenitally short clavicle (Sprengel + Ombredanne), or after over-correction.

**Avascular necrosis.** The ascending branch of the anterior humeral circumflex (arcuate artery) is the end-vessel for the humeral head; its disruption in a displaced surgical-neck fracture (especially the 4-part Neer pattern) precisely cuts off the blood supply and explains the high rate of AVN — one of the indications for hemiarthroplasty or reverse arthroplasty in the elderly.

## IV. Praesentatio et diagnosis · Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

**The surgically-relevant clinical examination** is organised around the structure the approach will engage. *Deltoid and axillary nerve*: sensory testing over the lateral deltoid and a test of isometric abduction after every anterior dislocation and proximal humerus fracture. *Rotator cuff*: Jobe (empty can) for supraspinatus; external rotation in neutral and the Hornblower sign for infraspinatus/teres minor; lift-off (Gerber) and belly-press for subscapularis; Hawkins-Kennedy and Neer for impingement. *Labrum and instability*: apprehension in abduction–external rotation plus relocation (Jobe), load-and-shift in neutral, sulcus sign for inferior laxity, O'Brien and dynamic labral shear for SLAP pathology. *AC joint*: palpation tenderness, cross-body adduction, O'Brien with stabilised clavicle. *SC joint*: palpation, anterior or posterior step on supination. *Brachial plexus*: motor-sensory C5–T1 screen, Tinel and compression tests, Roos and Adson for TOS.

**Imaging that drives approach selection.** A standard AP in the scapular plane plus a transaxillary or Velpeau-axillary view excludes posterior dislocation (a 50% miss rate is reported on standard AP alone). The *true AP* and *outlet (Y)* views show Bigliani acromial morphology and define acromioplasty indications. The *Zanca view* (10–15° cephalic tilt) shows the AC joint without the overlying humerus. The *Stryker notch view* demonstrates Hill-Sachs impressions. The *Garth view* (45° apical oblique) shows the antero-inferior glenoid rim in bone-defect cases. CT with 3D reconstruction is the standard for glenoid bone loss (precise Latarjet vs. Bankart planning), for surgical-neck fracture planning, and for identification of osteolytic tumours. MR-arthrography or high-field MRI shows labrum, cuff, biceps and capsule with high sensitivity. Ultrasound in trained hands is a dynamic and effective assessment of cuff tears and biceps long-head pathology.

## V. Classificationes · Classifications

**Bigliani — acromial morphology (outlet view):** I flat, II curved, III hooked. Type III correlates with impingement and full-thickness supraspinatus tears.

**Os acromiale** (1–8% of the population): pre-acromion (tip segment), meso-acromion (middle; most common), meta-acromion (posterolateral), basi-acromion. The mesoacromion is the substrate for impingement and for failure of classical acromioplasty.

**Rockwood — AC separation:** I (AC capsular distortion only, undisplaced), II (AC rupture, CC partial, <25% displacement), III (AC + CC complete, 25–100% displacement), IV (posterior clavicular displacement through the trapezius), V (severe superior-inferior displacement, 100–300%), VI (rare inferior dislocation under the coracoid). Type I–II — conservative; III — individualised; IV–VI — operative.

**Neer — proximal humerus fractures** (4-part anatomical principle): a fragment is defined by >1 cm displacement or >45° angulation. 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-part fractures; 4-part fractures carry the highest risk of AVN. *See Trauma-13.*

**Capsulolabral lesions in anterior instability:** *Bankart* — avulsion of the capsulolabral complex from the bony glenoid rim; *bony Bankart* — with a bone fragment; *ALPSA* (Neviaser) — medial avulsion and rolling of the labrum-periosteum-capsule sleeve along the anterior scapular neck with pseudo-healing and chronic deficiency; *HAGL* — humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament; *GLAD* — glenolabral articular disruption (chondral avulsion with intact labrum); *Perthes* — a non-displaced Bankart with intact periosteum.

**Hill-Sachs (Itoi):** the "glenoid track" concept separates posterolateral impressions into *on-track* (engaging the glenoid track in the ABER position; safe for pure labral repair) and *off-track* (extending beyond the track in ABER; require remplissage or bony augmentation). The off-track criterion is especially important with bony glenoid loss >25% (inverted-pear glenoid) — an indication for Latarjet.

**Snyder — SLAP (superior labrum anterior to posterior):** I — frayed, degenerative; II — avulsion of the biceps-labral anchor; III — bucket-handle with intact biceps; IV — bucket-handle with the tear extending into the biceps. Types V–X (added later) combine SLAP with Bankart extension etc.

**Cavendish — Sprengel:** I very mild (invisible beneath clothing), II mild (visible asymmetry on motion), III moderate (visible anterior groove with ~2 cm elevation), IV severe (>5 cm elevation, restricted motion). The Hamner-Capen radiographic classification is an alternative.

**Jonasch — clavicular pseudarthrosis** (congenital and traumatic): ranked by defect length and location, guiding the choice between a tibial cortico-periosteal interposition (small), a fibular segment (medium), or an iliac-crest or rib graft (large).

## VI. Therapia · Treatment

### A. Conservative Treatment · *Conservativa*

Velpeau-style immobilisation (3 weeks for first traumatic dislocations in patients under 25; individualised in older patients — a balance between recurrence and stiffness), physiotherapy with a focus on scapular setting and dynamic cuff stabilisation, infiltrations (subacromial for impingement, glenohumeral for adhesive capsulitis, AC for arthropathy). Hydrodilatation is a valid adjunct in adhesive capsulitis. Arthrodesis or arthroplasty when 6–12 months of systematic conservative treatment have failed.

### B. Operative Treatment · *Operativa*

**1. Deltopectoral approach.** The workhorse for the anterior shoulder, used for open reduction, proximal humerus fixation, anterior glenoid procedures (Bankart, Latarjet), arthroplasty, and tumour resection. Patient in beach-chair at 45–60°. Incision along the deltopectoral groove, beginning below the coracoid and running 10–15 cm distally. The cephalic vein marks the interval — usually retracted laterally with the deltoid (safer for vein preservation). The clavipectoral fascia is divided lateral to the conjoint tendon. The conjoint bundle is retracted medially, watching the musculocutaneous nerve (5–8 cm distal to the coracoid). The "three sisters" along the inferior border of subscapularis are ligated. Subscapularis is detached — vertical tenotomy, lesser-tuberosity osteotomy, or slipping. The extended deltopectoral lengthens the incision proximally and distally; the minimally invasive variant does the reverse.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>In the deltopectoral approach the anterior humeral circumflex vessels ("three sisters") along the inferior border of subscapularis must be controlled before subscapularis detachment — uncontrolled bleeding obscures the field. Post-operative subscapularis dysfunction is a recurrent concern; lesser-tuberosity osteotomy or peel with a bone block may give better results than the historical vertical tenotomy.</p>
</aside>

**2. Posterior shoulder approach.** For posterior glenoid procedures, posterior instability, posterior dislocations, and scapular fixation. Patient lateral or prone. Incision from the posterolateral corner of the acromion along the lateral border of the deltoid, 6–10 cm. The deltoid is split between the middle and posterior heads, limited to 5 cm distal to the acromion (axillary nerve!). The infraspinatus/teres-minor interval is developed; the posterior capsule is exposed. Alternative variants include splitting infraspinatus along its fibres or detaching it from the greater tuberosity with reinsertion.

**3. Anterior axillary approach (Henry).** A cosmetic alternative to the deltopectoral, incision in the anterior axillary fold below the coracoid; the dissection then turns up to the deltopectoral groove along the standard route. Less visible scar, but more limited proximal exposure.

**4. Superior (saber-cut) approach and classical transacromial variants.** Superior incision over the AC joint or greater tuberosity, 4–6 cm. The deltoid is split along its fibres, ≤5 cm distal to the acromion. Exposes the supraspinatus tendon and AC zone. The Bulgarian school describes two classical transacromial variants, didactically important for understanding the evolution of superior approaches. The *economical transacromial* variant performs a partial acromial osteotomy along a pre-drawn line (frontal or lateral), with a hole pre-drilled beneath the acromion to protect the cuff from the chisel; a small AC-zone deltoid origin is detached; reconstruction with wire or a small screw. The *saber-cut* is the extended form with full release of the acromion and the laterally directed portion of the clavicle lateral to the coracoclavicular ligament; the AC complex is reconstructed by osteosynthesis. Both give complete visualisation of the rotator cuff in massive retracted tears, in classical operations for calcified periarthritis, and in selected complex anterior dislocations with avulsion of the greater tuberosity.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>In a deltoid-splitting approach the separation between the deltoid fibres must not exceed 3–4 cm from the muscle origin — branches of the axillary nerve to pars acromialis enter at the midpoint between the lateral acromial edge and the deltoid tuberosity. A split longer than 5 cm is acceptable only in a paralysed deltoid. The safest deltoid-splitting routes are along the natural borders between pars clavicularis and pars acromialis, and between pars acromialis and pars spinalis.</p>
</aside>

**5. Wide scapular approaches.** *L-shaped approach* — vertical component along the vertebral border + horizontal component 2 cm above the superior angle; patient prone; the deep branch of the transverse cervical artery (0.5–1 cm medial to the border) is ligated; identification of the suprascapular notch (with omohyoid as landmark) protects the suprascapular nerve. *T-shaped (the Hejian)* — combines the vertical incision with an oblique arm along the scapular spine to the AC joint; provides the widest field for subtotal scapulectomy and release of the lateral angle with the glenoid. *Posterior trans-spinal approach of Hadzhistamov* — osteotomy of the scapular spine across its middle (without disrupting the AC joint); the bone fragment is hinged down like a page, exposing the upper cuff, the greater tuberosity facet, the long head of biceps, and the posterior segment of the capsule; osteosynthesis with wire or screw. A conceptual precursor of modern transacromial and "cobra" approaches.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Hadzhistamov's trans-spinal approach is the conceptual precursor of modern transacromial and superior "cobra" exposures: a scapular-spine osteotomy (without the AC joint) from a posterior skin incision delivers a wide superior view of the rotator cuff without disturbing deltoid function. Re-osteosynthesis of the osteotomised fragment is critical for postoperative stability.</p>
</aside>

**6. Clavicular approaches and operations.** Standard longitudinal, slightly curved incision, not directly over the bone (reduces scar tension at the muscular insertions); an S-shaped incision for simultaneous supra- and infra-clavicular access. The external jugular vein lies 0.5–1 cm lateral to the clavicular head of sternocleidomastoid; beneath the subclavius — the subclavian vessels; the brachial plexus and subclavian vessels cross the clavicle posteriorly an average of 2 cm lateral to the SCM insertion; the suprascapular artery runs along the posterior surface from the midshaft to the acromial end. *SC-joint approach* — skin incision over the medial quarter, with the jugular notch and the sternal clavicular end as landmarks. *AC-joint approach* — curved incision over the lateral quarter plus the anterosuperior acromial border. *Osteosynthesis of midshaft fractures* — K-wire (paediatric/simple transverse), tension-band wire (cerclage risk of breakage up to week 4), plate (adult standard). *Lateral end resection* — 1–1.5 cm in chronic post-traumatic AC pathology. *Disarticulating resection* — operations on vessels/plexus, bone tumours, bone-joint tuberculosis, osteomyelitis; subperiosteal work in benign disease, including periosteum and surrounding muscle in malignant. *Total cleidectomy* — destructive changes of the entire clavicle; the periosteal bed is filled with a fibrin sponge to prevent haematoma; segment-by-segment osteotomy and removal reduces the risk of vascular injury. *Pseudarthrosis (Jonasch)* — preserve length, use osteoplastic material (tibia with periosteum, fibular segment, iliac crest, rib), achieve stable osteosynthesis.

**7. AC and SC instability — operative treatment.** Over 30 techniques have been described for acute AC dislocations; the classical variants include: *Bosworth* — vertical channel through the clavicle above the coracoid with a clavicle-to-coracoid screw + percutaneous K-wire across the AC (removed at 3–4 weeks). *AO (Weber)* — cancellous screw + Weber tension band; CC ligaments reconstructed with half the coracobrachialis tendon. *Heyzlauer* — transposition of the CC ligament to form a new AC ligament; avoids any rigid fixation that would limit clavicular rotation. *Dewar-Barrington* — osteotomy of the coracoid tip with the conjoint muscles and fixation to the clavicle by screw — dynamic depression of the clavicle through the pulling muscles; preserving 1 cm and pre-drilling are technical critical points. *Bunnell* — passive fascial stabilisation with a fascia-lata strip through bone tunnels using Deschamps' needle; a temporary K-wire across the AC. *Rowe* — longitudinal incision + oblique distal-clavicle osteotomy + ligament repair in overlap. *Weaver-Dunn* — distal clavicle resection + transposition of the coracoacromial ligament (the modern historical standard). *SC dislocation (anterior or posterior)* — transverse incision; *posterior* dislocations may compress the trachea and great vessels and demand emergency treatment (Beckman 1923 bilateral, ~41 documented cases); preoperative tomography is essential (Kennedy 1949, Holmdahl 1954). A K-wire through the joint carries a recognised risk of intrathoracic migration and is discouraged by most authors; *Lowman* — a fascial graft through two central tunnels. SC-end resection alone is not recommended; reconstruction of the interclavicular ligament and fixation of the intra-articular disc are critical.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>A percutaneous K-wire through the sternoclavicular joint or into the manubrium carries a real risk of intrathoracic migration (including into the pericardium and great vessels) — fatal cases are recorded. Bending the two ends over the skin, early removal (3–4 weeks), and reconstructing the interclavicular ligament with a fascial strip are mandatory precautions.</p>
</aside>

**8. Congenital high-riding scapula (Sprengel).** Goals: excise the omovertebral link, lower the scapula, correct the functional and cosmetic deficit. Optimal age >10 years. Four operative groups: omovertebral excision, excision of the supero-medial scapula, subtotal scapulectomy, and translocation.

*Omovertebral excision* — patient prone; incision along the C1–T9 spinous processes; the rhomboids are divided; the omovertebral bony bridge is resected, freeing the superior angle. *Excision of the supero-medial portion* — transverse incision along the scapular spine; osteoplastic detachment of trapezius; excision of the supero-medial angle scaled to the prominence. *Green* — incision along the vertebral border; trapezius is carefully detached from the scapular spine (preserved for reinsertion); supraspinatus and infraspinatus are stripped strictly subperiosteally with attention to the suprascapular nerve and transverse scapular artery; serratus anterior is tagged; a steel wire is passed through a hole at the medial/middle-third junction of the scapular spine for downward traction; the scapula is anchored to a rib step-wise. *Woodward* — detachment of the inferior origin of trapezius and the rhomboids from the spinous processes with reinsertion more caudally (relocates the muscle origins rather than the bone itself). *Schrock* — 15–20 cm incision along the vertebral border; subperiosteal trapezius release; supraspinatus and infraspinatus released near the vertebral border and lifted ~1 cm laterally; step-wise reinsertion with wire suture. *Ombredanne — prophylactic clavicular osteotomy* in planned descents >2 cm or pre-operative paraesthesia: the congenitally short clavicle is osteotomised first to protect the brachial plexus from compression in the thoracic outlet.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Lowering the high-riding scapula carries a real risk of brachial plexus compression in the thoracic outlet: the congenitally short clavicle elevates the plexus over the first rib as the scapula descends. Prophylactic clavicular osteotomy (Ombredanne) is indicated for planned descents greater than ~2 cm or for pre-operatively documented paraesthesia.</p>
</aside>

**9. Muscle transpositions for deltoid paralysis.** Prerequisites: an intact rotator cuff and good hand/wrist function. *Léo Mayer* — detachment of trapezius from the acromion + extension with fascia lata; the distal end to proximal brachialis at 90° abduction; shoulder-spica cast 2 months. The effect in complete paralysis is largely tenodesis rather than active abduction — clinically useful as a working-position anchor. *Bateman* — osteoplastic detachment of trapezius + resection of the lateral clavicle; reinsertion of an osteoplastic fragment to the humerus (more secure bony union). *Ober* — saber incision; the short head of biceps is taken off the coracoid osteoplastically + the long head of triceps off the infraglenoid tubercle → transposition to the acromion at 90° abduction; cast 4 weeks. *Hildebrand* — pectoralis major is detached from its rib origins (not the humeral insertion); pars sternocostalis + pars sternoclavicularis are mobilised with care for the anterior thoracic nerves and the feeding thoracoacromial and axillary branches; the muscle is rotated 90° about its humeral insertion and fixed to the acromion and lateral clavicle. Active abduction from a robust donor.

**10. Obstetric brachial palsy — anterior tenotomy and transpositions.** *Fairbank-Sever* — deltopectoral route with deltoid retraction; Z-lengthening of pectoralis major (the proximal stump remains attached to reduce adductor pull); coracobrachialis retracted; transverse division of subscapularis. Cast in external rotation and 90° abduction → at 2 weeks an abduction brace + rehabilitation. Sever modified Fairbank by omitting the capsulotomy (stiffness risk). *Wickstrom* (muscle transposition for persistent contracture in children >2–4 years with preserved joint congruity) — anterior incision along the deltopectoral groove with lengthening of subscapularis and pectoralis major; the latissimus dorsi and teres major tendons are identified and divided close to bone; a second posterior incision — both tendons are routed through the proximal deltoid and fixed in a prepared bed on the lateral head of triceps at full external rotation and 90° abduction; immobilisation 3 weeks. *Merle d'Aubigné* — a conceptually similar technique with concurrent posterior capsulorrhaphy (to prevent posterior subluxation).

**11. Tendinopathy and rupture of the long head of biceps.** *Tenodesis* — deltopectoral route to the bicipital groove (opened along its entire length to the supraglenoid tubercle); the degenerated proximal stump is resected; the distal stump is fixed extra-articularly to the soft tissues around the groove. *Traumatic ruptures* — a small anterior arm incision to retrieve the distal stump. A long-arm cast in 130° elbow flexion for 25 days.

**12. Classical techniques for rotator cuff disease.** Conservative treatment succeeds in 85–98% of cases when applied systematically for over a year. After failure — classical open techniques.

*Debeyre* — a saber incision over the acromion; the periosteum is divided longitudinally between the trapezius and deltoid origins; the deltoid is fully detached, from the trapezius side a periosteo-tendinous flap is raised; with a chisel a portion or the whole acromion plus the AC and lateral clavicle to the coracoclavicular ligament is resected. After cuff repair — re-osteosynthesis or periosteal closure of the acromion. Maximum visualisation in massive retracted tears and for removal of calcifications in periarthritis. *Supraspinatus repair* — classical technique uses Debeyre's temporary acromial osteotomy for wide access; the cuff is sutured into a bony-periosteal bed and the acromion re-osteosynthesised. Shoulder-spica cast at 70° abduction and 20–30° forward flexion for 4 weeks. *Modern reinsertion* — arthroscopic or mini-open single- or double-row repair with suture anchors; the principles are unchanged.

**13. Resection and reconstruction of the proximal humerus.** Classical shoulder-joint resection was historically indicated for severe purulent or tuberculous arthritis — indications now greatly reduced. *Lawrence-Jones muscle-plastic resection* — an extended deltopectoral with detachment of the deltoid from clavicle, acromion, and scapular spine. Three capsulomuscular flaps are formed: anterior (subscapularis, 1–3 o'clock), superior (supraspinatus, 11–1 o'clock), posterior (infraspinatus + teres minor, 9–11 o'clock). Economical resection of the humeral head; the flaps are anchored with sutures or wire in longitudinal bony grooves. The retained muscular cuff preserves active function. *Tumour resection* — suprapidic, en-bloc abblastic resection of the involved muscles with the bone. Reconstructions: *Portugalov* — proximal portion of the ipsilateral fibula placed in the humeral medullary canal + 1–2 screws; the fibular head functions as the new caput; shoulder-spica cast at 70° abduction until union → abduction brace. *Moskov* — lateral half of the clavicle with the AC joint preserved (cosmetic shoulder contour preserved). *Imamaliev* — frozen allograft, inserted step-wise with reinsertion of muscle attachments; allograft complications (fracture, fragmentation, resorption) have shifted the standard to custom endoprostheses.

**14. Shoulder arthroplasty — classical techniques.** *Neer (1953)* — a unipolar vitallium prosthesis simulating the humeral head with holes for cuff reinsertion. Deltopectoral route; subscapularis tendon and long head of biceps are tagged and detached; the conjoint is taken off the coracoid osteoplastically. The head is removed and the medullary canal is reamed; three small cancellous grafts are placed in the triangular spaces between bone and stem. Retroversion 30–40° (neutral → anterior subluxation; >40° → posterior instability). Shoulder-spica cast in the "saluting" position for 4 weeks. *Engelhardt (1975)* — a cementless model with ceramic surface; primary fixation by bone plastic deformation + cone teeth; secondary fixation by bone ingrowth into surface microroughness. Olecranon-pin traction 3–4 kg in 60° abduction for 3 weeks → shoulder-spica cast 2 months. *Modern standard* — anatomic hemiarthroplasty or total shoulder replacement for degenerative GH arthritis with an intact cuff; reverse total shoulder (Grammont) for cuff-tear arthropathy — inverting the ball-and-socket (ball on the glenoid, socket on the humerus) makes the deltoid the principal abductor in a cuff-deficient shoulder.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>The version of the humeral component in shoulder arthroplasty is critical for stability: insufficient retroversion (head set in neutral) predisposes to anterior subluxation, while excessive retroversion (&gt;40°) predisposes to posterior instability. Classical Neer recommends approximately 30–40° of retroversion relative to the forearm, measured intra-operatively.</p>
</aside>

**15. Recurrent anterior dislocation — classical techniques.** Classical goals: *(1)* capsulorrhaphy/tenodesis limiting external rotation; *(2)* bony reconstruction in glenoid defect; *(3)* reinsertion of labrum and capsule.

*Putti-Platt (V. Putti, H. Platt)* — deltopectoral; subscapularis and the anterior capsule are cut in parallel, 1 cm lateral to the glenoid rim; at maximum external rotation the capsular sutures are tied with overlap; subscapularis is then sutured with shortening. Outcomes are unreliable in Bankart lesions or bony defects. *Magnuson-Stack* — 8 cm anteromedial incision; subscapularis is taken off the lesser tuberosity osteoplastically → transposition to the crest of the greater tuberosity; external rotation permanently reduced by 40–50%. Velpeau immobilisation for 3 weeks. *Bankart* — deltopectoral; conjoint osteoplastic; subscapularis tenotomy 1–1.5 cm from insertion (the capsule may be adherent); the anterior glenoid rim is exposed; freshening with curette; the labrum and antero-inferior capsule are reinserted through bone tunnels with transosseous sutures (today suture anchors). External rotation is preserved — the advantage over Putti-Platt and Magnuson-Stack. *du Toit-Roux* — 8–10 cm deltopectoral incision; the conjoint is retracted; subscapularis is split semi-bluntly along its fibres to the glenoid rim; the capsule is divided along the same line; 1–3 metal staples are driven through capsule and glenoid neck intra- or extra-articularly under image control; the capsule is repaired; the split subscapularis is not sutured.

*Bone blocks ("blocs")* — historical predecessors of Latarjet. *Eden* — a corticocancellous graft from tibia or iliac crest placed vertically in the antero-inferior glenoid neck (intra-articular block). *Max Lange* — the graft placed extracapsularly as an external buttress against anterior displacement. *Oudar* — coracoid lengthening by oblique cut and translation of the distal fragment with the conjoint muscles in front of subscapularis with 1–2 sutures — the conceptual ancestor of Latarjet. *Judet* — a free rib graft wedged into freshened beds in the coracoid and the antero-inferior glenoid neck with a K-wire. *Latarjet (1954)* — the modern standard in bony defects >25%: the whole coracoid with the conjoint muscles → transposition to the antero-inferior glenoid rim with screws; the triple effect (bony augmentation, conjoint sling in ABER, dynamic hammock configuration of subscapularis). *See Trauma-12.*

**16. Posterior recurrent dislocation — glenoplasties.** *Scott* — posterior approach with deltoid detachment and resection of the prominent portion of the acromion as a free graft for posterior glenoid augmentation. *Adams* — a posterior bone block + capsulorrhaphy in congenital posterior habitual subluxation and rare traumatic cases.

**17. Open reduction of fresh and old fracture-dislocations.** Used when closed reduction fails (impression fracture of the antero-superior humeral head with the posterior glenoid rim locked into it — reverse Hill-Sachs in posterior dislocation). Posterior approach; internal rotation of the arm → progressive release of adhesions → articular surfaces. The impression groove is filled with a contoured iliac-crest autograft and fixed with 2–3 screws; alternatively, the subscapularis + capsule are reinserted onto the new anterior rim Bankart-style. In children with open physes — K-wires. 70° abduction and neutral rotation for 45 days; with stable fixation — early active exercise (Müller).

**18. Shoulder arthrodesis.** Indications: deltoid paralysis with intact trapezius and serratus anterior, severe destruction by infection (tuberculosis, septic arthritis), neglected traumatic injury, multiple failed procedures, and unsuitability for arthroplasty. Optimal position: 45° abduction, 45° external rotation (relative to the horizontal forearm), 45° forward flexion. Müller recommends intra-operative measurement — the angle between the lateral scapular border and the humerus of ~75° corresponds to 45° abduction relative to the trunk. Three groups: extra-articular, intra-articular, combined.

*Extra-articular:* *Albee* — a compact tibial graft driven through the acromion into the head without opening the joint. *Watson-Jones* — a freshened acromion driven into a slot beneath the humeral cortex. *Inferior extra-articular* (biomechanically more sound): *Vreden-Putti* — internal scapular border as graft; *Brittain/Britton* — a compact tibial graft shaped like an arrow with a bifid base, wedged between scapula and humerus; long cast immobilisation is the drawback. *Intra-articular:* *Lexer* — antero-external approach with deltoid detachment; freshening and fixation. *Matz* — addition of metal osteosynthesis and compression. *Müller compression arthrodesis* (modern standard) — incision along the scapular spine distally over the humeral midline; articular surfaces freshened; primary fixation with 2 K-wires (horizontally through the humeral metaphysis into the scapular neck + obliquely through the head into the internal scapular border); a compression screw along one wire, a second along the other; a compression plate with 2 short screws into the scapular spine + 2 cancellous screws through the head into the scapular neck. Long-arm cast 2–3 months. *Combined:* *Merle d'Aubigné* — a pedicled scapular-spine graft with the attached posterior acromion, hinged down to bridge the joint (pedicled on trapezius for blood supply). *Gill* — posterolateral incision; the acromion is exposed from both sides; intra-articular fusion with the acromion seated in a slot in the head. *Holevich-Paneva* — saber incision across the scapula, acromion and lateral third of the clavicle; subperiosteal release of the deltoid; wide capsulotomy; the space between caput humeri and the inferior surface of the acromion is filled with a free iliac-crest autograft; temporary K-wires → screws. *External-fixator compression arthrodesis (Charnley, Ilizarov)* — anterior incision extended posteriorly; 3 pins in the head/shaft; after 4 weeks the fixator is removed; cast continues for another 30 days.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Optimal position for shoulder arthrodesis after the classical authors: 45° abduction, 45° external rotation (relative to the horizontal forearm), and 45° forward flexion. This permits the hand to reach the mouth, face, and contralateral ear with elbow flexion, and the perineum with extension. The intra-operative measurement is the angle between the lateral scapular border and the humerus — ~75° corresponds to 45° abduction relative to the trunk. Excessive abduction yields permanent restriction of adduction and chronic discomfort.</p>

**19. Scapulectomy and tumour resections.** *Tikhoff-Linberg* — total or subtotal scapulectomy with interscapulothoracic resection without limb sacrifice in malignant tumours with an intact humerus. The economical resections (acromion, body, coracoid) are reserved for post-traumatic states, osteomyelitis, tuberculosis, exostoses, and low-grade tumours. Access via Kocher's incision (slightly curved along the vertebral border). For superior-portion resection — protect the suprascapular nerve and artery. When limb salvage is not possible — Berger's interscapulothoracic disarticulation; if possible, high humeral amputation with resection of the proximal third (the scapula and clavicle preserve the shoulder contour). *Acromial-process fractures* (transverse, differential with persistent os acromiale) — a malleolar screw or a Weber-style tension band.

**20. Shoulder arthroscopy.** Has supplanted open surgery for many shoulder conditions. *Posterior portal* — 2–3 cm inferior and 1–2 cm medial to the posterolateral corner of the acromion; the "soft spot" between infraspinatus and teres minor; the main viewing portal for the glenohumeral joint. *Anterior portal* — established under direct visualisation through the rotator interval; the main working portal for anterior procedures. *Anterolateral* — inferior to the anterior acromial edge; for subacromial procedures and cuff repairs. *Posterolateral* — for subacromial procedures. *Lateral* — through the deltoid, 2–3 cm lateral to the acromial edge; for subacromial procedures. *Accessory portals* — the 5-o'clock portal, Neviaser portal, trans-cuff portal (for specific indications). The arthroscopic survey systematically visualises the glenohumeral joint (labrum — Bankart, SLAP; cuff from the articular side; biceps tendon; cartilage; capsule), then the subacromial space (bursal-side cuff, coracoacromial arch, AC joint).

## VII. Complicationes et resultus · Complications and Outcomes

**Neurological complications.** *Axillary nerve* — injured in ~10–20% of anterior dislocations and in many proximal humerus fractures (Trauma-13); protection in deltoid-splitting incisions — the 5 cm limit distal to the acromion; pre-operative sensory testing over the lateral deltoid is mandatory. *Musculocutaneous nerve* — neuropraxia from vigorous retraction of the conjoint bundle; limit to 5 cm distal to the coracoid. *Suprascapular nerve* — at the spinoglenoid notch in deep posterior dissection; at the suprascapular notch in wide scapular approaches (protect the superior transverse scapular ligament when there is no indication for release). *Brachial plexus* — compression in Sprengel correction without Ombredanne osteotomy; traction in high-energy dislocations.

**Vascular complications.** Bleeding from the anterior humeral circumflex ("three sisters") along the inferior subscapularis border — mandatory ligation before detachment. The axillary artery is at risk in deep medial dissection beneath the coracoid. The circumflex scapular artery in wide scapular exposures — retraction beneath the lateral border makes control difficult.

**Hardware complications.** K-wire migration (fatal cases reported in SC and AC fixation) — bend the ends, early removal (3–4 weeks). Tension-band wire on the clavicle — breakage by week 4. Inadequate retroversion in arthroplasty (neutral → anterior subluxation; >40° → posterior instability).

**Soft-tissue complications.** Subscapularis dysfunction after a classical vertical tenotomy — lesser-tuberosity osteotomy or peel with a bone block delivers better functional outcomes. Tight capsulorrhaphy (Putti-Platt, Magnuson-Stack) — permanent restriction of external rotation (40–50%) and post-operative degenerative GH arthritis (capsulorrhaphy arthropathy on long-term follow-up).

**Union and healing.** Clavicular fracture non-union rates ~<5% with plate fixation and 10–15% with cerclage; freshening with curette before Bankart reinsertion is critical to union; re-osteosynthesis of the osteotomised acromion in the Debeyre or Hadzhistamov approach is critical to stability.

**Functional outcomes.** Classical long-term series (Boychev, Holevich-Paneva): instability recurrence 5–15% in correctly selected patients without bone loss; modern meta-analyses of Bankart with anchor repair — recurrence 4–10%, Latarjet 1–5% in bony defect. Cuff repair: re-tear rates 20–40% depending on tear size, retraction, tendon quality and age; good-to-excellent Constant and UCLA scores in 75–85%.

## VIII. Български приноси · Bulgarian Contributions

The Bulgarian orthopaedic school has contributed to shoulder surgery at the topographic, reconstructive and prosthetic levels at once. Systematised in *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* of Boycho Boychev (with Ya. Holevich and A. Konforti; later editions with Chokanov), these contributions remain part of the national curriculum and are taught today at the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya.

**The topographic school.** The infraclavicular region is taught as three triangles — *trigonum clavipectorale* (between the clavicle and the superior border of pectoralis minor; pierced by branches of the thoracoacromial artery, which must be controlled in extended deltopectoral approaches), *trigonum pectorale* (behind pectoralis minor; the entire neurovascular bundle lies here, 2 cm anterior to subscapularis), and *trigonum subpectorale* (below its inferior border). This arrangement explains why medial dissection beyond the short head of biceps under the coracoid directly risks the axillary artery, median nerve, and ulnar nerve.

**Boychev's procedure** — the iconic Bulgarian operation for recurrent anterior dislocation without significant glenoid bone loss. The conjoint bundle (*coracobrachialis* + the short head of biceps) is taken off the coracoid osteoplastically, routed *behind subscapularis* (between the muscle and the anterior capsule), and reinserted to the coracoid in its new position. The transferred bundle creates an active anterior "hammock" over the antero-inferior head in abduction–external rotation and simultaneously shortens subscapularis with limitation of external rotation. The anatomical risk is the `musculocutaneous nerve` in coracobrachialis — the nerve enters 1–6 cm below the tip and is at risk during routing. Indications do not include patients with a bony defect of the anterior glenoid rim — for these, Latarjet or a bone block remain the choice.

**Holevich–Paneva–Popova** — a Bulgarian modification of the Bankart principle. Deltopectoral route; conjoint osteoplastic; subscapularis tenotomy 1–1.5 cm from insertion, the belly retracted medially; in full external rotation the glenoid rim is freshened with a curette; through pre-drilled holes small corticocancellous autografts ("transplants") are driven — 4–5 mm tall, ~1 cm long, with reverse-direction barbs to prevent pull-out. A strong silk suture is tied to the base of each transplant; the capsule is passed over it and sutured in overlap — simultaneous bony reconstruction of the antero-inferior rim and capsulorrhaphy. Soft immobilisation in moderate abduction and internal rotation for 20–30 days. The same authors describe a *posterior variant* for posterior habitual dislocation (posterior approach along the scapular spine; infraspinatus 2 cm from insertion with suprascapular-nerve protection; posterior capsule 2 cm from the rim; overlap capsulorrhaphy + infraspinatus reinserted slightly more laterally for active posterior tenodesis) and a *combined arthrodesis* (saber incision across the scapula, acromion and lateral third of the clavicle; the space between the head and the inferior acromion is filled with a free iliac-crest autograft).

**Hadzhistamov's posterior trans-spinal approach** — the conceptual precursor of modern transacromial and "cobra" approaches. An osteotomy across the middle of the scapular spine (without opening the AC joint); a cutaneo-muscular flap is hinged downward like a page, exposing the entire upper rotator cuff, the greater tuberosity facet, the long head of biceps, and the posterior segment of the glenohumeral capsule. Indicated in classical reconstructions of massive postero-superior tears in which the standard posterior interval between infraspinatus and teres minor is inadequate. Re-osteosynthesis of the osteotomised fragment is critical for postoperative stability.

**Balchev** — an independent Bulgarian modification in the Magnuson-Stack spirit: lateral transposition of the subscapularis tendon from the lesser to the greater tuberosity, with permanent reduction of external rotation and limitation of anterior instability. Described in parallel with the American technique and retained as a national variant in the Bulgarian curriculum.

**Reconstructions of the proximal humerus.** For tumoural or post-traumatic loss of the proximal third of the humerus the classical Bulgarian school describes three variants: *Portugalov* — replacement with the proximal part of the ipsilateral fibula, whose head acts as the new caput humeri, fixed in the medullary canal with 1–2 screws; *Moskov* — replacement with the lateral half of the clavicle with the AC joint preserved (preserves the cosmetic shoulder contour); *Imamaliev* — replacement with a frozen allograft, step-wise inserted with reinsertion of the muscle attachments. The latter was the historical standard, displaced by custom endoprostheses because of high rates of allograft fracture, fragmentation and resorption.

**Prophylactic clavicular osteotomy (Ombredanne)** (a French contribution adopted into the Bulgarian curriculum) — for planned descents in Sprengel correction >2 cm or pre-operative paraesthesia: pre-emptive osteotomy of the congenitally short clavicle protects the brachial plexus from compression in the thoracic outlet.

**Soviet and Russian schools in the Bulgarian curriculum.** *Vreden-Putti* (extra-articular arthrodesis with the internal scapular border), *Chaklin* and *Tsivyan* (in spinal surgery — see Anatomy-1), *Korzh* (complex revision procedures), and *Ilizarov* (compression arthrodesis with external fixator) — all are read and taught through the classical Bulgarian survey of operative orthopaedics.

## IX. ⭐ NB — Nota bene · Clinical Rules

1. The axillary neurovascular bundle lies ~2 cm anterior to subscapularis within the pectoral triangle.
2. The axillary nerve wraps the surgical neck of the humerus 5–7 cm distal to the acromion on the deep surface of the deltoid — the limit for a deltoid-splitting incision.
3. The musculocutaneous nerve enters coracobrachialis 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid — the safe zone for conjoint-bundle retraction.
4. The axillary nerve is injured in 10–20% of anterior dislocations and in many proximal humerus fractures → sensory testing over the lateral deltoid is mandatory.
5. The cephalic vein marks the deltopectoral interval; retraction laterally with the deltoid is usually safer for vein preservation.
6. The "three sisters" (anterior humeral circumflex artery + venae comitantes) along the inferior subscapularis border are identified and ligated before muscle detachment.
7. Humeral retroversion 30–40° in arthroplasty — a neutral position predisposes to anterior subluxation; >40° to posterior instability.
8. Optimal position for shoulder arthrodesis after the classical authors: 45° abduction, 45° external rotation, 45° forward flexion.
9. Bigliani III (hooked acromion) correlates with impingement and full-thickness supraspinatus tears.
10. Os acromiale (1–8% of the population, mesoacromion most common) — a recognised substrate for impingement and for failure of classical acromioplasty.
11. The Bankart procedure requires freshening of the glenoid rim with a curette before reinsertion — critical for union.
12. In Sprengel correction, a prophylactic Ombredanne clavicular osteotomy should be considered for planned descents >2 cm or pre-operative paraesthesia.
13. Percutaneous K-wires across the SC joint are acceptable only with the ends bent above the skin and with removal at 3–4 weeks; intrathoracic migration is a recorded fatal complication.
14. Boychev's procedure is indicated only when there is no significant bone loss of the anterior glenoid rim; for bony defect — Latarjet or bone block.
15. An "off-track" Hill-Sachs defect requires remplissage or bony augmentation in addition to labral repair.

## X. ⚠️ Cave · Pitfalls

1. Dissection medial to the short head of biceps beneath the coracoid — risks the axillary artery, median nerve, and ulnar nerve.
2. Deltoid-splitting incision >5 cm distal to the acromion — axillary nerve.
3. Rough mobilisation of the conjoint bundle in Boychev's procedure — musculocutaneous nerve in coracobrachialis.
4. Posterior approach with deep dissection in the spinoglenoid notch — suprascapular nerve.
5. Uncontrolled bleeding from the "three sisters" before subscapularis detachment — obscures the operative field.
6. An overlooked os acromiale in acromioplasty → persistent pain and iatrogenic instability of the acromial fragment.
7. Placement of the humeral component in neutral retroversion → anterior subluxation.
8. Bankart without glenoid-rim freshening → non-union and recurrence.
9. Holevich-Paneva transplants without reverse-direction barbs → pull-out from the bony bed.
10. Sprengel correction without clavicular-length assessment → brachial-plexus compression in the thoracic outlet.
11. K-wire across the SC or AC joint without bent ends → intrathoracic migration (recorded fatal cases).
12. A deltoid-splitting approach in the AC zone without identifying os acromiale → iatrogenic symptoms.
13. Putti-Platt in a bony glenoid defect → recurrence (requires Latarjet or bone block).
14. Arthrodesis in excessive abduction → chronic discomfort and permanent restriction of adduction.
15. Detachment of supraspinatus in wide scapular approaches without identifying the transverse-cervical / suprascapular anastomosis → significant uncontrolled bleeding.

## XI. Fontes classici · Classical Sources

The surgical anatomy of the shoulder was systematised at the beginning of the 19th century by Velpeau (1832), whose *Nouveaux éléments de médecine opératoire* gave the founding description of the shoulder bandage that still bears his name. Dupuytren's *Leçons orales* (1832) contributed the Hôtel-Dieu clinical series on shoulder dislocations. Astley Cooper's *A Treatise on Dislocations* (1822) gave the founding English-language description of recurrent anterior dislocation. Heinrich Braun (1907) introduced the supraclavicular brachial plexus block — the antecedent of modern ultrasound-guided ISB/SCB — and Hoffa (1905) consolidated the orthopaedic operative tradition. The Bulgarian school (Boychev, Holevich, Hadzhistamov) carried these classical lines into the local 20th-century curriculum.

## Summary

The shoulder offers a rich set of surgical approaches reflecting its mobility, complex muscular envelope, and proximity to neurovascular structures. The deltopectoral approach is the workhorse for the anterior shoulder, using the avascular plane between the deltoid (axillary nerve) and pectoralis major (pectoral nerves), with the cephalic vein as the surface landmark; the musculocutaneous nerve entering coracobrachialis 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid limits medial retraction; subscapularis management (vertical tenotomy, lesser-tuberosity osteotomy, or peel) is a key surgical decision. The posterior approach between infraspinatus and teres minor gives access to the posterior glenoid, with the axillary nerve limiting deltoid split to 5 cm distal to the acromion. The anterior axillary and superior saber-cut add cosmetic and superior-cuff exposures. Bulgarian contributions — Boychev (conjoint transfer), Holevich-Paneva-Popova (bone transplants + capsulorrhaphy), Hadzhistamov (trans-spinal approach), Balchev (Magnuson-Stack variant), Moskov/Imamaliev/Portugalov (proximal humerus reconstructions) — and the topographic concept of the clavipectoral/pectoral/subpectoral triangles form the Bulgarian operative identity in shoulder surgery. The chapter that follows turns to the arm and humerus, continuing the systematic regional review of the upper extremity.
