## Overview
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The arm (regio brachii), extending from the shoulder to the elbow, contains the humerus and its surrounding muscular envelope, with the principal neurovascular structures — musculocutaneous nerve, radial nerve, median nerve, ulnar nerve, brachial artery, and brachial vein — traversing the segment in characteristic relationships to the bone. Surgical approaches to the humerus are dictated by the location of pathology along its length and, above all, by the proximity of the radial nerve to the periosteum in the spiral groove — the principal neurological consideration in approaches to the middle and distal thirds. The two main approaches — the anterolateral for the proximal and middle thirds, and the posterior for the middle and distal thirds — together provide access to most regions of the diaphysis; the medial Karagyozov approach and the distal-lateral approach are specialised variants from the Bulgarian operative school. This chapter presents the regional surgical anatomy, the principal operative approaches, and the classical operative techniques (Hackenthal, Küntscher, AO/Kozhuharov plating, osteotomies, lengthening, amputations, muscle transfers) embedded in the curriculum of the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya.

</details>

The humerus is the largest long bone of the upper extremity, extending from the glenohumeral joint proximally to the elbow joint distally. The principal anatomical regions are the proximal humerus (head, anatomical neck, greater and lesser tuberosities, surgical neck — see Topic Trauma-13 for fracture anatomy), the diaphysis with its characteristic anterior bow and the spiral groove on the posterior surface, and the distal humerus (medial and lateral columns, trochlea, capitellum — see Topic Trauma-15).

The arm is divided into two principal muscular compartments. The **anterior compartment** contains biceps brachii (short head from the coracoid and long head from the supraglenoid tubercle, both inserting via the bicipital aponeurosis on the radial tuberosity), coracobrachialis (from the coracoid to the medial humerus), and brachialis (from the lower half of the humerus to the coronoid process of the ulna). The compartment is supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve (C5–C7), which pierces coracobrachialis about 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid and runs between biceps and brachialis to emerge as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve.

The **posterior compartment** contains triceps brachii with its three heads (long head from the infraglenoid tubercle, lateral head from the upper posterior humerus, medial head from the lower posterior humerus), inserting on the olecranon. The compartment is supplied by the radial nerve (C5–T1), which crosses the spiral groove of the humerus from medial-superior to lateral-inferior, supplying branches to triceps before becoming superficial laterally and passing anterior through the lateral intermuscular septum.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The two arm compartments — anterior (musculocutaneous nerve) and posterior (radial nerve) — host almost every possible surgical approach; the medial neurovascular bundle is not in its own compartment but in a specialised fascial sheath between biceps and the medial intermuscular septum.</li>
    <li>Brachialis has dual innervation — lateral two-thirds from the musculocutaneous nerve, medial third from the radial nerve — which is why midline splitting of the muscle is the safe option in the anterolateral approach.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Regional Borders and Surface Landmarks
<details id="regional-landmarks">
<summary>Regional Borders and Surface Landmarks</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Classical anatomy defines the proximal border of the regio brachii as the arcuate line joining the lower border of pectoralis major with that of latissimus dorsi and the teres major tendon; the distal border lies approximately 5–6 cm proximal to the two humeral condyles. On either side of biceps brachii two longitudinal sulci form — the medial bicipital groove and the lateral bicipital groove.

The medial bicipital groove houses the brachial neurovascular bundle (brachial artery and veins together with the median nerve); the lateral groove carries the superficial course of the cephalic vein up to the deltopectoral groove, where it dives beneath the fascia. The basilic vein lies subcutaneously medially and pierces fascia brachii at the level of the lower border of biceps in the proximal third, continuing as a deep vein adjacent to the neurovascular bundle. These grooves are the principal operative landmarks: the lateral bicipital groove defines the line of the anterolateral approach, while the medial defines the medial neurovascular approach.

The skin over the anteromedial surface is thin and freely mobile; over the anterolateral surface it is thicker but retains mobility. Cutaneous innervation is provided medially by the medial brachial cutaneous nerve (from the medial cord) and the intercostobrachial nerve (lateral branch of the second intercostal nerve); laterally by branches of the axillary nerve (superior lateral brachial cutaneous); and posteriorly by branches of the radial nerve (posterior brachial cutaneous and inferior lateral brachial cutaneous) — the posterior branches emerging through the fascia at the level of the crossing with the long head of triceps.

The radial nerve is the principal neurological structure at risk in approaches to the humerus. It branches from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus, descends posterior to the brachial artery, and enters the spiral groove of the posterior humerus, where it passes obliquely from medial-superior to lateral-inferior. In the spiral groove the nerve lies immediately adjacent to periosteum (within 5 mm), with no muscular protection — this is the anatomical fact underlying the high rate of radial nerve injury in humeral shaft fractures (Topic Trauma-14) and the demand for meticulous identification and protection during surgical approaches to the posterior and lateral humerus.

Classical Bulgarian anatomy describes the posterior osseo-muscular course of the radial nerve as the *canalis humeromuscularis* (the spiral groove together with the overlying medial and lateral heads of triceps). The canal has a spiral course from above downwards and from medial to lateral, with a total length of approximately 11–12 cm; it terminates with an exit through the lateral intermuscular septum approximately 4–6 cm distal to the deltoid insertion or 7–8 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle. Within the canal, the radial nerve is accompanied by the profunda brachii artery and its venae comitantes. This measurement is critical for planning the length of a posterior approach and for plate placement on the posterior surface — the canal's exit marks the precise level at which the neurovascular bundle leaves the posterior compartment for the anterior, and is the site of potential iatrogenic engagement.

After traversing the spiral groove, the radial nerve pierces the lateral intermuscular septum at the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the humerus and enters the anterior compartment of the distal arm, where it lies between brachialis (medial) and brachioradialis / extensor carpi radialis longus (lateral). At the level of the elbow the radial nerve divides into the superficial sensory branch and the deep motor branch (posterior interosseous nerve, PIN), which passes through the arcade of Frohse at the proximal margin of supinator and into the forearm.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The radial nerve in the spiral groove lies within 5 mm of periosteum with no muscular cushion; periosteal stripping along the postero-medial surface of the middle third without prior identification and protective retraction of the nerve is a documented cause of iatrogenic radial palsy.</li>
    <li>Canalis humeromuscularis is 11–12 cm long and exits 7–8 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle or 4–6 cm distal to the deltoid insertion — the two reproducible landmarks for identifying the nerve.</li>
    <li>Distal to the spiral groove the radial nerve runs in the anterior compartment between brachialis and brachioradialis / ECRL — at the distal-lateral approach the nerve is no longer in the posterior field and is the first structure to be identified.</li>
    <li>Once it has pierced the lateral intermuscular septum, the radial nerve splits into the superficial sensory branch and the PIN, which dives through the arcade of Frohse at the proximal supinator (Topic Anatomy-5).</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Musculocutaneous Nerve
<details id="musculocutaneous">
<summary>Musculocutaneous Nerve</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The musculocutaneous nerve branches from the lateral cord of the brachial plexus and pierces coracobrachialis typically 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid. It then runs between biceps and brachialis in the anterior compartment, supplying the three muscles of the compartment (coracobrachialis, both heads of biceps brachii, and the lateral two-thirds of brachialis). It emerges from the lateral border of biceps as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (sensory) at the level of the elbow.

In the anterolateral approach the nerve is protected within the corridor between the two muscles and is rarely directly in the field; the risk arises from excessive medial retraction of biceps or from extending dissection over coracobrachialis in the proximal third.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The point at which the musculocutaneous nerve pierces coracobrachialis is on average 5–8 cm below the coracoid — significantly higher or lower variants are documented and can be a cause of iatrogenic injury in the deltopectoral or proximal anterolateral approach.</li>
    <li>Postoperative elbow-flexion weakness with paraesthesia along the lateral forearm is the first clinical sign of musculocutaneous nerve injury after anterior humeral surgery.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

The brachial artery descends along the medial bicipital groove between biceps and the medial intermuscular septum, accompanied by the median nerve, which runs lateral to the artery proximally and crosses to the medial side at the elbow. The ulnar nerve lies in the posterior compartment for most of the arm, medial to the radial nerve, and in the distal third passes behind the medial intermuscular septum through the septum intermusculare mediale to reach the groove of the medial epicondyle (sulcus n. ulnaris). In standard humeral approaches the ulnar nerve is not at risk, except in the medial paratricipital posterior approach and in the medial distal approach, where its identification is the first step.

The profunda brachii artery branches from the brachial artery in the proximal third and accompanies the radial nerve in the canalis humeromuscularis — its injury during a posterior approach without identification is a real cause of intraoperative haemorrhage and of a risky post-periosteal haematoma.

### Intermuscular Septa
<details id="septa">
<summary>Intermuscular Septa</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The lateral intermuscular septum separates the anterior and posterior compartments laterally and runs from the deltoid tuberosity to the lateral epicondyle. The medial intermuscular septum is the medial counterpart — from the coracobrachialis insertion to the medial epicondyle. The radial nerve pierces the lateral intermuscular septum at the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the humerus — a critical anatomical landmark for identifying the nerve in the anterolateral and distal-lateral approaches. The ulnar nerve pierces the medial intermuscular septum in the distal third and passes posterior to it toward the groove of the medial epicondyle.

## Pathophysiology and Mechanism
<section class="topic-section" id="pathophysiology">
<details>
<summary>Pathophysiology and Mechanism</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The pathology of the arm region is dominated by three categories: traumatic (shaft fractures with risk of radial nerve injury, proximal and distal humeral fractures with risk to the glenohumeral and elbow joints, muscle ruptures), neurocompressive (radial nerve compression in the canalis humeromuscularis, high radial palsy at the arcade of Frohse), and tumour/infection (primary and metastatic humeral tumours, osteomyelitis, essential bone cyst in the proximal metaphysis).

**Biomechanics of shaft fractures.** The humeral diaphysis is a tubular bone with an anterior bow and asymmetric cortical thickness — the postero-lateral cortex is thicker and bears compression, the antero-medial is thinner and bears tension. The classical fracture mechanisms are: direct trauma (transverse and short-oblique fractures), indirect torsional-bending trauma (spiral fractures, typical in throwing sports), high-energy trauma (comminuted and segmental), and pathological (metastatic carcinoma, myeloma).

**Radial nerve injury in shaft fractures.** Because of the nerve's proximity to periosteum in the spiral groove and at the piercing of the lateral intermuscular septum, fractures of the middle third (Holstein-Lewis for a spiral fracture of the distal third) are associated with a 12–20% risk of primary radial palsy. The mechanisms of injury are: contusion (most common, spontaneous recovery in 70–90% within 3–4 months), entrapment between fragments, laceration by sharp bony edges, and neuroma-in-continuity with prolonged apposition.

**Arm compartment syndrome.** Rare compared with the forearm and leg but possible after high-energy trauma, vascular injury with reperfusion, prolonged immobilisation, or electrical injury. The classical symptoms (severe pain out of proportion, paraesthesia, painful passive finger flexion, late loss of pulses) require immediate fasciotomy of both compartments.

**Biceps brachii ruptures.** Proximal rupture of the long head (the most common, usually in men over 50 years with prior degeneration in the subacromial canal — the characteristic "Popeye sign") and distal avulsion from the radial tuberosity (younger active patients; complete loss of elbow flexion and supination).

**Essential bone cyst.** The proximal humeral metaphysis is one of the typical sites for essential (juvenile or aneurysmal) bone cyst, presenting with pathological fracture; the risk of physeal injury and recurrence is higher in younger children.

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis
<section class="topic-section" id="clinical">
<details>
<summary>Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Radial palsy.** The classical presentation is wrist drop with complete loss of extension of the wrist, fingers, and thumb, and sensory loss over the dorsal first/second metacarpal spaces. Preserved elbow extension (triceps innervated proximal to the spiral groove) indicates that the lesion is distal to the spiral groove; lost elbow extension indicates a proximal lesion. Electrodiagnostic study (ENMG) at 3–4 weeks distinguishes neurapraxia (recovery) from axonotmesis/neurotmesis (warranting exploration).

**Popeye biceps.** A palpable lump in the middle or lower third of the arm in proximal rupture of the long head — the muscle belly retracts distally because of loss of the proximal anchor. Active elbow flexion is almost preserved (the short head remains functional); supination is mildly weakened. Distal avulsion produces the opposite contour — the muscle belly retracts proximally, visible as a high lump in the upper half of the arm, and active supination at flexed elbow is markedly weakened.

**Shaft fracture.** Pain, swelling, deformity (typical varus or anterior angulation depending on the level and the pull of the muscles), and functional incompetence of the limb. Examination of the radial nerve is mandatory before and after every manipulation — a documented primary or secondary palsy dictates operative tactics.

**Imaging.** AP and lateral radiography of the humerus including the glenohumeral and elbow joints is the standard; for tumours, infections, and proximal metastasis — CT with 3D reconstruction and MRI for soft-tissue assessment. For suspected vascular injury — CT angiography or Doppler.

## Classification
<section class="topic-section" id="classification">
<details>
<summary>Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Shaft fractures (AO/OTA 12).** 12-A simple two-part (A1 spiral, A2 oblique, A3 transverse); 12-B wedge with a fragment in the fracture zone (B1 spiral wedge, B2 bending wedge, B3 fragmented wedge); 12-C complex/segmental (C1 spiral, C2 segmental, C3 fragmented). Location is denoted by an additional digit — 1 (proximal third), 2 (middle third), 3 (distal third).

**Holstein-Lewis classification.** Spiral fracture of the distal third of the diaphysis with the characteristic line running from proximolateral to distomedial — threatening the radial nerve as it pierces the lateral intermuscular septum; approximately 22% primary radial palsy.

**Proximal humeral fractures (Neer 1970).** Analysed by the four anatomical segments (head, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, shaft); one-, two-, three-, and four-part on the basis of > 1 cm displacement or > 45° angulation. See Topic Trauma-13 for details.

**Distal humeral fractures (Jupiter / AO 13).** Extra-articular (13-A), partial articular (13-B, single column), complete articular (13-C, T- or Y-configuration). See Topic Trauma-15.

**Hertel criteria for head ischaemia.** Calcar length below 8 mm, disruption of the medial hinge, and a basic displaced fragment are independent predictors of avascular necrosis in proximal humeral fractures.

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Treatment
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Nonoperative
<details id="nonoperative">
<summary>Nonoperative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Nonoperative treatment remains first choice for most humeral shaft fractures because of the segment's tolerance to mild angular and rotational deficit and the high union rate. The classical methods are:

**Hanging cast (Caldwell).** A long plaster cast from the axilla to the wrist, hung against the body by its weight; elbow flexed 90°; the weight of the cast provides constant traction and progressive reduction. Indicated for fractures of the middle and lower third without significant primary angulation.

**Functional brace (Sarmiento 1977).** A short plastic or plaster-plastic brace around the middle and lower arm, leaving the elbow and shoulder free; works on the principle of hydrostatic pressure through the soft tissues, maintaining the humeral axis. The brace is applied after initial immobilisation in a coaptation splint and resolution of the acute oedema phase (about 1–2 weeks). High union rate (around 90%) in appropriately selected patients.

**U-shaped coaptation splint.** A plaster splint from the axilla through the medial elbow and around it to the lateral, with spiral wrapping; initial treatment in the acute phase or definitive for selected stable fractures.

**Velpeau and Desault dressings.** Classical methods for immobilising the arm against the body in proximal humeral fractures, after operative biceps repair, and for postoperative stabilisation. Velpeau fixes the flexed elbow to the opposite shoulder; Desault fixes the arm against the trunk with a three-loop plaster wrap.

**Skeletal traction.** For temporary stabilisation of polytrauma patients and when operative treatment is delayed — olecranon screw traction with a transfixion screw through the olecranon or distal humeral vertical traction.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Acceptable conservative parameters for humeral shaft fractures: < 20° anterior angulation, < 30° varus angulation, < 3 cm shortening — beyond these, operative treatment.</li>
    <li>The Sarmiento brace is applied after 1–2 weeks in coaptation splint or hanging cast; a premature functional brace does not control the fresh fracture.</li>
    <li>High union rate (85–95%) in correctly selected patients — which is why nonoperative treatment remains first choice in humeral fractures, in contrast to most other long bones.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Operative
<details id="operative">
<summary>Operative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Absolute indications for operative treatment of closed shaft fractures: unacceptable conservative alignment, segmental fractures, pathological fractures, polytrauma patients, floating elbow with ipsilateral forearm fracture, bilateral humeral fractures, fractures with primary or secondary radial palsy requiring exploration, and failure of the conservative trial. Open fractures are an independent indication for debridement and stabilisation.


### Surgical Approaches
<details id="approaches">
<summary>Surgical Approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<details>
<summary>Anterolateral Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray411.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>

The anterolateral approach provides access to the proximal and middle thirds of the humeral shaft for plate fixation of fractures, tumour resection, infection drainage, and biopsy. The approach uses the natural plane between biceps and brachialis.

**Positioning.** The patient is supine with the affected arm draped freely, usually supported on an arm board or hand table. The shoulder is in slight abduction.

**Landmarks and incision.** The deltopectoral groove marks the proximal limit; the lateral margin of biceps marks the line of the incision distally. The skin incision begins just below the deltoid tuberosity and runs along the lateral border of biceps for the desired length.

**Technique.** Skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised; fascia brachii is opened in line with the skin incision. Biceps is identified and retracted medially, exposing brachialis. The muscle is split longitudinally in the midline — lateral two-thirds supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve, medial third by the radial nerve, so the midline split preserves at least partial innervation of both halves. Beneath the brachialis split, the humerus is exposed subperiosteally. When the dissection is extended into the distal third, the radial nerve must be specifically identified and protected — it pierces the lateral intermuscular septum at this level and enters brachialis from the radial side.

**Distal extension.** The anterolateral approach can be extended distally to expose the elbow joint by curving the incision laterally over the lateral epicondyle, developing the interval between brachialis (medial) and brachioradialis (lateral), with the radial nerve protected in this interval.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Midline splitting of brachialis preserves at least partial innervation of each half — lateral two-thirds from the musculocutaneous, medial third from the radial nerve. The lateral part of the muscle frequently receives an additional branch directly from the radial nerve, so a midline split rarely produces complete denervation — an observation confirmed in classical operative anatomy.</li>
    <li>The anterolateral approach is the workhorse for the proximal and middle thirds; when extended into the distal third, identification of the radial nerve is mandatory.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Circumferential periosteal stripping of the middle third without prior identification and protective retraction of the radial nerve is a documented cause of iatrogenic radial palsy.</li>
    <li>When the approach is extended distally — the nerve is more lateral than the surgeon initially expects; an early tangential application of a periosteal elevator on the postero-medial aspect of the middle third is particularly dangerous.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Posterior Approach (Triceps-Splitting and Paratricipital Variants)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-3/radial-nerve.png" data-figure-alt="Radial nerve on the posterior arm" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/anatomy-3/radial-nerve.png" alt="Radial nerve on the posterior arm" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Classical Gray's plate of the radial nerve on the back of the arm: course from the posterior cord through the canalis humeromuscularis with the profunda brachii artery, piercing the lateral intermuscular septum, and terminal division into superficial sensory branch and PIN — the anatomical basis for frequent nerve injury in shaft fractures and for the limit on triceps split during the posterior approach.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray818.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The posterior approach provides access to the middle and distal thirds of the humerus for plate fixation, radial nerve exploration, distal humeral osteosynthesis, triceps tendon procedures, and tumour or infection access. The approach uses either the natural plane between the lateral and medial heads of triceps or a triceps-splitting technique, depending on the desired exposure.

**Positioning.** The patient is in the lateral decubitus position with the affected side up and the arm draped over a padded support, or prone with the arm on a small platform. The elbow is flexed about 90°.

**Landmarks and incision.** The olecranon distally and the midline of the posterior arm proximally mark the line of the incision. The skin incision runs along the midline of the posterior arm from 10–15 cm proximal to the olecranon down to the olecranon.

**Triceps-splitting technique.** Skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised. The triceps fascia is opened in the midline. The muscle is split longitudinally between the lateral and medial heads (alternatively, the long head can be split centrally). The radial nerve must be specifically sought and protected at the lateral aspect of the proximal third of the humerus, where it traverses the spiral groove — palpation along the posterior humerus identifies the nerve, which lies in the deep groove against the periosteum. The split is carried down to periosteum, and subperiosteal dissection exposes the humerus. The profunda brachii artery accompanies the radial nerve and must be protected.

**Paratricipital technique (Bryan-Morrey).** Retracts triceps medially or laterally rather than splitting it. The lateral version develops the interval between triceps and the lateral intermuscular septum, retracting triceps medially; the medial — between triceps and the ulnar nerve / medial intermuscular septum, retracting triceps laterally. Preserves triceps function but provides less direct access.

**Triceps reflection (Bryan-Morrey).** Detaches the triceps insertion from the olecranon and reflects it proximally; gives wide exposure of the distal humerus but creates an insertion that must be repaired at closure.

**Olecranon osteotomy (chevron).** For the widest exposure of the distal humeral articular surface — chevron osteotomy at the narrowest point of the olecranon (anatomically the thinnest area), reflecting the proximal fragment with the attached triceps. Fixation with tension-band wiring or a plate. Standard approach for distal humerus ORIF (Topic Trauma-15).

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The radial nerve in the spiral groove must be identified before the triceps split reaches periosteum — palpation at the lateral aspect of the proximal third of the posterior humerus is the first step.</li>
    <li>The profunda brachii artery accompanies the radial nerve in the canalis humeromuscularis — its injury produces troublesome bleeding.</li>
    <li>The medial paratricipital approach requires explicit identification and protection of the ulnar nerve in the posterior compartment and in the groove of the medial epicondyle.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Olecranon osteotomy creates a separate fracture line that can go on to symptomatic nonunion; a chevron shape at the narrowest point and tension-band fixation at closure are mandatory.</li>
    <li>Triceps reflection and olecranon osteotomy require protection of the insertion/osteotomy fragment postoperatively — triceps weakness is a documented problem with the reflecting techniques.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Posterolateral Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The posterolateral approach is a classical Bulgarian-school variant exposing the lateral surface of the proximal and middle thirds of the humeral shaft. The patient is semi-lateral with a sandbag under the scapula of the operated side; the arm is free and draped for intraoperative manipulation. Surface landmarks are the posterior border of deltoid and the deltoid tuberosity.

The skin incision runs along the posterior border of deltoid, reaches its insertion, and continues distally along the lateral border of brachialis to the junction of middle and distal thirds. The posterior border of deltoid is retracted anteriorly (preserving the axillary nerve in its deeper proximal course), the lateral head of triceps is retracted posteriorly, and brachialis anteriorly, developing the approach to the lateral humeral surface.

In the distal end of the wound the radial nerve emerges through the lateral intermuscular septum and lies in front of it, continuing distally between brachioradialis and brachialis — this anatomical landmark sets the distal boundary of safe extension without explicit nerve identification. The approach is particularly suited to plate fixation of the lateral column for proximal and mid-diaphyseal fractures when the anterior approach is compromised by soft-tissue injury.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Distal-Lateral Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The distal-lateral approach exposes the distal third of the diaphysis and the immediate supracondylar segment. The patient is supine with the arm slightly abducted on a side table; landmarks are the lateral epicondyle, the origin of brachioradialis, and the triceps tendon.

The skin incision starts at the lateral epicondyle and runs proximally along the groove between triceps (posteriorly) and brachioradialis (anterolaterally) over the distal third. Subcutaneous tissue is divided and blunt dissection reaches the lateral intermuscular septum; triceps is retracted posteriorly and brachioradialis is either retracted anteriorly or subperiosteally peeled from the bone and pulled medially and anteriorly. The radial nerve, which by this level has already pierced the lateral intermuscular septum and lies in front of brachioradialis, must be specifically identified and protected. The periosteum is then incised over the distal diaphysis.

Specific indications: open reduction of distal diaphyseal and metaphyseal fractures extending to the lateral column, biopsy and curettage of distal bone tumours, and access to infected diaphyseal foci when the posterior skin is compromised.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>At the distal-lateral approach the radial nerve has already pierced the lateral intermuscular septum and lies in front of brachioradialis. Identification of the nerve is the first step before peeling brachioradialis off the bone; failure is a documented cause of complete radial palsy after an apparently routine distal humeral exposure.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Medial Approach — Neurovascular Bundle and the Karagyozov Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The medial approach is used primarily for exposure of the brachial neurovascular bundle (brachial artery and veins, median nerve, ulnar nerve); more rarely it provides indirect access to the medial humeral surface. The patient is supine with the limb abducted and externally rotated; landmarks are the medial bicipital groove and the distal biceps tendon.

The skin incision runs along the medial border of biceps in line with the groove. The basilic vein is identified in the subcutaneous tissue and either ligated or gently retracted. Fascia brachii is opened in line with the incision, biceps is retracted laterally, and the neurovascular bundle is exposed within its sheath between biceps and the medial intermuscular septum. The brachial artery is central, the median nerve lies lateral to it proximally and crosses to the medial side in the distal third, and the ulnar nerve sits behind the medial intermuscular septum in the distal two-thirds — the ulnar nerve must be identified and protected before more caudal extension.

**Karagyozov approach.** A Bulgarian modification for exposing the radial nerve from the medial side. From the standard medial incision the posterior border of deltoid is elevated and retracted anteriorly, revealing the radial nerve along the lateral border of the latissimus dorsi tendon as it enters the canalis humeromuscularis. For more caudal exposure the lateral head of triceps may be partially divided to fully reveal the neurovascular bundle along the canal; on closure the divided head is reattached with several sutures. The radial nerve may be traced distally by entering the interval between brachioradialis and brachialis.

The approach is preferred for:

- radial nerve revision after prior posterior surgery (when scarring makes re-splitting triceps technically hazardous);
- simultaneous exposure of the neurovascular bundle and the radial nerve;
- extremely medial compressive lesions (bony callus, dense fibrous ring).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The Karagyozov approach exposes the radial nerve from the medial side — from a standard medial incision the posterior border of deltoid is elevated and the radial nerve is identified along the latissimus dorsi tendon.</li>
    <li>Particularly useful for revision after a prior posterior approach with scarring, where repeated triceps splitting is technically hazardous.</li>
    <li>Ligation or gentle retraction of the basilic vein is the first step; iatrogenic injury is a frequent cause of intraoperative bleeding in this approach.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Anterior Approach to the Distal Humerus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The anterior approach to the distal humerus is used for selected procedures involving the distal humerus, the elbow joint, or the brachial artery. The approach is less commonly used than the anterolateral or posterior but has specific indications: vascular injury repair, exploration of the brachial artery, decompression of the median nerve in the distal arm (rarely), and access to intra-articular lesions when the posterior and lateral skin is compromised.

A longitudinal incision is made over the anterior aspect of the distal arm in the line of the brachial artery. Brachialis is identified and retracted laterally; biceps is retracted medially. The brachial artery and median nerve are identified and retracted as needed. The anterior aspect of the distal humerus is exposed. The approach is occasionally extended distally as the Henry approach to the proximal forearm and elbow (see Topic Anatomy-5).

</div>
</details>

</div>
</details>


### Surgical Technique
<details id="technique">
<summary>Surgical Technique</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<details>
<summary>Closed Reduction and Crossed Wires</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For selected mid-shaft fractures (particularly stable transverse patterns in younger patients), closed reduction and percutaneous crossed Kirschner wires from both epicondyles, driven retrograde into the medullary canal of the proximal fragment, may provide adequate stability. The classical Bulgarian modification uses two percutaneous wires from the medial side and two from the lateral side, protruding 2–3 mm from each cortex; two tension-band wires (medial and lateral) are woven and tightened around the protruding wire ends to form a tension-band construct that achieves sufficient stability without exposing the fracture.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Hackenthal Intramedullary Bundle Nailing</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Hackenthal technique uses a bundle of thin (3–4 mm) steel pins introduced through a hole drilled over the olecranon fossa, advanced retrograde into the medullary canal until the canal is filled. A 5 cm posterior incision starts 4–5 cm proximal to the olecranon in the midline; the triceps fascia and fibres are split, the periosteum is exposed over the olecranon fossa, and the bone is perforated with a drill or gouge. Four to six pins are sequentially introduced, each wedging in the proximal metaphysis and adding stability. The method is particularly suited to stable transverse and short-oblique mid-shaft fractures in older patients; limitations include inability to provide rigid rotational stability and unsuitability for segmental and highly comminuted patterns.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Küntscher Nail — Antegrade and Retrograde Techniques</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Küntscher medullary nail may be inserted through either of two approaches.

**Retrograde (distal) technique.** A 5 cm posterior incision is made starting 4–5 cm proximal to the olecranon in the midline. Skin, subcutaneous tissue, and triceps fascia are divided; the fibres and tendon of triceps are split to bone over the olecranon fossa. A drill or gouge creates the entry portal; a guidewire is advanced into the medullary canal of the proximal fragment, closed reduction is attempted, and on success the guidewire is advanced. Fluoroscopic confirmation precedes nail insertion over the guidewire. Modern modification recommends prior reaming of the canal to accept a larger nail for improved rotational stability. Closed reduction failure converts to an open anterolateral exposure.

**Antegrade (proximal) technique.** The skin incision starts at the lateral edge of the acromion and extends 5 cm distally over the deltoid. The deltoid fibres are split longitudinally (avoiding extension beyond 5 cm distal to the acromion to protect the axillary nerve). The entry portal is created on the superior aspect of the humeral head (medial to the greater tuberosity, in line with the canal), and the nail is passed over the guidewire into the distal fragment. The antegrade approach is technically simpler but carries a risk of rotator cuff irritation with postoperative pain and contracture; retrograde is preferred for more distal fractures.

When the nail is undersized for the canal and rotational stability is inadequate, the classical Bulgarian recommendation is to drill a small hole in the lateral cortex of both fragments near the fracture and pass a tension-band wire that is tightened after nail insertion. Additional elevation of bone tongues at the fracture line ("peel technique") promotes osteogenesis.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Compression Plate Osteosynthesis (AO/Kozhuharov)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Plate fixation, preferably with dynamic compression plates (DCP) or modern locking compression plates (LCP), is well suited to the humerus and provides rigid stabilisation with a high primary union rate. The Bulgarian Kozhuharov modification of the AO technique uses a pre-contoured compression plate with at least 8 cortices per fragment (6 for smaller bones or metaphyseal-diaphyseal junctions). The approach is anterolateral for the proximal and middle thirds or posterior for the middle and distal thirds (with explicit identification of the radial nerve). Fragments are reduced and the plate is temporarily fixed with bone clamps (Verbrugge or Lambotte). Three central holes are drilled in each fragment, the plate is tensioned in compression mode, and definitive screws are placed. A suction drain is left and the wound is closed in layers. With stable fixation, postoperative immobilisation is limited to a Velpeau or Desault sling for 2–3 weeks, followed by early functional rehabilitation.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>External Fixation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

External fixation is indicated for open fractures with extensive soft-tissue loss, infected non-unions, and polytrauma patients in whom early damage-control stabilisation is the priority. In Bulgarian practice the single-side Kozhuharov fixator and the Ilizarov circular frame are both used. Pins and wires are placed only after careful mapping of safe anatomical corridors — primarily the lateral and anterolateral surfaces of the proximal and middle thirds, with a strict 2 cm corridor in front of the radial nerve in the distal humerus.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Iatrogenic radial palsy remains a real risk with any form of fixation of the distal half of the diaphysis. Primary palsy after a closed fracture with initially preserved function → exploration within 24–48 hours if entrapment between fragments is suspected.</li>
    <li>Secondary palsy after closed manipulation or nailing: choice between immediate exploration and a 3–4 month observation period with electrodiagnostic monitoring; most secondary palsies recover spontaneously provided the nerve is not interposed between bone edges.</li>
    <li>The Kozhuharov single-side fixator — anterolateral and anterior pin trajectories in the middle third; biplanar fluoroscopy is mandatory.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Bridging Defects and Pseudarthrosis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Bridging bone defects of the humerus is less often necessary than in other long bones because the limb tolerates 3–5 cm of shortening without significant functional or cosmetic deficit. For larger defects, septic non-unions, and defects with compromised soft tissues, length reconstruction is mandatory.

The classical Boychev/Cleveland technique uses a double cortico-cancellous tibial graft: a long anteromedial tibial segment is divided transversely into two grafts, each fixed as a strut on opposite lateral surfaces of the humeral ends with four steel bolts and lock-nuts. The gap between the two strut grafts is packed with cancellous chips from the tibial condyle or iliac crest. Alternatively one graft (or a fibular segment) is impacted into the freshened medullary canals at both humeral ends, providing a central pillar with additional lateral stabilisation. Suction drains are placed, layered closure is performed, and the limb is immobilised in a plaster splint and later in a Velpeau thoraco-brachial cast for 6–8 weeks.

The modern equivalent for septic non-union is the Masquelet (induced membrane) technique or segmental bone transport with an Ilizarov frame; for aseptic atrophic non-union — compression plate osteosynthesis with autologous iliac crest cancellous grafts. Vascularised free fibular transfer remains an option for severe segmental defects exceeding 6 cm in a poorly vascularised bed.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Valgus Osteotomy of the Proximal Metaphysis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Indications: post-traumatic varus deformity with functionally significant loss of abduction; humerus varus congenitus (varus of the proximal humerus with medial tilt of the head and resulting loss of elevation); deformity after post-traumatic physeal arrest.

The patient is supine with a sandbag under the scapula. An 8 cm incision is made along the deltopectoral groove; the deltoid-pectoralis major interval is developed (preserving the cephalic vein), the fascia overlying coracobrachialis and the short biceps tendon is opened, and these muscles are retracted medially. The humerus is exposed subperiosteally over a short segment, and a wedge osteotomy with a medial base is performed with an oscillating saw approximately 1 cm distal to the surgical neck. The distal fragment is abducted to the desired neutral or slight valgus angle and temporarily fixed with K-wires.

Definitive fixation uses a compression plate or a 90°/95° angled blade plate calculated to the corrective wedge angle. When combined shortening is significant — as in congenital humerus varus — the valgus osteotomy can serve as the preparatory stage prior to simultaneous lengthening with an external apparatus. The goal of valgisation is restoration of a more favourable mechanical lever for biceps brachii and of the normal diaphyseal-epiphyseal angle, which improves active shoulder abduction.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Derotation Osteotomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Derotation osteotomy is indicated for rotational deformity after consolidated fracture, for fixed internal-rotation contracture in obstetric brachial plexus palsy, and as an adjunct in the treatment of habitual shoulder dislocation, where redirection of the head into external rotation provides mechanical stability for the anterior capsulolabral complex. Access is via an anterolateral approach to the middle third or a deltopectoral approach for the proximal metaphysis. After subperiosteal exposure a transverse osteotomy is performed, the distal fragment is rotated as desired (typically 30–60° of external rotation) under fluoroscopy, and the segments are fixed with a compression plate or an interlocking intramedullary nail allowing precise rotational correction.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Humeral Lengthening (Ilizarov / Kozhuharov)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Indications: shortening > 3–4 cm from traumatic loss of bone, growth arrest after septic osteoarthritis, congenital humerus varus with marked shortening, and post-ablation defects following oncological resection.

Under general anaesthesia, the patient is supine with the limb on a side table. The middle third of the humerus is exposed through a lateral incision. At the planned osteotomy site, two cortico-periosteal flaps are raised in Judet decortication fashion — preserving periosteal vascularity and stimulating regenerate formation. Two perpendicular Kirschner wires (or half-pins) are passed through the proximal fragment under fluoroscopy, oriented to avoid the neurovascular bundle and the radial nerve — anterolateral and anteromedial in the proximal third, strictly anterior in the middle third to avoid the canalis humeromuscularis. Two more wires are passed through the distal fragment.

The Ilizarov apparatus is assembled (two full or partial rings connected by distraction rods), then a low-energy osteotomy is performed with a thin osteotome or vibrating saw, preserving the periosteum.

Distraction begins on day 2–3 at 0.5–1 mm per day in four equal increments — this rhythm is critical for quality regenerate and for avoiding premature consolidation (too slow) or hypotrophic regenerate (too fast). Lengthening continues until target length is reached, after which 2–3 months of consolidation are allowed before frame removal. Delayed regenerate maturation is managed with cyclic compression-distraction ("accordion") to stimulate osteogenesis.

As an alternative, the Kozhuharov single-side fixator may be used, offering greater patient comfort and a lower risk of iatrogenic neurovascular injury, at the cost of reduced rotational stability and a more limited multiplanar correction range.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>For wire and half-pin placement during humeral lengthening, the canalis humeromuscularis (with the radial nerve and profunda brachii artery) is the principal forbidden zone — spiraling on the posterolateral surface between 7–8 cm above the lateral epicondyle and 4–6 cm below the deltoid insertion.</li>
    <li>A percutaneous wire in the middle third must follow a strictly anterolateral or anterior trajectory under biplanar fluoroscopy.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Operative Treatment of Essential Bone Cyst</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Indications: incidentally discovered or post-pathological-fracture essential bone cyst (unicameral/aneurysmal) in the proximal humeral metaphysis in childhood, adolescence, or young adulthood with risk of recurrent pathological fractures and physeal injury. First-line treatment is percutaneous aspiration and injection of methylprednisolone or bone morphogenetic protein; operative treatment is indicated after failure of 2–3 consecutive percutaneous cycles or for large cysts with impending fracture.

Under general anaesthesia, with the limb on a side table, a deltopectoral incision extends distally below the deltoid insertion along the lateral border of biceps. The humerus is exposed economically without circumferential stripping. A 1 cm window (trephination) is created over the cyst, extending to both poles. The medullary canal is opened; if the cyst extends immediately beneath the physis, the cyst wall under the physis is not removed, to avoid physeal injury. The cyst is emptied, the endosteal membrane is thoroughly curetted (high-speed bur to eliminate residual cells), and the cavity is packed with cancellous allograft or iliac crest autograft, impacted from distal to proximal. The wound is closed in layers over a suction drain.

Cancellous autograft gives faster incorporation but requires an additional procedure; classical Bulgarian experience shows that preserved (lyophilised or deep-frozen) allograft gives adequate results. A cast is required only after pathological fracture (4–6 weeks); otherwise early functional rehabilitation with load restriction for 8–12 weeks is advised. Long-term follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months is mandatory because of a real recurrence rate.

</div>
</details>

<summary>Biceps Brachii Rupture — Operative Repair</summary>

For incomplete rupture and early-diagnosed partial tears, conservative management with a Velpeau dressing at flexion above 90° is usually adequate. Operative repair is indicated for complete rupture with a palpable groove in the muscle belly, failure of conservative trial, and chronic ruptures with fibrosed ends.

The technique uses an anterolateral incision parallel to the lateral border of biceps (specifically chosen to avoid skin-muscle adhesions). The torn ends are mobilised, approximated, and sutured with interrupted heavy silk or modern synthetic mattress sutures (Bunnell or Krackow type for tendon ends, mattress sutures for muscle belly). For chronic ruptures with fibrosed ends, freshening of the ends to healthy bleeding fibres is required before repair.

The classical Christeas reinforcement adds a transversely applied patch of fascia lata across the defect — a fascial bridge that bears part of the tensile load while the muscle regenerates. For complete proximal long-head avulsions modern practice favours subpectoral tenodesis; for distal avulsions from the radial tuberosity — reinsertion through a single- or two-incision technique with suture anchors or cortical button.

Postoperatively a Velpeau dressing at flexed elbow is maintained for 3 weeks before progressive active and passive mobilisation to full function at 8–12 weeks.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Arm Fasciotomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The arm compartments are the anterior (biceps, brachialis, coracobrachialis) and the posterior (triceps). Arm compartment syndrome is uncommon but can occur after high-energy trauma, vascular injury with reperfusion, or prolonged immobilisation. Fasciotomy of the arm involves anterior compartment release through a longitudinal anterior incision with division of the overlying fascia, and posterior compartment release through a longitudinal posterior incision with division of the fascia over triceps. Both incisions can be combined with carpal tunnel release if hand compartment syndrome is also present.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Above-Elbow (Supracondylar) Amputation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The principal rule of arm amputation is to amputate as distally as possible, because stump length correlates directly with prosthetic quality and functional outcome. A stump shorter than 5 cm below the axillary fold loses functional value to a standard shoulder disarticulation, but the proximal humerus must be preserved to maintain shoulder contour and prosthetic socket fit.

The supracondylar region is the ideal level for above-elbow amputation, providing a long lever for prosthetic control and a strong muscular envelope for myoelectric control.

The patient is supine with the limb free. Two flaps (anterior-posterior or medial-lateral, depending on viable skin) are fashioned with a total length approximately equal to the limb diameter at amputation level. Fascia brachii is divided in line with the skin incision. The medial and lateral borders of biceps are mobilised; the muscle is divided transversely 2–3 cm above the skin incision.

The brachial artery and veins are doubly ligated (0 or 1 sutures with transfixion suture proximally for additional security) and divided. The median, ulnar, and radial nerves are identified, drawn distally, and sectioned high with a sharp blade — this traction manoeuvre allows the cut end to retract proximally into the muscle mass, away from the stump, reducing painful neuromas and phantom pain. Brachialis is divided transversely.

The entire anterior muscle mass is elevated to expose the bone. The periosteum is incised circumferentially, the bone is stripped over 5 mm, and the humerus is divided transversely with an oscillating or Gigli saw; edges are bevelled and rounded with a file. Triceps is divided, drawn forward as a posterior flap, and sutured to brachialis and biceps — a myodesis providing stable muscular coverage of the bone end and improving stump function. A suction drain is placed and the fasciocutaneous flaps are sutured in the midline without tension.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Cineplastic Amputation — Biceps Cineplasty</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The cineplastic amputation (after the Krukenberg/Sauerbruch tradition) creates an active biological motor from a muscle belly that can power a cable or pin transmission to the prosthesis, providing active phalangeal or grasping motion under direct voluntary control. Indications are limited to patients with well-healed amputation stumps, strong and non-adherent musculature, freely mobile adjacent joints, and adequate local skin for tunnel lining.

In above-elbow amputation, biceps cineplasty is the classical procedure. Skin flaps are pre-marked with sterile methylene blue with carefully planned geometry. A transverse tunnel is created through the biceps belly with a dedicated dilator, of length and diameter matching the prosthetic pin. With the dilator in place, the muscle end is over-sewn circumferentially to prevent adhesions.

A skin tube (formed from a free or local flap) is passed through the muscle tunnel, rotated approximately 90° so that its seam will not be pinched during pin traction. The proximal portion of the lateral edge of the skin tube is sutured to the lateral skin incision with several subcutaneous stitches, fixing it transversely to the arm. The lower skin edges are sutured together over about 2 cm; the lateral opening of the skin tunnel is sutured to the distal transverse incision and the muscle belly; the medial opening is kept open with sutures anchored to muscle. The tube is packed with vaseline gauze. Any residual skin defect is covered with a split-thickness skin graft from the thigh.

Cineplastic amputations remain historically relevant and have been largely replaced by modern myoelectric prostheses, in which surface electrodes capture myographic signals from the muscle belly without a skin tunnel; the concept retains educational value for understanding classical amputation surgery and for revision cases with a pre-existing tunnel.

</div>
</details>

For irreversible complete radial palsy without recovery after 12–18 months of monitoring and electromyography, function is restored by tendon transfers — the standard set comprises pronator teres to extensor carpi radialis brevis (wrist extension), flexor carpi radialis or flexor carpi ulnaris to extensor digitorum communis (finger extension), and palmaris longus to extensor pollicis longus (thumb extension); these procedures are addressed in detail in Topic Anatomy-5.

For combined injuries with biceps palsy (musculocutaneous nerve injury or combined C5–C6 plexus lesion) the classical transfers include: pedicled latissimus dorsi transfer with reinsertion at the acromion and biceps tendon to restore elbow flexion; pedicled pectoralis major transfer (Clark/Schottstaedt), mobilised on the lateral pectoral nerve pedicle and reinserted on the distal biceps tendon; the Steindler flexor-pronator origin transfer (medial epicondyle to coracoid), converting wrist and finger flexors into a secondary elbow flexor giving 15–20° of flexion. In severe palsy without viable donor muscles, free muscle transfer with microvascular anastomosis (typically gracilis with reinnervation from intercostal nerves or spinal accessory) is the final reconstructive option.

For post-traumatic habitual shoulder dislocation the derotation osteotomy of the proximal humerus (see above) may serve as an adjunct to anterior stabilisation — redirecting the head into external rotation places the anterior capsulolabral complex on a more favourable mechanical axis.

### Postoperative Care
<details id="postoperative">
<summary>Postoperative Care</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**After plate osteosynthesis.** Soft Velpeau or Desault sling for 2–3 weeks; early functional rehabilitation — active pendulum exercises from day 1, progressive active-assisted from week 2, loaded active exercises from week 6 on radiographic signs of union.

**After intramedullary nailing.** Antegrade — higher risk of subacromial pain and contracture; early shoulder mobilisation with emphasis on external rotation. Retrograde — limited elbow flexion in the first 2 weeks, followed by progressive active range-of-motion work.

**After amputation.** Immediate prosthetic fitting (rigid removable dressing) reduces postoperative oedema and time to definitive prosthesis. Psychological support for phantom pain and for vocational reintegration.

**After lengthening.** Distraction 0.5–1 mm per day in 4 increments; radiographic monitoring of regenerate formation every 2 weeks; for poor regenerate — compression-distraction ("accordion"). Frame removal at full cortical consolidation.

**After radial nerve revision.** Dynamic splint maintaining functional wrist position (30° extension) for the first 6 weeks; ENMG follow-up at 3 and 6 months to document reinnervation.

</div>
</details>

## Outcomes and Complications
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Anterolateral approach.** Musculocutaneous nerve injury (rare; preserved within the muscle split), radial nerve injury (the principal concern when the approach is extended into the distal third), elbow stiffness with distal extension and extensive soft-tissue disturbance.

**Posterior approach.** Radial nerve injury (the principal concern; the nerve is identified and protected in the spiral groove), profunda brachii artery injury, triceps weakness (with the reflecting techniques and olecranon osteotomy), ulnar nerve injury (with medial paratricipital approach if performed without explicit ulnar identification and protection), olecranon osteotomy nonunion.

**Posterolateral and distal-lateral approaches.** Radial nerve injury where it pierces the lateral intermuscular septum without explicit identification; axillary nerve injury with proximal extension over the posterior border of deltoid.

**Medial approach.** Ulnar nerve injury (with more caudal extension without identification), median nerve injury at its distal crossing with the brachial artery, iatrogenic bleeding from the basilic vein or brachial artery.

**Humeral fractures and osteosyntheses.** Primary radial palsy (12–20% in middle-third fractures and in Holstein-Lewis); secondary radial palsy (5–10% after operative treatment, up to 20% after retrograde intramedullary nailing); nonunion (5–10% in closed fracture, up to 20% in infected or septic non-union); infection (1–5%); adjacent joint stiffness — shoulder (in antegrade nailing) or elbow (in retrograde nailing).

**Distal humeral ORIF.** Olecranon osteotomy nonunion (5–10%); elbow stiffness (10–25%); iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury (5–10%); heterotopic ossification (5–15%).

**Amputation and cineplasty.** Painful neuroma (10–20%), phantom pain (50–70% transient; persistent in 5–10%), stump infection, muscular contracture and tunnel adhesions in cineplasty.

**Lengthening and osteotomies.** Pin-track infection (10–30%), delayed regenerate formation, regenerate fracture on premature frame removal, iatrogenic radial or vascular injury from misplaced wires through the radial canal.

## Local Contributions
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school; the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curricular reference and contains detailed descriptions of the classical approaches to the arm and humerus, including the anterolateral, posterolateral, and medial approaches, as well as the technique of bridging humeral defects with a double tibial graft (Boychev–Cleveland).

**Karagyozov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Medial approach to the radial nerve: from the standard medial incision the posterior border of deltoid is elevated and the nerve is identified along the latissimus dorsi tendon as it enters the canalis humeromuscularis. The lateral head of triceps may be partially divided to fully reveal the canal. Particularly useful for revision after prior posterior surgery with scarring, where repeated triceps splitting is technically hazardous.

**Kozhuharov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian modification of AO plate osteosynthesis: a pre-contoured compression plate with at least 8 cortices per fragment (6 at metaphyseal-diaphyseal junctions), anterolateral approach for the proximal/middle thirds or posterior for the middle/distal with explicit identification of the radial nerve. Compression mode tensioning with Verbrugge or Lambotte clamps. In addition — the single-side Kozhuharov fixator, a Bulgarian modification of a standard single-side distractor for humeral lengthening and stabilisation.

**Tanchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Classical Bulgarian modification of closed reduction with crossed Kirschner wires from both epicondyles, with two tension-band wires (medial and lateral) woven and tightened around the protruding wire ends to form a stable tension-band construct without exposing the fracture.

**Canalis humeromuscularis <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** The classical Bulgarian description gives the precise measurements of the canal (length 11–12 cm, exit 4–6 cm below the deltoid insertion or 7–8 cm above the lateral epicondyle) — the anatomical basis for the forbidden zone for pin placement and for planning the length of the posterior approach.

From the classical German, Russian, and French schools, tightly woven into the Bulgarian curriculum:

**Hackenthal <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>.** Intramedullary bundle nailing with thin (3–4 mm) steel pins through the olecranon fossa — particularly suited to stable transverse and short-oblique mid-shaft fractures in older patients.

**Küntscher <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>.** Classical medullary nail with antegrade (proximal, deltoid incision) and retrograde (distal, olecranon fossa) techniques; precursor of modern intramedullary nails with interlocking screws.

**Sarmiento <sup class="natflag">US</sup>.** Functional brace for nonoperative treatment of humeral shaft fractures (1977); works on the principle of hydrostatic pressure through the soft tissues, maintaining the humeral axis.

**Bryan & Morrey <sup class="natflag">US</sup>.** Paratricipital and triceps-reflecting techniques for posterior approaches to the distal humerus — preserve triceps function compared with classical triceps-splitting.

**AO Müller <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>.** Compression plate osteosynthesis with dynamic compression plate (DCP) and modern locking compression plate (LCP); humeral fracture classification (AO/OTA 12).

**Velpeau <sup class="natflag">FR</sup>.** The classical surgical anatomy of the arm and the eponymous immobilisation dressing (1832); founder of the Parisian surgical school in the upper limb.

**Larrey <sup class="natflag">FR</sup>.** Battlefield humeral amputations codifying the principle "the higher the better" for proximal injuries (1812); founder of field surgery.

**Henry <sup class="natflag">UK</sup>.** Classical extensile anterolateral approach from shoulder to wrist (1924), including extension of the distal humeral approach into the proximal forearm.

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Classical Sources
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">
<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical lineage — arm surgery</strong>
  <p>The surgical anatomy and operative principles of the upper limb in the modern sense are a product of the early nineteenth century, when the anatomical schools of Paris and Vienna systematised the approaches to the humerus and the brachial plexus. The classical textbook source is *Nouveaux éléments de médecine opératoire* by Alfred Velpeau (1832), which describes the two principal approaches to the humerus and the principles of the dressing that still bears his name.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Velpeau 1832</strong> — *Nouveaux éléments de médecine opératoire*, volume 1; surgical anatomy of shoulder and arm, Velpeau immobilisation dressing (<a href="https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_43901x01">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Larrey 1812</strong> — *Mémoires de chirurgie militaire et campagnes*, volume 2; battlefield humeral amputations codifying the principle "the higher the better" for proximal injuries (<a href="https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdechiru02larr">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Heinrich Braun 1907</strong> — *Die Lokalanästhesie*; supraclavicular brachial plexus block, precursor of modern ultrasound-guided ISB and SCB (<a href="https://archive.org/details/dielokalansthe00brau">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Hackenthal 1959 → Küntscher 1962 → Müller / AO 1969 → Kozhuharov 1980s</strong> — the evolution of humeral osteosynthesis from the bundle of pins through the medullary nail to the contoured compression plate.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Classical descriptions by approach: Velpeau 1832 (anterior anterolateral); Bergmann–Bruns 1899 (posterior triceps-splitting); Henry 1924 (extensile anterior approach from shoulder to wrist); Hackenthal 1959 (intramedullary bundle nailing); Küntscher 1940s–1960s (medullary nail); Bryan-Morrey 1982 (paratricipital and triceps-reflecting techniques); Karagyozov (medial approach to the radial nerve, Bulgarian school); AO Müller 1969 (compression plate with fracture classification); Sarmiento 1977 (functional brace for nonoperative treatment).

Additional classical sources: **Bergmann–Bruns** (1899), *Handbuch der praktischen Chirurgie*; **Hoffa** (1905), *Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie* (Stuttgart: Enke; <a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderortho00hoff">facsimile</a>); **Lexer** (1922), *Lehrbuch der allgemeinen Chirurgie*. **Langenbeck-Archiv für klinische Chirurgie** (from 1860) — first descriptions of many approaches and techniques.
