## Overview
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">


The elbow joint combines a hinge articulation (humeroulnar) with a pivot articulation (proximal radioulnar) within a single synovial capsule. Its thin muscular envelope places the surrounding neural and vascular structures — ulnar, median, radial nerves with the posterior interosseous branch (PIN), the medial and lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerves, and the brachial artery — directly accessible and correspondingly directly vulnerable in every operative approach. Regional surgery is organised around four windows: lateral (Kocher) for the radial head, capitellum, and the lateral collateral ligament complex; posterior (triceps-splitting, paratricipital, Bryan-Morrey reflecting, and olecranon osteotomy) for the distal humerus and ulnohumeral joint; medial (with optional medial epicondyle osteotomy) for the medial collateral ligament, ulnar nerve, and medial coronoid; and anterior (Henry's approach for the elbow) for the brachial artery, distal biceps reinsertion, and anterior capsulectomy. This chapter presents the surgical anatomy, landmarks, and step-by-step technique for each approach, their biomechanical consequences, and the classical Bulgarian and Russian operative tradition — which remains part of the orthopaedic curriculum in Bulgaria — alongside the modern principles of total elbow arthroplasty, collateral-ligament reconstruction, and flexion restoration.

</section>

### Bony and Articular Anatomy
<section class="topic-subsection" id="bony-articular">
<div class="ts-body">

The elbow joint comprises three articulations within a single synovial capsule. The humeroulnar joint is a hinge between the trochlea of the distal humerus and the trochlear notch of the proximal ulna; it provides flexion-extension (normal range 0–145°, functional range 30–130°). The humeroradial joint sits between the capitellum and the radial head and participates in both flexion-extension and pronation-supination. The proximal radioulnar joint is a pivot — the radial head rotates within the radial notch of the ulna, held by the annular ligament — with normal supination of 85° and pronation of 85°. The carrying angle of the extended elbow averages 11° valgus in women and 7–11° in men; loss of the angle after a supracondylar fracture with a medialised angulated distal fragment produces cubitus varus ("gunstock"), and an excessive angle produces cubitus valgus.

**Distal humerus.** Divergent medial and lateral columns whose triangular organisation around the articular block is the conceptual basis for parallel-column or orthogonal plating in distal humeral fractures. The trochlea (spool-shaped) and capitellum (rounded, spherical) are covered in cartilage and lie at approximately 25° of anterior angulation relative to the shaft axis. The olecranon fossa posteriorly receives the olecranon tip at full extension; the coronoid fossa anteriorly receives the coronoid in flexion. Approximately 1–2% of individuals have a supratrochlear foramen connecting the two fossae.

**Proximal ulna.** The olecranon (with triceps insertion), the coronoid process (an anterior bony buttress against posterior dislocation — the conceptual basis of O'Driscoll's "terrible triad"), and the trochlear notch. The "bare area" — a narrow transverse strip without articular cartilage in the middle of the trochlear notch — is the anatomically defined site for safe olecranon osteotomy in the posterior approach to the articular surface of the distal humerus. On the medial surface of the coronoid lies the sublime tubercle (tuberculum sublime), the insertion point of the anterior bundle of the medial collateral ligament.

**Proximal radius.** A disc-shaped radial head with a slightly concave articular surface meeting the capitellum, the radial neck beneath it, and the radial tuberosity — a medial-volar insertion of the distal biceps tendon. The radial head is a significant secondary stabiliser against valgus stress; excision in the presence of an incompetent MCL leads to progressive instability (Essex-Lopresti mechanism when the interosseous membrane is also disrupted, with proximal radial migration).

    <li>The distal humerus is anteverted approximately 25° relative to the shaft axis — critical for restoration in supracondylar fractures and for positioning the humeral component in total elbow arthroplasty.</li>

The capsule is thin anteriorly and posteriorly and relatively robust at its medial and lateral margins, where it thickens into the collateral ligament complexes. The synovial membrane forms recesses in the coronoid, radial, and olecranon fossae — the clinical basis for palpable effusions at the posterolateral "soft spot" (a triangle bounded by the olecranon, radial head, and lateral epicondyle, the main aspiration point).

**Medial collateral ligament complex (MCL).** Three bundles. The anterior bundle — from the inferior-anterior surface of the medial epicondyle to the sublime tubercle of the coronoid — is the primary restraint against valgus stress at mid-flexion (30–120°). This is the "Tommy John" ligament, whose chronic microtearing in overhead throwing athletes is the indication for reconstruction (Jobe 1974 or Konforti-Paneva). The posterior bundle — from the medial epicondyle to the medial olecranon — reinforces the posteromedial capsule and contributes to stability in deep flexion. The transverse (Cooper's) bundle — between olecranon and coronoid — contributes minimally to stability.

**Lateral collateral ligament complex (LCL).** The radial collateral ligament runs from the lateral epicondyle and inserts into the annular ligament. The annular ligament encircles the radial head and inserts into the anterior and posterior margins of the radial notch of the ulna, forming a V-shaped fibrous loop that retains the head in the proximal radioulnar joint. The lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL) — described as a discrete component by O'Driscoll in 1991 — originates from the lateral epicondyle at a point isometric to the axis of rotation, passes posterior to the radial head, and inserts onto the supinator crest of the ulna. LUCL is the primary restraint against posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI) — the form of chronic elbow instability following aggressive radial head excision, iatrogenic annular ligament incision above the equator, or traumatic lateral dislocation. The accessory lateral collateral ligament reinforces LUCL and runs from the inferior surface of the annular ligament to the supinator crest.

### Cubital Fossa and Vasculature
<section class="topic-subsection" id="cubital-fossa">
<div class="ts-body">

The lacertus fibrosus covers the brachial artery and median nerve as a protective sheet.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Anatomist90, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cubital_Fossa2.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The cubital fossa is a triangular space anterior to the elbow, bounded by brachioradialis laterally, pronator teres medially, and an imaginary line between the two epicondyles proximally. Its floor is formed by brachialis (medial two-thirds) and supinator (lateral third). Its roof is the deep fascia, reinforced by the lacertus fibrosus (bicipital aponeurosis), which blends with the fascia overlying the flexor-pronator mass.

Contents lateral to medial: the radial nerve with its two terminal branches (superficial — sensory; deep — the posterior interosseous nerve, PIN); the biceps tendon angled toward the radial tuberosity; the brachial artery, which bifurcates at the level of the radial neck into the radial and ulnar arteries; the median nerve, medial to the brachial artery. The radial artery continues distally beneath brachioradialis; the ulnar artery passes deep to the pronator teres arcade and enters the forearm with the median nerve between the two pronator heads. The brachial artery gives off the ulnar recurrent artery (medially, forming an anastomotic network around the medial epicondyle with the superior and inferior ulnar collateral arteries) and the radial recurrent artery (laterally, within Henry's mobile wad — between brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus, and brevis — a potential source of bleeding in anterior approaches and supinator release).

The brachial artery typically lies lateral to the median nerve in the cubital fossa, but in approximately 15–20% of individuals this relation is reversed — neurovascular identification before dissection is mandatory. About 2% have a high bifurcation of the brachial artery in the mid-arm (superficial brachial artery), in which the radial artery passes superficial to pronator teres — an anatomic surprise that may cause heavy arterial bleeding from anterior elbow incisions.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Cubital fossa contents lateral to medial: radial nerve → biceps tendon → brachial artery → median nerve.</li>
    <li>In approximately 15–20% of individuals the brachial artery lies medial to the median nerve — mandatory identification before dissection.</li>
    <li>Henry's mobile wad — brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus, and brevis as a single mobile group — is retracted laterally to separate it from the cubital fossa; the radial nerve lies in its bifurcation zone deep to the wad.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Ulnar Nerve and Cubital Tunnel
<section class="topic-subsection" id="ulnar-nerve">
<div class="ts-body">

The ulnar nerve descends from the medial cord of the brachial plexus along the anteromedial surface of biceps brachii. In the mid-arm it pierces the medial intermuscular septum (in a region known as the arcade of Struthers — a fibrous thickening in the distal third of the arm between medial triceps and the medial intermuscular septum, a potential proximal compression site) and enters the posterior compartment of the arm, lying close to the posterior surface of medial triceps. At the elbow the nerve enters the cubital tunnel — an anatomically defined channel between the medial epicondyle anteriorly and the olecranon posteriorly, with floor formed by the medial collateral ligament and roof formed by Osborne's ligament (the arcuate ligament of Osborne — a fibrous thickening between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris). After exiting the tunnel the nerve passes between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris and enters the forearm.

Within the cubital tunnel the cross-sectional area of the nerve decreases significantly in flexion due to stretching of the medial collateral ligament and Osborne's ligament — the anatomic explanation for symptom exacerbation in cubital tunnel syndrome with prolonged elbow flexion. Cubital tunnel syndrome is the second-most-common compressive neuropathy of the upper limb after carpal tunnel syndrome. Tinel's sign over the tunnel and a provocation test with sustained elbow flexion for one minute reproducing sensory symptoms in the ulnar distribution are pathognomonic. Electrodiagnostic findings: delayed nerve conduction across the elbow (below 50 m/s) and a positive "inching" test.

McGowan classification (1950) of severity: 1 — paraesthesia without motor deficit; 2 — motor weakness with intact intrinsic function; 3 — atrophy and paralysis of the intrinsic muscles.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The ulnar nerve is at risk in every medial approach to the elbow; the first step of every medial procedure is identification of the nerve in the ulnar groove behind the medial epicondyle, encirclement with a rubber loop, and gentle retraction throughout the case.</li>
    <li>With osteotomy of the medial epicondyle for an extended medial approach, the nerve is first freed from its submuscular bed; otherwise distraction of the epicondyle may tear the nerve in its subcutaneous course.</li>
    <li>In total elbow arthroplasty (Coonrad-Morrey) routine ulnar nerve transposition is standard — postoperative positional traction over the nerve from the hinge mechanism is a real risk.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>


### Median Nerve
<section class="topic-subsection" id="median-nerve">
<div class="ts-body">

The median nerve descends along the medial side of the brachial artery in the distal arm; in the cubital fossa it lies medial to the artery beneath the cover of the lacertus fibrosus. At the boundary between cubital fossa and forearm the nerve passes between the two heads of pronator teres (the humeral and ulnar heads) — a potential compression site (pronator teres syndrome), described by Seyffarth (1951) with paraesthesia in the radial three-and-a-half digits, pain along the proximal volar forearm, and a positive provocation test with resisted forearm pronation. Beyond pronator teres the nerve passes deep to the flexor digitorum superficialis arch (sublimis arch) and continues distally between flexor digitorum superficialis anteriorly and flexor digitorum profundus posteriorly.

At the elbow the nerve gives motor branches to pronator teres, flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, and flexor digitorum superficialis, as well as the anterior interosseous nerve, which descends along the anterior surface of the interosseous membrane with the anterior interosseous artery and innervates flexor pollicis longus, the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus, and pronator quadratus. Isolated compression of the anterior interosseous nerve (Kiloh-Nevin syndrome) produces a characteristic pinch weakness ("pinch sign") without sensory deficit — the nerve is entirely motor.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The lacertus fibrosus covers the brachial artery and median nerve during the anterior approach — always incise it with the sharp edge directed superficially, under direct visualisation of the underlying structures.</li>
    <li>In anterior capsulectomy for a stiff elbow the median nerve and brachial artery are identified proximally before dissection — brachialis serves as a protective layer between the neurovascular bundle and the anterior capsule.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>


### Radial Nerve and Posterior Interosseous Nerve
<section class="topic-subsection" id="radial-nerve-pin">
<div class="ts-body">

The radial nerve reaches the elbow anteriorly — after piercing the lateral intermuscular septum in the distal third of the arm (approximately 10 cm above the lateral epicondyle, transitioning from posterior to anterior compartment) — and passes through the cubital fossa in its lateral angle, between brachialis medially and brachioradialis laterally. At the level of the radial head or just proximal to it the nerve divides into its two terminal branches.

**Superficial branch** — purely sensory — continues distally beneath brachioradialis; at the boundary of the middle and distal thirds of the forearm it emerges subcutaneously between brachioradialis and extensor carpi radialis longus tendons and innervates the dorsum of the radial two-and-a-half digits (excluding their distal phalanges, which receive innervation from the median nerve).

**Deep branch — posterior interosseous nerve (PIN)** — passes through the fibrous proximal margin of supinator (the arcade of Frohse, described by H. Frohse in 1908, present as a discrete structure in 30–80% of individuals depending on the series) and continues through the muscle, exiting into the posterior compartment of the forearm beyond its distal margin (the supinator canal). Here the nerve gives motor branches to all the muscles of the posterior compartment except brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus, and anconeus (the latter receives a branch directly from the radial nerve in its proximal course).

**PIN topography relative to the radial head.** The bifurcation of the radial nerve typically lies at the level of the upper margin of supinator or just proximal to it — anterior to the joint capsule, at the level of the radiocapitellar joint. PIN enters the supinator canal approximately 2–3.5 cm distal to the radial head and exits it 6–8 cm distal to the head. This morphometry defines the safe window for distal extension of the Kocher approach.

**Influence of forearm rotation on PIN position.** Pronation rotates the proximal radius and brings PIN closer to the anterolateral surface of the radius — the nerve moves medially and into the operative field with the Kocher approach. Supination rotates the radius in the opposite direction and moves PIN laterally — away from the operative field. For this reason every dissection of the lateral surface of the radial head or proximal radius is performed with the forearm supinated, and supinator retraction with a blunt instrument is limited to the minimum required.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>PIN enters the supinator canal 2–3.5 cm distal to the radial head — the boundary of safe distal extension of the Kocher approach without visual nerve identification.</li>
    <li>Supinate the forearm during lateral dissection along the radius — pronation brings PIN into the field.</li>
    <li>In single-incision distal biceps tendon reinsertion, preparation of the radial tuberosity is a direct threat to PIN; the classical Boyd-Anderson two-incision technique distributes the risk via a separate posterolateral window.</li>
    <li>In anterior approaches to the proximal radius (Henry), detach the origin of supinator from the radial origin and work in the supinator-splitting plane with the forearm supinated; do not retract the radius laterally without visual identification of PIN.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

The motor branch to anconeus arises from the radial nerve in its proximal course in the posterior compartment of the arm and enters the muscle along its medial surface. In the Kocher interval (between anconeus posteriorly and extensor carpi ulnaris anteriorly), gentle posterior retraction of anconeus that respects this innervation is standard. The arcade of Frohse — the fibrous proximal margin of supinator — is the compression site in PIN syndrome (Kaplan, 1955), characterised by progressive extensor weakness of the fingers with preserved sensation (the nerve is purely motor distal to the bifurcation).

</div>
</section>


### Fascial Spaces and Subcutaneous Structures
<section class="topic-subsection" id="fascial-spaces">
<div class="ts-body">

The elbow region is bounded by transverse lines 4 cm above and 4 cm below the elbow crease; the lateral epicondyle lies approximately 1 cm higher than the medial epicondyle. It is divided into anterior (regio cubiti anterior) and posterior (regio cubiti posterior) regions. The skin of the anterior surface is thin, mobile, and hairless; posteriorly it is thicker and adherent to the underlying structures.

**Subcutaneous veins and nerves.** Anteriorly the basilic vein passes subcutaneously medially (accompanied by the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve — a branch from the medial cord of the brachial plexus, C8–T1, which crosses the field diagonally and on transection produces a characteristic painful neuroma over the medial epicondyle), the cephalic vein passes laterally (accompanied by the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve — the terminal sensory branch of the musculocutaneous nerve, piercing the deep fascia 4–5 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle at the lateral border of biceps), and the median cubital vein connects them diagonally across the elbow crease. The venous connections across the elbow crease are inconsistent and individually variable.

**Fascial spaces.** Three fascial spaces are recognised in the anterior elbow — anterior brachial (deep to the fascia of brachialis), lateral, and anterior antebrachial — communicating with one another via loose areolar tissue. This communication makes the elbow a preferred site for the spread of phlegmons and haematomas with potential extension toward supinator and pronator teres.

**Olecranon bursa.** A subcutaneous bursa overlying the olecranon tip — chronic mechanical stress ("student's elbow"), trauma, or rheumatoid arthritis can cause bursitis requiring operative resection. Bursectomy is performed through a lateral or medial incision near the olecranon tip (a midline incision over the tip itself creates a chronically irritated scar).

## Biomechanics
<section class="topic-section" id="biomechanics">


The elbow joint provides two independent ranges of motion: flexion-extension (humeroulnar and humeroradial articulations) and pronation-supination (proximal and distal radioulnar articulations, coupled by the interosseous membrane). Normal flexion-extension is 0–145° (some individuals — particularly children and the hypermobile — reach hyperextension to –10°). The functional arc for most daily activities is 30–130° of flexion and 50° pronation / 50° supination — the critical targets in reconstructive surgery and arthrodesis.

**Axes of rotation.** The flexion-extension axis passes approximately through the centre of the trochlea and the centre of the capitellum — a line that, in lateral view, can be identified as the presumed axis of the isometric collateral ligament insertion. This is the basis for isometric reconstruction of MCL and LUCL — the graft must be anchored at the point that does not change length through flexion and extension. The pronation-supination axis passes from the centre of the radial head to the base of the distal ulna — an axis oblique through the forearm.

**Restraints to valgus stress.** Anterior bundle of MCL — primary restraint at mid-flexion (30–120°). Radial head — significant secondary restraint; excision with an incompetent MCL leads to progressive instability. Bony geometry — minimal contribution in the normal range but critical in low-extension positions, where the trochlea and olecranon "lock" the joint.

**Restraints to varus stress.** Bony geometry (dominant factor); LCL complex (LUCL and radial collateral ligament).

**Posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI; O'Driscoll, 1991).** Described as a characteristic form of chronic instability following traumatic lateral dislocation or aggressive radial head excision with LUCL injury. O'Driscoll's pivot-shift test: the patient supine with the arm over the head, elbow at 40° flexion, valgus stress plus forearm supination — apprehension or reproducible subluxation is a positive test. PLRI is the conceptual foundation for the multi-layered classification of complex elbow instability and for the staged approach to collateral ligament reconstruction.

**Carrying angle.** The extended elbow shows a valgus carrying angle — averaging 11° in women and 7–11° in men. Reduction of the angle following a supracondylar fracture with a medialised angulated distal fragment produces cubitus varus ("gunstock") — primarily a cosmetic concern in adults but also a functional one in children due to impaired overhead grasp. An increased angle (cubitus valgus) most often results from non-union of the lateral condyle in childhood or growth disturbance — clinically important because of late ulnar neuropathy from progressive traction on the nerve ("tardy ulnar nerve palsy", Childress, 1956).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Functional arc of flexion-extension 30–130° and pronation/supination 50°/50° — targets for arthrodesis, reconstructive surgery, and postoperative rehabilitation.</li>
    <li>The isometric insertion point of MCL/LUCL on the epicondyles must coincide with the presumed axis of rotation — optimal on the posterior-inferior surface of the medial epicondyle and the centre of the lateral epicondyle.</li>
    <li>Radial head excision with incompetent MCL → progressive valgus instability and potential proximal radial migration (Essex-Lopresti syndrome with concomitant interosseous membrane disruption).</li>
  </ul>

## Landmarks and Imaging
<section class="topic-section" id="landmarks-imaging">


**Palpable landmarks.** Medial epicondyle and lateral epicondyle — the two main lateral landmarks. Olecranon — the posterior tip. Triangular relationship: at 90° flexion the three landmarks (medial epicondyle, lateral epicondyle, olecranon) form an equilateral triangle; at full extension — a straight line. Violation of this relationship is a sign of elbow dislocation or supracondylar fracture. The "soft spot" — a depression bounded by the olecranon, radial head, and lateral epicondyle in the posterolateral region — is the main point for arthrocentesis and for insufflation before arthroscopy.

The radial head is palpated during forearm rotation as a rotating structure under the skin 2–3 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle. The biceps tendon is palpated in the middle of the elbow crease, tensed with 90° flexion against resisted supination.

**Imaging.** Standard radiographs — anteroposterior and lateral projections. Mandatory interpretation lines: the radiocapitellar line — the long axis of the radius should always point through the centre of the capitellum (violation → radial head dislocation, Monteggia in children); the anterior humeral line — on the lateral projection, a line along the anterior cortex of the distal humerus passes through the middle or anterior third of the capitellum (violation → supracondylar fracture with dorsal displacement); the fat-pad signs — a visible anterior fat pad is normal; a visible posterior fat pad is always pathological, a sign of joint effusion or occult fracture.

CT is the standard preoperative imaging for complex intra-articular fractures of the distal humerus, the coronoid, and the radial head — 3D reconstructions are determinative for planning column osteosynthesis and for the trajectory of interfragmentary compression screws. MRI is the standard for chronic soft-tissue injury: MCL microtears in throwing athletes, LCL complex in PLRI, distal biceps tendon, osteochondritis of the capitellum (Panner's disease in children, osteochondritis dissecans in adolescents).

## Classification of Approaches
<section class="topic-section" id="classification">


Surgical approaches to the elbow are classified along two intersecting axes.

**By incision location:** lateral (Kocher; or splitting through extensor digitorum communis); posterior (midline with triceps-splitting; paratricipital medial or lateral; Bryan-Morrey reflecting; chevron olecranon osteotomy); medial (along the medial surface, with or without osteotomy of the medial epicondyle); anterior (Henry for the elbow, typically a "lazy S" incision across the cubital fossa).

**By goal:** exposure of the distal humeral articular surface (posterior with olecranon osteotomy is standard); exposure of the radial head and lateral column (Kocher); exposure of the medial epicondyle and MCL (medial); exposure of the brachial artery and median nerve (anterior); combined exposure for complex elbow trauma (posterior with lateral + medial pockets through a single posterior incision).

The choice of approach is determined by the location of the pathology, the planned procedure (nerve decompression, ligament reconstruction, osteosynthesis, arthrodesis, arthroplasty, replacement), concomitant injury (open fracture, soft-tissue loss), and the surgeon's experience. Hoppenfeld's principle that "the penalty of the approach must not exceed the benefit of the procedure" remains directive; extended approaches (combined anterior + posterior for vascular reconstruction, posterior with double osteotomy for total elbow replacement) are reserved for cases with a firm indication.

## Treatment
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">



### Nonoperative
<section class="topic-subsection" id="nonoperative">
<div class="ts-body">

Nonoperative management remains first-line for most elbow pathology without structural injury. Simple elbow dislocation without concomitant fracture is reduced under sedation or general anaesthesia with traction and angulation correction; stability after reduction is tested through the full arc of flexion — a stable elbow is splinted for 7–10 days and mobilised early. Lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow") and medial epicondylitis ("golfer's elbow") are managed conservatively for the first 6–12 months with rest, NSAIDs, an extensor unloading brace, and in recalcitrant cases — local corticosteroid injections (with caution because of soft-tissue atrophy and a paradoxical reduction in long-term efficacy with repeated injections). Cubital tunnel syndrome in McGowan grade 1 (paraesthesia only) — conservative therapy with night-time splinting in semi-extension, avoidance of prolonged flexion at work and sleep, physiotherapy.

Splint and cast immobilisation at the elbow has specific rules: prolonged immobilisation beyond 3 weeks in an adult is a direct cause of reversible — and beyond that, irreversible — stiffness. Early active mobilisation (with protected orthosis in unstable constructs) is critical for functional outcome in every elbow procedure.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The elbow is "stiffness-prone" with immobilisation beyond 3 weeks in an adult — the postoperative programme always includes early active mobilisation in a protected arc.</li>
    <li>Local corticosteroid injections in lateral epicondylitis give rapid short-term relief but show no proven long-term benefit over observation; limit to 2 injections in recalcitrant cases.</li>
    <li>After closed reduction of an elbow dislocation, check stability through the full flexion arc; valgus or varus drift beyond 30° from mid-range — consider early surgical repair of LUCL or MCL.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Operative
<section class="topic-subsection" id="operative">
<div class="ts-body">

<details>
<summary>Kocher Lateral Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical lateral approach to the elbow, described by Emil Theodor Kocher (1911) and accepted as standard for the radial head, capitellum, lateral column of the distal humerus, lateral collateral ligament complex, and posterolateral capsule.

**Indications.** Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) or excision/replacement of radial head fracture; ORIF of capitellum fracture; LUCL reconstruction for posterolateral rotatory instability; removal of loose bodies; synovectomy (as part of a multi-approach procedure).

**Positioning.** The patient supine with the injured limb on a hand table or arm board; alternatively lateral decubitus with the arm in front of the body. Tourniquet on the proximal arm (for non-proximal procedures).

**Landmarks.** Lateral epicondyle — primary; tip of the olecranon; the radial head — palpable during passive forearm rotation 2–3 cm distal to the epicondyle.

**Skin incision and internervous plane.** A 4–6 cm incision proximal to the lateral epicondyle continuing to 4–6 cm distal to the radial head, along the line between the two landmarks; total length 8–12 cm. After opening the deep fascia the Kocher interval between anconeus (posterior, innervated by a branch of the radial nerve in its proximal course) and extensor carpi ulnaris (anterior, innervated by PIN) is identified — a true plane between two nerve territories, from which the approach takes its name. The interval is developed bluntly in proximal and distal directions, exposing the lateral joint capsule.

**Surgical technique.** For the radial head and proximal radius — the annular ligament is identified and incised (anterior to LUCL, below the equator, to avoid iatrogenic posterolateral rotatory instability). The radial head and capitellum become directly visible. For more proximal exposure (capitellum, lateral epicondyle, lateral column) the incision is extended proximally with detachment of the common extensor origin from the lateral epicondyle; LUCL remains attached to the posterior-inferior surface of the epicondyle and must be preserved during dissection, otherwise a systematic reconstruction is performed at closure. For more distal exposure (proximal radius, supinator) the dissection is extended distally with the forearm supinated, to shift PIN laterally out of the field; do not extend beyond 2–3 cm distal to the radial head without visual identification of the nerve.

**Dangers.** PIN — the principal neurological concern; supination of the forearm during dissection shifts it laterally, aggressive supinator retraction or dissection beyond 2–3.5 cm from the radial head are direct threats. LUCL — annular ligament incision above the equator, or detachment of LUCL from the posterior-inferior surface of the epicondyle without planned reconstruction → posterolateral rotatory instability. Lateral collateral ligament complex — in extended proximal approaches with detachment of the common extensor origin, the integrity of LUCL must be preserved. Lateral branch of the radial recurrent artery in the proximal angle of the wound — typically ligated without consequence.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The Kocher interval (anconeus posteriorly, extensor carpi ulnaris anteriorly) is a true plane between two nerve territories — the standard lateral window.</li>
    <li>Incise the annular ligament below the equator (anterior to LUCL) — above the equator you risk LUCL and posterolateral rotatory instability.</li>
    <li>Supinate the forearm during distal dissection — PIN shifts laterally, away from the field.</li>
    <li>An alternative is the extensor digitorum communis (EDC) splitting approach — more direct to the anterior surface of the radial head, with the same PIN considerations.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Classical anatomical rules (Bulgarian school).** The Bulgarian operative school codifies three rules valid in every extended lateral approach. First: immediately proximal to the upper border of supinator, anterior to the joint capsule, the radial nerve divides into superficial and deep branches — the bifurcation point is critical for identifying PIN before it enters supinator. Second: the deep branch enters the supinator canal approximately 2–3.5 cm distal to the radial head; extension of the distal dissection beyond this border without supination and without visual control is a direct threat to the nerve. Third: the superficial branch passes distally between brachioradialis and extensor carpi radialis longus and must be identified when extending the incision caudally.

<details>
<summary>Posterior Approaches to the Elbow</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Posterior approaches are discussed in detail in the context of the distal humerus in [Trauma 15](/en/trauma/15/) (distal humerus, olecranon, radial head fractures). Here we summarise the four variants with their anatomical considerations and indications.

**Positioning for all posterior approaches.** Lateral decubitus with the arm resting on a support across the chest; or prone with the arm hanging laterally. Tourniquet on the proximal arm. Skin incision along the midline of the subcutaneous olecranon plaque, skirting the tip laterally (to avoid a scar on the tip that is irritated by armrests), with extension along the posterior border of the ulna.

**Triceps-splitting approach.** Longitudinal split of the triceps from the musculotendinous junction down to the olecranon insertion. Exposure is direct to the olecranon fossa and posterior joint capsule; limited at the distal humerus to the posterior half of the columns. Used for biopsy, ORIF of simple supracondylar fracture without intra-articular extension, and selected tumour resection. Closure restores triceps with a robust tendinous suture.

**Paratricipital approaches (Alonso-Llames, medial or lateral).** Triceps retraction medially or laterally with complete preservation of the olecranon insertion. Exposure is more limited than the splitting approach but preserves the extensor mechanism — appropriate for total elbow arthroplasty (eliminating the need to protect a repaired triceps), for ORIF of relatively simple extra-articular fractures, and for combined interposition arthroplasty.

**Bryan-Morrey reflecting triceps approach (1982).** Detachment of the triceps insertion from the olecranon together with a thin osteoperiosteal sleeve (with the underlying anconeus fascia and ulnar periosteum) and proximal reflection. Exposure is wider than the paratricipital, with preservation of the triceps muscle itself. Closure restores the insertion via two transverse osseous tunnels in the olecranon with non-absorbable sutures. The standard approach for total elbow arthroplasty at Mayo Clinic; postoperative extensor strength (against gravity) may remain subnormal in approximately 10–15% of patients.

**Olecranon osteotomy approach.** Chevron or transverse osteotomy of the olecranon in the "bare area" (the narrow transverse strip without articular cartilage in the middle of the trochlear notch); the olecranon with the attached triceps is reflected proximally, providing maximal exposure of the articular surface of the distal humerus. Standard for complex intra-articular distal humeral fractures (AO type C3) and for selected total elbow arthroplasty in marked contracture. Closure restores the osteotomy with Weber tension-band wiring or an intramedullary compression screw; non-union of the osteotomy occurs in 1–5% and is a known complication.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Olecranon osteotomy in the "bare area" — the narrow transverse strip without articular cartilage in the middle of the trochlear notch — provides maximal exposure of the distal humeral articular surface and is the standard for AO type C3 fractures.</li>
    <li>The Bryan-Morrey reflecting approach is the standard for total elbow arthroplasty at Mayo Clinic; mandatory identification and gentle protection of the ulnar nerve before dissection.</li>
    <li>In all posterior approaches — routine identification and gentle protection of the ulnar nerve in the ulnar groove behind the medial epicondyle; in total elbow arthroplasty — submuscular transposition to the anteromedial bed.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Classical Bulgarian posterior midline approach (Boychev).** The patient is supine or semi-lateral with the limb placed transversely across the chest. External landmarks are the terminal triceps tendon, the olecranon, and the ulnar groove. The skin incision begins 6–7 cm proximal to the olecranon, runs distally along the midline, reaches the tip of the olecranon, skirts it laterally, and continues 5–6 cm distally along the posterior border of the ulna. After fascial opening the distal triceps and the periosteum of the distal humerus are elevated subperiosteally, and the joint is opened in the olecranon fossa region. For triceps lengthening in flexion contracture a triangular tendinous flap with its base at the olecranon and a proximally directed apex is fashioned; after contracture correction the flap is sutured in Z-form with resultant tendon lengthening. With an olecranon osteotomy variant the olecranon borders are released subperiosteally, the osteotomy is performed at the base (at the level of the "bare area") and the osteoperiosteal flap with the attached triceps is reflected proximally, widely exposing the posterior joint and the supracondylar region. At closure the osteotomy is synthesised with a screw or with Weber tension-band wiring. The anatomical limit for proximal extension is set by the radial nerve crossing the lateral intermuscular septum (approximately 10 cm above the lateral epicondyle) — beyond this point the dissection is limited in order to avoid the nerve.

<details>
<summary>Medial Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The medial approach provides access to the medial epicondyle, the medial collateral complex, the ulnar nerve, and the medial coronoid.

**Indications.** MCL reconstruction (Tommy John procedure or classical Konforti-Paneva); fixation of a medial coronoid fracture (particularly the anteromedial facet pattern described by O'Driscoll, discussed in [Trauma 15](/en/trauma/15/)); ulnar nerve transposition; cubital tunnel decompression; release of an entrapped medial epicondyle in a child ("locked" medial epicondyle); Hotchkiss "over the top" medial coronoid approach.

**Positioning.** The patient supine with the arm on a hand table, elbow in supination and abduction (medial elbow open).

**Landmarks.** Medial epicondyle — primary; the ulnar groove on its posterior surface; the sublime tubercle of the coronoid — palpable over the proximal ulnar metaphysis.

**Skin incision.** A longitudinal or slightly curved incision from 5 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle, extending distally for approximately 8–10 cm centred over the epicondyle. Subcutaneous dissection with identification of the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve (typically crossing the field diagonally) and gentle preservation of its branches.

**Surgical technique and internervous plane.** Proximally the medial intermuscular septum is identified; the ulnar nerve is identified behind the medial epicondyle, isolated with a rubber loop, and retracted posteriorly throughout the procedure. The flexor-pronator mass is separated according to goal: Jobe's "muscle-splitting" approach — a vertical incision through the common flexor origin with separation of flexor carpi ulnaris fibres (more conservative); classical "detach" — complete detachment of the common flexor origin from the epicondyle with distal reflection. For more extended access — osteotomy of the medial epicondyle with distal reflection of the entire flexor-pronator mass (see below).

**Dangers.** Ulnar nerve — the principal neurological concern; identification and protection throughout; cubital tunnel decompression includes release of Osborne's ligament (the arcuate ligament between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris). Medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve — branches cross the field subcutaneously; transection produces a painful neuroma over the medial epicondyle. Flexor carpi ulnaris and the common flexor mass — disruption of the insertion without adequate reattachment produces decreased grip strength and medial pain.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The ulnar nerve is identified and protected as the first step in every medial approach; never cut or retract "blindly" near the medial epicondyle.</li>
    <li>The medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve is a diffusely arborising structure — branches cross the field diagonally; identify and protect every visible branch to avoid painful neuroma.</li>
    <li>A "locked" medial epicondyle after a paediatric dislocation requires mandatory release — left intra-articular, the bony fragment produces stiffness, cartilage degeneration, and late tardy ulnar nerve palsy.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Classical medial approach with medial epicondyle osteotomy (Bulgarian school).** An extended medial approach in which the medial epicondyle is osteotomised and reflected distally and laterally together with the attached flexor-pronator mass. First the ulnar nerve is identified and palpated in the ulnar groove, the fascia over it is carefully incised, the nerve is isolated with a rubber loop, and it is retracted posteriorly. Then the medial border of flexor carpi ulnaris is elevated and the underlying ulnar nerve is protected. The lateral border of pronator teres is elevated toward the medial epicondyle for visualisation of the median nerve, its flexor branches, and the brachial artery. The medial epicondyle is osteotomised obliquely — the osteotomy preserves the medial collateral ligament insertion on the distal bony fragment — and reflected distally together with the flexor-pronator mass. The approach may be extended subperiosteally beneath brachialis to expose the anterior joint capsule and the anterior supracondylar region; the brachial vessels and median nerve remain protected by the overlying brachialis. At closure the osteotomised epicondyle is fixed with a screw. The approach is used in the classical removal of an entrapped medial epicondyle in a child, in the Hotchkiss medial approach to the coronoid, and in total elbow arthroplasty with medial exposure.

<details>
<summary>Anterior Approach (Henry for the Elbow)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The anterior approach is used for vascular injury (brachial artery), reinsertion of the distal biceps tendon from a single anterior window, anterior capsulectomy for the stiff elbow, and access to the coronoid along its anteromedial corner.

**Positioning.** The patient supine with the injured limb on a hand table, forearm supinated.

**Landmarks.** Elbow crease, medial epicondyle, lateral epicondyle, lateral border of biceps brachii (where the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve emerges).

**Skin incision.** "Lazy S" or "lazy J", crossing the elbow crease at an angle to avoid scar contracture. Begins over the biceps tendon in the distal arm, crosses the elbow crease diagonally, and turns distally along the medial border of brachioradialis.

**Surgical technique and internervous plane.** Skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised; the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is identified 4–5 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle and preserved. The lacertus fibrosus (bicipital aponeurosis) is identified and incised, providing access to the cubital fossa. The following are identified (lateral to medial): the radial nerve with its two branches (at the level of the radial head), the biceps tendon (heading toward the radial tuberosity), the brachial artery with its branches, and the median nerve. Brachialis separates the brachial vessels and median nerve from the joint capsule.

For distal biceps tendon reinsertion the tendon is freed from its retracted position, whipstitched with a Bunnell crossed suture, and reattached to the radial tuberosity. The Boyd-Anderson two-incision technique (1961) — anterior incision for tendon preparation and a second posterolateral window over the radial tuberosity — reduces the risk to PIN during preparation of the posterior tuberosity. A single anterior approach with cortical button fixation or interference screws is the modern standard; PIN remains at risk during drill perforation of the dorsal cortex of the radius.

**Dangers.** Median nerve — the central neurological concern; identification and protection proximally before dissection. Brachial artery — routine identification and vascular control before dividing the lacertus. Lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve — subcutaneous; transection → painful neuroma. PIN — during single-incision distal biceps reinsertion with tuberosity preparation. Brachialis — the separating layer between the neurovascular bundle and the anterior capsule; keep it intact during anterior capsulectomy.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>"Lazy S" incision — never a vertical line across the elbow crease, because of scar contracture and flexion contracture.</li>
    <li>The lacertus fibrosus overlies the brachial artery and median nerve — always incise it with the sharp edge directed upward under direct visualisation.</li>
    <li>In single-incision distal biceps reinsertion, drilling two corridors in the radius endangers PIN as the dorsal cortex is perforated; the classical Boyd-Anderson two-incision technique distributes the risk via a separate posterolateral window.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Ulnar Nerve Decompression and Transposition</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The cubital tunnel is approached through a longitudinal incision behind the medial epicondyle. All five potential compression sites are identified and released from proximal to distal: the arcade of Struthers (a fibrous thickening in the distal third of the arm between the medial triceps and the medial intermuscular septum), the medial intermuscular septum, muscular hypertrophy (anconeus epitrochlearis — a variant muscle present in approximately 10% of individuals, replacing Osborne's ligament), the cubital tunnel itself (with Osborne's ligament as its roof), and the arcade between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris distal to the tunnel. In situ decompression includes incision of Osborne's ligament from proximal to distal with control of the sensory branches to flexor carpi ulnaris.

**Anterior transposition** — additional mobilisation of the nerve from the cubital tunnel and transfer anterior to the medial epicondyle. Subcutaneous: the nerve is placed in subcutaneous tissue anterior to the epicondyle; sometimes a fascial sling or tendinous strap is used to maintain position. Submuscular (Learmonth, 1942): the flexor-pronator origin is detached, the nerve is placed deep to the muscle on brachialis, and the muscle is reattached over the nerve — better protection, more extensive dissection. Intramuscular: the nerve is placed in a groove fashioned in the flexor-pronator mass — rarely used. Medial epicondylectomy — an alternative procedure in which the medial epicondyle is removed, allowing the nerve to lie anteriorly by default; out of use because of concerns over MCL function and persistent pain.

**The classical Bulgarian submuscular transposition (Learmonth variant with fascial-fat flap).** In incipient ulnar palsy from valgus deformity or cicatricial compression after epicondylar fracture: a longitudinal incision along the course of the nerve from the distal third of the arm to the proximal third of the forearm; the deep fascia over the medial epicondyle is incised and the bed of the nerve is opened proximally-to-distally. In the distal part of the incision a few fibres of flexor carpi ulnaris are divided to release the nerve from the arcuate ligament of Osborne. A curved Terrier instrument is passed beneath the insertions of the flexor-pronator mass, the deep intermuscular septum from the medial epicondyle is split, creating a bed over brachialis into which the ulnar nerve is placed without tension or kinking. The detached muscles are sutured back over the nerve, and the limb is immobilised for approximately 3 weeks in semi-flexion.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Classical Procedures for Lateral and Medial Epicondylitis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Chronic tendinosis of the common extensor origin at the lateral epicondyle — clinically known as "tennis elbow" or Runge's epicondylitis — rarely requires surgery, but with failure of 6–12 months of conservative management the classical operative procedures remain relevant.

**Hohmann procedure (G. Hohmann, 1926).** Simple detachment of the common extensor origin from the lateral epicondyle with light burnishing of the bony relief — a technique that releases chronic tension on the tendon.

**Bosworth procedure (D. M. Bosworth, 1955).** A more extensive procedure with detachment of the extensors, partial resection of the annular ligament (anterior-lateral one-third), removal of the hypertrophied semilunar synovial fold between the capitellum and the radial head, and burnishing of the epicondyle with an osteotome. According to the original description, division of the annular ligament or even excision of one-third of its anterior-lateral portion does not produce significant instability, and reinsertion of the extensors into a freshened bony bed is recommended but not essential.

**Nirschl procedure.** The modern equivalent — more conservative, targeting only the pathological extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon. The ECRB tendon is identified, the degenerative tissue is excised, the bed is freshened, and the tendon is reinserted into the freshened bony bed. Postoperative early mobilisation is standard.

In medial epicondylitis ("golfer's elbow") an analogous procedure includes detachment of the common flexor origin with tangential resection of the degenerative tendon, with primary concern for protecting the ulnar nerve in the adjacent cubital tunnel — in approximately 20% of patients a concomitant in situ decompression or transposition is performed.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>MCL Reconstruction — Tommy John and Konforti-Paneva</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Tommy John procedure (F. W. Jobe, 1974) reconstructs the anterior bundle of the medial collateral ligament in throwing athletes with chronic microtearing or acute rupture from baseball pitching. A free tendon graft — typically palmaris longus, alternatively gracilis or semitendinosus when palmaris is absent — is passed through bone tunnels in the medial epicondyle and the sublime tubercle of the coronoid in a figure-of-eight configuration. The anchor points must coincide with the isometric zone of the native ligament: the posterior-inferior surface of the medial epicondyle and the sublime tubercle. The graft is tensioned at 90° flexion and mid-rotation. The postoperative programme is protected and prolonged — full return to professional pitching at 12–18 months.

**The classical Konforti-Paneva technique.** The Bulgarian tradition describes reconstruction of the medial collateral ligament in a lax elbow after resection or arthroplasty: a free tendon graft passed through a transverse drill tunnel in the distal humeral metaphysis and through two slightly oblique tunnels in the proximal ulna — one on the anteromedial and one on the posteromedial surface. The graft is tensioned at 90° flexion and mid-rotation. In both approaches success depends on accurate identification of the isometric zone of the anterior bundle insertion — the posterior-inferior part of the medial epicondyle, just above the axis of elbow rotation.

For lateral instability following post-traumatic LUCL injury (posterolateral rotatory instability per O'Driscoll), reconstruction uses a free graft passed from the isometric point of the lateral epicondyle to the supinator crest in a similar figure-of-eight configuration — the anatomical lateral parallel of the Tommy John procedure.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The isometric MCL insertion point on the medial epicondyle is the posterior-inferior surface, just above the axis of rotation — reconstruction outside this zone produces either stiffness or laxity.</li>
    <li>Standard graft — palmaris longus (present in 85% of individuals); alternatives — gracilis, semitendinosus, an Achilles tendon strip.</li>
    <li>The classical figure-of-eight gives the most uniform tension distribution; alternative "docking" techniques with a single bone tunnel in the epicondyle are newer with similar outcomes.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Distal Biceps Tendon Reinsertion</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Distal biceps tendon avulsion from the radial tuberosity typically occurs with an extreme eccentric load on a flexed elbow in a middle-aged man. Clinically — "Popeye" deformity (proximal retraction of the biceps belly with visible asymmetry compared with the contralateral side), weakened supination (biceps is a stronger supinator than flexor), and a palpable defect in the cubital fossa where the tendon is normally tensed with 90° flexion against resisted supination.

**Classical Boyd-Anderson two-incision technique (1961).** Anterior incision for identification and preparation of the retracted tendon — whipstitching with a Bunnell crossed suture. A blunt instrument is passed between the radius and ulna at the level of the tuberosity. A second posterolateral incision over the radial tuberosity is performed with the forearm supinated (to protect PIN); the tendon is sutured to the freshened bony bed of the tuberosity. The elbow is immobilised at 90° flexion and mid-rotation for 3 weeks.

**Modern single anterior approach.** Cortical button fixation (EndoButton) or interference screws reduce the need for a second posterior incision. Tuberosity preparation is performed through the anterior approach — drill perforation with risk to PIN; forearm supination shifts the nerve maximally lateral. Outcomes are equivalent to the two-incision technique, but the risk of PIN injury and of heterotopic ossification in the proximal radioulnar joint (approximately 5–10% with the two-incision technique, lower with the single incision) remain individually prognosticated.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Olecranon Fixation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

  <figcaption>

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · James Heilman, MD, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Olecranon_Fracture_X-Ray.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>

Olecranon fixation with tension-band wiring per Weber (M. E. Müller, M. Allgöwer, R. Schneider, H. Willenegger, AO, 1969) is the standard treatment for simple transverse and short oblique displaced olecranon fractures. Through a posterior incision over the olecranon tip the fracture is exposed, reduction is verified, and two parallel Kirschner wires are passed through the triceps insertion such that their tips just penetrate the opposite cortex of the ulna distal to the fracture line. A transverse drill hole is made in the distal fragment 3–4 cm distal to the fracture line; a stainless steel wire is passed through this hole and around the proximal ends of the K-wires in a figure-of-eight configuration. It is twisted laterally with figure-of-eight tension — the flexion pull of triceps is converted into compressive force across the articular surface (Pauwels' tension principle). The K-wires are bent caudally and turned so they do not penetrate the skin in flexion.

**Nachev's alternative technique.** Intramedullary compression screw — a single proximal opening of the olecranon, canal reaming, and oblique insertion of a long compression screw from the tip of the olecranon into the medullary canal of the ulna; applicable in larger distal fragments with sufficient metaphyseal bone stock.

In comminuted fractures or fractures with small fragments at the olecranon tip (involving no more than one-third of the bone), the fragments are excised and the triceps is sutured directly to the freshened residual bony bed with a transosseous suture or anchor. This resection strategy is unacceptable in distal fragments that would jeopardise anterior-posterior stability of the ulnohumeral joint (there — plate osteosynthesis with anteriorly placed plates per Müller).

</section>

<details>
<summary>Elbow Flexion Restoration (Steindler, Clark, Hovnanian)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

With complete loss of elbow flexion (traumatic brachial plexus palsy, post-polio paralysis) a series of classical muscle transfers were developed and remain relevant where modern nerve reconstructions are unavailable. A prerequisite for success is active shoulder motion (or prior shoulder arthrodesis in moderate abduction) and preserved hand grasp function.

**Pectoralis major transfer (Clark, modified by Merle d'Aubigné and Postel).** Applied in brachial plexus palsy with preserved most-distal roots (C8–T1), since the distal pectoralis major receives innervation from these roots and has an independent neurovascular pedicle. In Clark's original description the belly of the distal pectoralis is mobilised from the chest wall with preservation of the humeral insertion; Merle d'Aubigné and Postel showed that a better functional result is obtained with complete transfer of the free neurovascular bundle, sutured to the biceps tendon near its insertion on the radial tuberosity at 120° elbow flexion and moderate belly tension. The limb is immobilised for 3 weeks in full shoulder adduction and 150° elbow flexion, followed by a further 3 weeks in a sling at 90° flexion.

**Latissimus dorsi transfer (Hovnanian).** For more extensive paralysis. A curved incision begins at the level of the axillary fossa along the anteromedial surface of the arm and in the proximal part curves outward approximately along the course of pronator teres. Latissimus dorsi is dissected off the chest wall with preservation of the overlying fascia and the thoracodorsal neurovascular pedicle, detached at both its ends (humerus and lumbodorsal fascia), and transferred through a subcutaneous tunnel to the biceps tendon. Schottstaedt's modification adds reattachment of the proximal end to the coracoid process for stabilisation and improved lever arm. The same principle is applied to extension restoration — transfer of latissimus to the triceps in extensive post-polio paralysis, particularly in patients who must use walking aids.

**Steindler flexorplasty (A. Steindler, 1918).** The same general principle: proximal transfer of the common flexor origin from the medial epicondyle to the distal third of the arm, so that wrist and finger flexors become secondary elbow flexors. The patient is supine, a curved incision is made over the medial epicondyle, and the common flexor mass is osteotomised together with a thin bony lamina from the epicondyle; the bony fragment is reattached to the anterior surface of the humerus approximately 7–8 cm above the elbow with preservation of the ulnar nerve in its new position. With pre-existing flexion contracture the procedure loses effectiveness, so passive extension must be restored preoperatively. Reattachment to the anterolateral, rather than anteromedial, surface minimises concurrent pronation with flexion — the classical Steindler effect described by Bunnell.

**Conversion of biceps from supinator to pronator.** With a non-functioning biceps in the presence of preserved triceps, the function of biceps can be reversed. Detachment of the distal tendon from the radial tuberosity, passage around the radial neck in the opposite direction, and reattachment at the same site. During flexion the muscle now produces pronation instead of supination — particularly important in children with obstetric palsy with a fixed supinator contracture and in post-polio sequelae. If the forearm has a fixed supinator contracture, release is first performed by dividing the interosseous membrane along its entire length close to the ulna (with gentle dorsal muscle displacement to protect PIN), detaching supinator from the radius, and, if necessary, distal radioulnar capsulotomy.

Prognostically, active elbow flexion after passive flexion to 90° in patients with palsy is a sign that the preserved active flexion strength is sufficient for some functional needs and is an argument against transfer.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Elbow Arthrodesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Elbow arthrodesis is relatively rarely performed for aesthetic and functional reasons but retains an indication in heavy manual workers with severely destroyed joints, particularly in bilateral upper limb involvement where at least one elbow must remain mobile. Bony union between the fragments is difficult to achieve because of the dependent position of the limb — for this reason classical techniques use autografts. Before arthrodesis the radial head is always excised, otherwise rotational motion of the forearm may be blocked at the proximal radioulnar joint.

**Putti-Wittek arthrodesis.** Intramedullary bone graft between humerus and ulna.

**Brittain arthrodesis.** A transarticular tricortical tibial graft driven through the humerus into the proximal ulna.

**Campbell arthrodesis.** An angled corticoperiosteal graft fixed with cerclage.

**Steindler arthrodesis.** An inverted olecranon impacted into the distal humerus for biomechanical stability and improved bony surface for union.

**Charnley compression arthrodesis.** Steinmann pins and compression staples — abandoned because of the bulkiness of the pins and the lower stability of the construct.

**Ilizarov compression arthrodesis (modern standard).** Two parallel K-wires are passed through the humerus 4–5 cm apart and fixed to upper half-rings; two K-wires are passed through the ulna — one along the joint axis, one slightly more distal — fixed to lower half-rings. Compression is applied with infinite screws between the half-rings; cast immobilisation is usually not required, and union typically occurs in about 2 months.

The optimal position for arthrodesis is 70–90° of flexion, mid-rotation of the forearm.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Interposition Arthroplasty and Total Elbow Arthroplasty</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Interposition arthroplasty (before the era of reliable replacement).** In the typical Hass arthroplasty (or its Konforti-Paneva modification of the Bulgarian school) a posterior incision is used, the triceps fibres are split, the joint is opened, the bony surfaces are remodelled (the distal humerus is resected 1–1.5 cm in cylindrical form, the ulnar articular surface is recessed), the radial head is resected, and a free fascia lata graft (5–7 cm wide, about 20 cm long) is placed in interposition — half of it wraps the distal humerus and is fixed to transverse cortical channels. The classical "resection arthroplasty" is simpler — the articular surfaces are resected transversely with a defect of about 3 cm, without interposition, forming an unstable but mobile elbow; the indication is in severe septic or tuberculous destruction where stability is not a priority.

**Hemiarthroplasty (Bulgarian school, Paneva).** In isolated destruction of the distal humeral surface alone with a preserved ulna — the distal humerus is resected 1–1.5 cm and remodelled to the shape of the trochlear notch of the ulna, after which the surface is covered with a fascia lata graft. Autotransplantation of the distal humeral articular surface (Paneva) — in severe stiffness with complete loss of motion but with preserved articular congruity: the distal fragment is resected together with the cartilage, the proximal fragment is shaped and impacted into the harvested allograft, fixation with 2 K-wires.

**Hemireplacement of the distal humerus — Konforti-Paneva (modification of Bakeen-Vainio).** Posterior Campbell approach, subperiosteal exposure of the distal humerus, and placement of a cylindrical Kirschner wire transversely through the metaphysis at 90° to the shaft axis as an axis of rotation. The bone around the wire is shaped as a cylinder with a diameter slightly smaller than the inner diameter of the polyethylene prosthesis, which is then carefully impacted from ulnar to radial. Impaction must be firm enough that cementing is not required. An analogous procedure is performed for radial head replacement — indicated in comminuted Mason 4 fractures in adults, particularly when associated with Essex-Lopresti or complex elbow instability, in which simple excision is unsuitable.

**Hinged semi-constrained replacement — Coonrad-Morrey.** The modern standard for total elbow arthroplasty in rheumatoid arthritis and complex post-traumatic destruction. Both components (humeral and ulnar) have intramedullary stems, usually cement-fixed, coupled by a hinge mechanism with a small degree of varus-valgus and rotational tolerance (semi-constrained design), which distributes rotational forces to the soft tissues and reduces loosening. Approach is posterior paratricipital or Bryan-Morrey reflecting triceps; the radial head is resected; the humerus is opened immediately above the olecranon fossa, the ulna about 1 cm from the coronoid process. The canals are prepared approximately 2 mm wider than the stems and about 1 cm longer to allow space for bone cement. The ulnar nerve is transposed routinely (see above).

**Unlinked surface replacements (Souter-Strathclyde, Kudo).** Biomechanically gentler on the soft tissues, but require a competent ligament complex and competent collaterals — not applicable in severe destruction with ligamentous insufficiency.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Hinged semi-constrained replacement in a young active patient — a real risk of loosening and mid-to-long-term failure; reserve for patients over 65 years with rheumatoid arthritis or severe post-traumatic destruction.</li>
    <li>Unlinked surface replacements require competent collateral ligaments — for osteoarthritis with preserved ligamentous stability, not for rheumatoid arthritis with severe destruction.</li>
    <li>Routine ulnar nerve transposition in total elbow arthroplasty — postoperative positional traction over the hinge mechanism is a real risk.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Correction of Cubitus Varus and Valgus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Cubitus varus (gunstock deformity) is a classical late complication of poorly reduced supracondylar or transcondylar fracture in childhood. Cubitus valgus most commonly results from non-union of the lateral condyle or growth disturbance of the distal humerus. Corrective supracondylar osteotomy is performed through a lateral approach for varus and a medial approach for valgus. After subperiosteal exposure of the distal humerus (approximately 4 cm) at a standardised distance above the olecranon fossa, a wedge osteotomy is planned — a laterally based open wedge for varus and a medially based open (or laterally based closed) wedge for valgus. The slightly wider dorsal wedge corrects the concurrent extension limitation. Fixation is with 1–2 K-wires with tips left under the skin (particular care in the paediatric patient not to penetrate the growth plate). With cubitus valgus correction the ulnar nerve must be identified, its bed opened, and the nerve retracted before osteotomy — the motivation for correction is often prevention of tardy ulnar nerve palsy rather than purely cosmetic.

A comparative radiograph of both elbows is mandatory for accurate planning of the correction angle.

</div>
</section>

The classical indication for radial head excision is a comminuted Mason 3–4 fracture in the adult with comminution that makes reduction and fixation infeasible, and without significant Essex-Lopresti or concomitant instability (which requires preservation of the radial buttress through reconstruction or replacement). The approach is Kocher; the annular ligament is opened or resected, and the head is excised at the level of the radial neck. Operative reduction of a chronically dislocated radial head (unreduced 2–6 weeks) is performed through a posterolateral approach: the joint capsule is opened, the dislocated head is reduced with gentle elevators with concurrent elbow flexion; if the annular ligament is repairable, it is sutured, otherwise the reconstruction uses a free tendon graft passed through a transverse channel in the posterior border of the proximal ulna at the level of the radial neck. Hohmann's classical technique uses a pedicled tendon autograft from triceps for annular ligament reconstruction. In cases beyond 6 months of injury significant soft-tissue changes make operative reduction impractical — the alternative is head resection or replacement.

</section>

<details>
<summary>Bloody Reduction of Elbow Dislocation and Arthrolysis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In chronic unreduced dislocations (over 2–3 weeks in adults; regardless of duration in children), bloody reduction is performed through a posterior Campbell approach. The distal humerus is exposed subperiosteally; the ulnar nerve is identified and reidentified; cicatricial tissues and ossifications around and within the joint are excised, with respect for the cartilage surfaces; usually a significant part of the fibrotic and retracted joint capsule is also excised. Reduction is checked — it should be easily obtained without a tendency to redislocation. With persistent instability the existing obstacles are reassessed and additional soft-tissue or radial head resection performed if necessary. Immobilisation in semi-flexion for 3 weeks, followed by active rehabilitation. In combined fracture-dislocations of months-to-years duration the chances of motion restoration are limited; in these cases arthroplasty or replacement may be preferred over reduction attempts.

**Arthrolysis for flexion contracture.** With a fixed soft-tissue flexion contracture (without collateral ligament shortening and without bony block), the anterior Henry approach for the elbow is used. The lacertus fibrosus is divided, the superficial veins are ligated, the biceps tendon is Z-lengthened, brachialis is divided obliquely or lengthened similarly. The median nerve and brachial artery are exposed and gently retracted. If needed the anterior joint capsule is divided and the intra-articular adhesions removed. After extension is restored the tendons are reconstructed with appropriate lengthening to allow passive extension. Immobilisation in maximal extension, followed by early active rehabilitation. Concomitant resection of heterotopic ossification (HO) is indicated only after maturation of the focus — clinically with pain resolution, radiologically with sharp delineation and density of the HO contours, and usually no less than 6 months from the trauma or prior surgery. Premature excision of "live" HO causes recurrence; postoperative prophylaxis includes indomethacin or single-dose low-dose radiation.

</section>

<details>
<summary>Synovectomy for Rheumatoid Arthritis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Elbow synovectomy is indicated in chronic rheumatoid inflammation with persistent pain, failed conservative management, and relatively preserved articular surfaces (Larsen 1–2). The approach is usually lateral Kocher, supplemented by posterior or medial incisions for complete synovectomy if needed. The radial head is resected routinely — it is often significantly damaged and resection facilitates access to the radiocapitellar joint and the proximal radioulnar joint. Early mobilisation (24–48 hours postoperatively) is critical to maintain range of motion.

</section>

<details>
<summary>Paediatric Considerations</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Epiphyseal fractures of the capitellum in children represent Salter-Harris type 4 epiphysiolyses: the fracture line passes obliquely through the growth cartilage and the fragment usually includes the nucleus of the head, part of the trochlea, and a small piece of the metaphysis. Because the extensors and the lateral collateral ligament attach to the fragment, it rotates externally. Operative treatment is absolutely indicated: a lateral approach through the Kocher interval without opening the articular cartilage; exact reduction; fixation with 2 K-wires or a thin screw introduced through the metaphyseal part of the fragment, without crossing the growth plate. In instability with a tendency to subluxation — particularly with trochlear involvement — Merle d'Aubigné recommends additional suturing of the medial collateral ligament through a separate medial incision. Immobilisation at 90° flexion for 25–30 days.

Release of an intra-articularly entrapped medial epicondyle after a "locked" paediatric dislocation requires a medial incision, gentle retraction of the ulnar nerve, opening of the torn joint capsule, removal of the epicondyle with a curved clamp, and osteosynthesis with a screw. Immobilisation at 90° flexion for 3 weeks.

In children the most common elbow fracture is the supracondylar — malunion with a medialised angulated distal fragment produces the classical cubitus varus deformity. Management of acute supracondylar fracture (closed reduction and percutaneous fixation with K-wires per Judet) is presented in detail in [Trauma 15](/en/trauma/15/).

## Outcomes and Complications
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">


Complications in operative elbow surgery group by structure and by procedure type.

**Neurovascular.** Ulnar nerve injury is the most common neurological complication (3–10% in medial approaches and in total elbow arthroplasty without transposition; below 1% with routine anterior transposition). PIN injury — the principal threat in lateral and anterior approaches to the proximal radius (1–5%), minimised by forearm supination. Medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury in the medial approach — a painful neuroma over the medial epicondyle, sometimes requiring excision and proximal reflection into muscle tissue. Brachial artery injury — rare (below 1%), potentially catastrophic in anterior approaches.

**Stiffness (loss of motion).** The most common postoperative complication of every elbow procedure (10–30% with clinically significant functional limitation in flexion-extension or rotation). Prevention — early active mobilisation, limited immobilisation time, arthrolysis for recalcitrant contracture. Heterotopic ossification (HO) is a significant contributor — risk factors: prolonged immobilisation, severe trauma, head and neurological injury, premature surgery. Prophylaxis — indomethacin (75 mg/day for 6 weeks) or single-dose low-dose radiation (7 Gy in the first 24–72 hours).

**Infection.** 1–3% in clean procedures, higher in open fractures (5–10%) and in rheumatoid arthritis (with long-term immunosuppression). In total elbow arthroplasty — 1–5% deep infection, with potentially catastrophic destruction of the construct.

**Instability.** Postoperative posterolateral rotatory instability after annular ligament incision above the equator or after aggressive radial head excision with LUCL injury. Valgus instability after radial head excision with incompetent MCL → progressive instability and Essex-Lopresti syndrome with concomitant interosseous membrane disruption and proximal radial migration.

**Non-union.** Olecranon — 1–5% after tension-band wiring (higher in complex and oblique fractures). Distal humerus — 5–10% in column osteosynthesis (higher in osteoporosis, smokers, rheumatoid arthritis).

**Arthroplasty.** Deep infection (1–5%); aseptic loosening (5–15% at 10 years); periprosthetic humeral or ulnar fracture (1–5%); triceps insufficiency after reflecting approach (10–15% with gravitational weakness); hinge mechanism loosening (semi-constrained design) in young active patients.

</section>



## Local Contributions
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">


**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the orthopaedic school in Bulgaria; the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a reference in the curriculum and contains detailed descriptions of the posterior, medial, and lateral approaches to the elbow in the Bulgarian tradition, including the triangular tendinous flap technique for triceps lengthening in flexion contracture and the medial approach with medial epicondyle osteotomy.

**Konforti-Paneva <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** The classical technique for plastic reconstruction of the medial collateral ligament in a lax elbow following resection or arthroplasty — a free tendon graft passed through a transverse drill tunnel in the distal humeral metaphysis and through two slightly oblique tunnels in the proximal ulna in figure-of-eight configuration, anatomically equivalent to the modern Tommy John procedure by Jobe. The same school describes a Hass interposition arthroplasty modification with fascia lata for severe post-traumatic destruction. Hemireplacement of the distal humerus by Konforti-Paneva (modification of Bakeen-Vainio with a polyethylene prosthesis) remains a historical technique replaced for severe destruction by modern hinged replacement (Coonrad-Morrey).

**Paneva-Dobrev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Elbow hemiarthroplasty with autotransplantation of the distal humeral articular surface — in severe stiffness with complete loss of motion but with preserved articular congruity. The distal fragment is resected together with the cartilage, the proximal fragment is shaped and impacted into the harvested allograft, fixation with 2 K-wires.

**Nachev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Alternative olecranon fixation — intramedullary compression screw from the tip of the olecranon into the medullary canal of the ulna; applicable in larger distal fragments with sufficient metaphyseal bone stock. Complements the classical Weber tension-band wiring in cases where K-wires and cerclage have a relatively limited application.

**Adaptation of Steindler flexorplasty <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** The Bulgarian school adapts the original procedure by A. Steindler <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1918) by reattaching the osteoperiosteal flexor flap to the anterolateral, rather than the anteromedial, surface of the humerus — minimising the concomitant pronation with flexion (the classical Steindler effect described by Bunnell <sup class="natflag">US</sup>) and standardising preoperative assessment of passive extension as a condition for success.

**Classical Bulgarian posterior midline approach (Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>).** A posterior incision skirting the olecranon tip laterally (to avoid a scar on the tip), with a triangular tendinous flap of triceps for Z-lengthening in flexion contracture — a standard Bulgarian school technique that obviates the need for olecranon osteotomy in selected reconstructive scenarios.

**Medial approach with medial epicondyle osteotomy <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Oblique osteoplastic detachment of the medial epicondyle with distal reflection of the flexor-pronator mass; preserves the medial collateral ligament insertion on the distal bony fragment. Used in classical removal of an entrapped medial epicondyle in a child, in the medial approach to the coronoid, and in medial exposure for total elbow arthroplasty.

**Classical anatomical rules for PIN <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** The Bulgarian school codifies three rules valid in every extended lateral approach: the bifurcation of the radial nerve above the upper border of supinator (anterior to the joint capsule); entry of the deep branch into the supinator canal 2–3.5 cm distal to the radial head; the superficial branch between brachioradialis and extensor carpi radialis longus.

**Submuscular transposition of the ulnar nerve with fascial-fat flap <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** An adaptation of the classical Learmonth <sup class="natflag">UK</sup> (1942) technique — adds a protective fascial-fat flap as a biological buffer between the transposed nerve and the reinserted flexor-pronator mass; reduces the postoperative risk of cicatricial compression.

**Compression arthrodesis of the elbow with Ilizarov fixator <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** A Bulgarian adaptation of the classical Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> compression for elbow arthrodesis — two parallel K-wires through the humerus and two through the ulna, fixed to half-rings with infinite screws for graded compression; bony union typically in 2 months without cast immobilisation.

From the classical Russian and Ukrainian school, closely integrated in the Bulgarian curriculum:

**G. A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Compression apparatus with fine transosseous wires and rings — the basis for elbow arthrodesis under compression and for distraction osteogenesis in the limbs.

**Putti <sup class="natflag">IT</sup> and Wittek <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>.** Elbow arthrodesis with an intramedullary bone graft between the humerus and the ulna — a classical descriptor from the Italian and German schools, closely integrated in the Bulgarian curriculum.

**Vreden <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** A classical posterior reconstructive technique using semi-cut bony structures as a support point — the conceptual basis for the biological principles of classical arthrodesis and interposition.

</section>



## Classical Sources
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">


<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical lineage — elbow surgery</strong>
  <p>The systematic operative anatomy of the elbow dates from the early 19th century, when Velpeau (1832) and Dupuytren (1832) defined the regional vocabulary for elbow dislocations and supracondylar fractures that remains in use today. Astley Cooper (1822) gave the foundational English description of dislocation patterns. Malgaigne (1847) added the systematic analysis of olecranon and capitellum fractures. The approaches to the elbow in their modern sense were established by Kocher (1911), who described the lateral interval between anconeus and extensor carpi ulnaris, and by Henry (1957) — the anterior "lazy S" approach.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Astley Cooper 1822</strong> — *A Treatise on Dislocations*; the patterns of elbow dislocation (<a href="https://archive.org/details/atreatiseondisl00coopgoog">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Velpeau 1832</strong> — *Nouveaux éléments de médecine opératoire*, vol. 2 — elbow (<a href="https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_43901x02">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Dupuytren 1832</strong> — *Leçons orales de clinique chirurgicale*, vol. 1 — dislocations of the elbow (<a href="https://archive.org/details/leonsoralesdec01dupu">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Malgaigne 1847</strong> — *Traité des fractures et des luxations*, vol. 1 — olecranon and capitellum (<a href="https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_32985x01">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Kocher 1911 → Henry 1957 → Bryan-Morrey 1982 → O'Driscoll 1991</strong> — the evolution of the lateral, anterior, and posterior approaches with the recognition of posterolateral rotatory instability and LUCL as a discrete structure.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Classical descriptions by approach and procedure: Hohmann 1926 (lateral epicondylitis); Bosworth 1955 (more extensive tennis elbow procedure); Steindler 1918 (flexorplasty); Learmonth 1942 (submuscular ulnar nerve transposition); Boyd-Anderson 1961 (two-incision distal biceps reinsertion); Weber-AO 1969 (tension-band wiring of olecranon); Jobe 1974 (Tommy John MCL reconstruction); Bryan-Morrey 1982 (reflecting triceps approach); O'Driscoll 1991 (posterolateral rotatory instability, LUCL); Nirschl (modern lateral epicondylitis procedure).

Additional classical sources: **Boycho Boychev** (three volumes, Sofia) — posterior and medial approaches in the Bulgarian tradition, the triangular tendinous flap for triceps lengthening. **Hodgson-Watson** (1957), *Surgery of the Shoulder and Elbow* — modern synthesis of operative anatomy. **Morrey** (from 1985, multiple editions), *The Elbow and Its Disorders* — the modern equivalent of the classical textbooks; reference for operative anatomy, post-traumatic destruction, and hinged replacement (Coonrad-Morrey construct).

  /* V4 anatomy-4: open the target <details> when the page loads with a hash
     or when the reader clicks an in-page anchor. Native <details> isn't

  (function () {
    function openByHash(hash) {
      if (!hash) return;
      var id = hash.replace(/^#/, "");
      if (!id) return;
      var el = document.getElementById(id);
      if (!el) return;
      if (el.tagName === "DETAILS") el.open = true;
      var inner = el.querySelector(":scope > details");
      if (inner) inner.open = true;
      var node = el.parentElement;
      while (node && node !== document.body) {
        if (node.tagName === "DETAILS") node.open = true;
        node = node.parentElement;
      }
      requestAnimationFrame(function () {
        el.scrollIntoView({ behavior: "smooth", block: "start" });
      });
    }
    function wire() {
      openByHash(window.location.hash);
      document.addEventListener("click", function (e) {
        var t = e.target;
        while (t && t !== document) {
          if (t.tagName === "A" && t.getAttribute && t.getAttribute("href") && t.getAttribute("href").charAt(0) === "#") {
            openByHash(t.getAttribute("href"));
            break;
          }
          t = t.parentNode;
        }
      });
      window.addEventListener("hashchange", function () { openByHash(window.location.hash); });
    }
    if (document.readyState === "loading") {
      document.addEventListener("DOMContentLoaded", wire);
    } else {
      wire();
    }
  })();
