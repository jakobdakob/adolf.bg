## Introduction

The elbow joint, with its complex combination of hinge motion (humeroulnar articulation) and pivot motion (proximal radioulnar joint) within a single capsule, presents distinct anatomical and surgical challenges. The surrounding muscular envelope is relatively thin compared with the shoulder, and the proximity of the ulnar nerve, the radial nerve and its branches (particularly the posterior interosseous nerve), the median nerve, and the brachial artery to the joint demand careful attention during surgical approaches. The orthopedic surgeon working at the elbow has multiple approach options, each providing access to different regions of the joint: the Kocher approach for the lateral side and radial head/capitellum, the posterior approaches (triceps-splitting, paratricipital, reflecting, and olecranon osteotomy) for the distal humerus and ulnohumeral joint, the medial approaches for the medial collateral ligament and ulnar nerve, and the anterior approaches for vascular and neural exploration. This chapter draws on Orthopaedic Surgical Approaches, Netter's Concise Orthopaedic Anatomy, Gray's Anatomy, and the classical Bulgarian Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology of Boycho Boychev (B. Boychev, ed.; Sofia) — the namesake of the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya — supplemented with the leading procedures for elbow flexion restoration (Steindler, Clark, Merle d'Aubigné, Hovnanian), arthrodesis (Brittain, Steindler, Campbell), interposition arthroplasty, and contemporary replacement (Coonrad-Morrey, Souter-Strathclyde, Kudo), together with the classical Bulgarian procedures of Konforti-Paneva, Paneva-Dobrev, and Nachev that remain part of the Bulgarian orthopedic curriculum.

## Regional Boundaries and Fascial Spaces

The elbow region is bounded by transverse lines 4 cm above and 4 cm below the elbow crease, with the lateral epicondyle lying approximately 1 cm higher than the medial epicondyle. It is divided into anterior (regio cubiti anterior) and posterior (regio cubiti posterior) regions. The anterior skin is thin, mobile, and hairless; the posterior skin is thicker. Subcutaneously the anterior region contains the basilic vein (medial, accompanied by the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve), the cephalic vein (lateral), and the median cubital vein connecting them. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (terminal sensory branch of the musculocutaneous nerve) pierces the deep fascia approximately 4-5 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle — a structure to preserve during anterior approaches for distal biceps tendon repair. The deep fascia is reinforced by the lacertus fibrosus (bicipital aponeurosis), which links it to the flexor-pronator mass fascia. Posteriorly the subcutaneous olecranon bursa overlies the olecranon tip — chronic mechanical irritation or rheumatoid arthritis can cause bursitis requiring bursectomy.

The elbow joint comprises three articulations within a single synovial capsule: Humeroulnar joint: Hinge articulation between the trochlea of the distal humerus and the trochlear notch of the proximal ulna. Provides flexion-extension (normal range approximately 0 to 145 degrees of flexion; functional range 30 to 130 degrees). Humeroradial joint: Articulation between the capitellum of the distal humerus and the radial head. Participates in flexion-extension and forearm rotation. Proximal radioulnar joint: Pivot articulation between the radial head and the radial notch of the ulna, with the annular ligament holding the radial head in position. Provides forearm rotation (normal supination approximately 85 degrees, pronation approximately 85 degrees). The carrying angle of the extended elbow is approximately 11 degrees of valgus in females and 7 to 11 degrees in males. Loss of carrying angle (e.g., from supracondylar malunion) produces cubitus varus (gunstock deformity); excessive carrying angle produces cubitus valgus. The distal humerus anatomy was detailed in [Topic Trauma-15](/en/trauma/15/): divergent medial and lateral columns, trochlea (spool-shaped), capitellum (rounded), olecranon and coronoid fossae. The proximal ulna comprises the olecranon (with triceps insertion), coronoid process (anterior buttress), and trochlear notch. The proximal radius has the disc-shaped radial head (with the annular ligament around it), radial neck, and radial tuberosity (biceps insertion).

## Ulnar Nerve

The ulnar nerve descends through the posterior medial intermuscular septum and enters the cubital tunnel between the medial epicondyle and the olecranon. The tunnel is bounded medially by the Osborne ligament (the arcuate ligament between the two heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris) and laterally by the medial collateral ligament. The nerve then passes between the two heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris and enters the forearm. The cubital tunnel syndrome (the second most common compression neuropathy in the upper extremity, after carpal tunnel) is a recognized entity. Tinel’s sign over the cubital tunnel, and elbow flexion test with maintained flexion for 1 minute reproducing symptoms, are positive in cubital tunnel syndrome. Surgical treatment options include in situ decompression (release of the cubital tunnel retinaculum), submuscular anterior transposition (transferring the nerve anterior to the medial epicondyle with the muscle protection), and subcutaneous anterior transposition (similar but without muscle covering). The ulnar nerve is at risk in any medial elbow approach and is routinely identified and protected during distal humerus surgery.


## Median Nerve and Brachial Artery

The median nerve and brachial artery pass through the cubital fossa, with the artery typically lateral to the nerve at the elbow. The artery and nerve are deep to the bicipital aponeurosis (lacertus fibrosus), a fibrous expansion of the biceps tendon that crosses the cubital fossa medially. The brachial artery bifurcates into the radial and ulnar arteries near the level of the radial neck. The median nerve continues distally between the two heads of the pronator teres (where it is occasionally compressed in pronator syndrome), then passes deep to the flexor digitorum superficialis arch and enters the forearm.

## Radial Nerve

The radial nerve approaches the elbow anteriorly (after piercing the lateral intermuscular septum in the distal arm) and passes through the cubital fossa lateral to the biceps tendon, between the brachialis (medial) and the brachioradialis (lateral). At the level of the radial head, the nerve divides into: Superficial branch (sensory): Continues distally beneath the brachioradialis to the distal forearm, where it becomes superficial. Deep branch (posterior interosseous nerve, PIN): Passes through the arcade of Frohse (the fibrous proximal margin of the supinator) and through the supinator muscle to enter the posterior forearm. The PIN supplies the extensor compartment muscles. The PIN is at risk in lateral approaches to the proximal radius and the radial head, particularly during pronation of the forearm (which moves the nerve closer to the operative field; the forearm should be supinated during surgery to displace the nerve laterally). The PIN is also at risk in radial head dislocation and in surgical manipulation of the radial head.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>Pronation brings the PIN closer to the lateral elbow; supinate the forearm during the Kocher approach to displace the nerve laterally away from the operative field. </aside>

## Cubital Vessels

The cubital fossa contains the median nerve, brachial artery, and brachial veins, with the bicipital aponeurosis crossing the artery superficially. The superficial veins (median cubital vein, cephalic vein, basilic vein) traverse the cubital fossa subcutaneously.

The Kocher approach is the classical lateral approach to the elbow joint, providing access to the radial head, capitellum, coronoid process (from the lateral side), lateral collateral ligament complex, and posterolateral elbow capsule.

## Patient Positioning

The patient is supine with the affected arm on a hand table or arm board, or lateral decubitus with the arm supported in front of the body. The elbow is positioned to expose the lateral aspect.


## Surface Landmarks and Incision

The lateral epicondyle is the principal landmark. The skin incision extends from approximately 4 to 6 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle distally over the radial head to approximately 4 to 6 cm distal to the radial head. The total length is approximately 8 to 12 cm.


## Approach

The skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised. The fascia is opened in line with the skin incision. The Kocher interval between the anconeus (posterior, supplied by the radial nerve) and the extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) (anterior, supplied by the PIN) is identified — this is the classical internervous plane that gives the Kocher approach its name. The interval is developed bluntly, with the dissection carried down to the lateral elbow capsule. The annular ligament is identified at the level of the radial head. For exposure of the radial head, the annular ligament can be incised and reflected. The radial head and capitellum are then visualized. For more proximal exposure (capitellum, lateral epicondyle, lateral column of the distal humerus), the incision can be extended proximally with elevation of the common extensor origin from the lateral epicondyle. For more distal exposure (proximal radius, supinator), the dissection extends distally with forearm supination to protect the PIN (which moves laterally away from the operative field with supination).

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The Kocher interval (anconeus posteriorly, radial nerve / ECU anteriorly, PIN) is a true internervous plane and the classical lateral elbow approach. Detaching the common extensor origin proximally extends the exposure to the lateral column and capitellum; incising the annular ligament exposes the radial head.</p>
</aside>

## Complications

The complications of the Kocher approach include PIN injury (the principal concern; minimized by forearm supination during dissection and avoidance of vigorous lateral retraction of the supinator), damage to the lateral collateral ligament complex (with iatrogenic posterolateral rotatory instability), and stiffness of the elbow if the approach is extensive.

## Modifications

The extensor digitorum communis (EDC) split approach uses the muscle-splitting plane within the EDC rather than the Kocher interval, providing more direct access to the anterior aspect of the radial head. The PIN considerations are similar.

## Classical Anatomical Landmarks for the Lateral Approach

The Bulgarian operative school emphasizes three anatomical rules that hold for any extended lateral elbow approach. First: immediately proximal to the upper border of the supinator, anterior to the joint capsule, the radial nerve divides into superficial and deep branches — identifying this bifurcation is the key to locating the PIN before it enters the supinator. Second: the deep branch (PIN) enters the canalis supinatorius approximately 2 to 3.5 cm distal to the radial head; extending distal dissection beyond this point without supination and direct visual control directly jeopardizes the nerve. Third: the superficial radial branch runs distally between brachioradialis and extensor carpi radialis longus and must be identified when extending the incision caudally. The motor branch to the anconeus arises most often at the level of the distal outlet of the canalis supinatorius and must be preserved during the Kocher interval dissection. The proximal angle of the wound encounters the terminal branches of the radial collateral artery, which are usually ligated without consequence.

## Classical Operations for Lateral Epicondylitis (Tennis Elbow)

Chronic tendinosis of the common extensor origin at the lateral epicondyle ("tennis elbow," Runge epicondylitis) rarely requires surgery, but after failure of 6 to 12 months of conservative care the classical procedures remain relevant. The Hohmann procedure (G. Hohmann, 1926) is a simple release of the common extensor origin from the lateral epicondyle with light bony recontouring — relieving chronic traction on the tendon. The Bosworth procedure (D. M. Bosworth, 1955) is more extensive: detachment of the extensors, partial resection of the annular ligament (the anterolateral one-third), excision of the hypertrophic synovial fringe between the capitellum and radial head, and smoothing of the epicondyle. Per Bosworth, transection or even excision of one-third of the anterolateral annular ligament does not produce significant instability, and reattachment of extensors to the freshened bony bed is preferred but not mandatory. The modern analogue is the Nirschl procedure, more focused, targeting only the pathologic ECRB tendon. For medial epicondylitis ("golfer's elbow") an analogous operation involves detachment of the common flexor origin with tangential excision of the degenerate tendon, with the principal concern being protection of the adjacent ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel.

## Posterior Approaches to the Elbow

The posterior approaches were discussed in detail in the context of the distal humerus approaches in [Topic Anatomy-3](/en/anatomy/3/) (arm/humerus). They include: Triceps-splitting approach: Midline split of the triceps muscle, providing access to the distal humerus.

Paratricipital (Bryan-Morrey) approaches: Retraction of the triceps medially or laterally, preserving the triceps insertion. Triceps-reflecting (Bryan-Morrey) approach: Detachment of the triceps insertion from the olecranon with reflection proximally. Olecranon osteotomy approach: Chevron-shaped osteotomy of the olecranon to expose the distal humeral articular surface for complex intra-articular fractures. The choice among these approaches for elbow surgery depends on the planned procedure. For distal humerus fracture ORIF, the olecranon osteotomy provides the best visualization. For total elbow arthroplasty, the paratricipital approach (or triceps-reflecting in older techniques) is preferred to preserve the olecranon and triceps insertion. For simple distal humerus exposure (e.g., for biopsy, tumor resection without articular involvement), the triceps-splitting or paratricipital approach may suffice.

## Classical Posterior Midline Approach

The classical Bulgarian posterior midline approach is systematically described in Boycheva and remains foundational for the anatomy of the posterior elbow. The patient is supine or in lateral decubitus with the limb across the chest. Landmarks are the triceps tendon, the olecranon, and the sulcus n. ulnaris. The skin incision begins 6-7 cm proximal to the olecranon, runs distally over the midline, curves around the olecranon laterally (to avoid a scar over the tip, which is chronically irritated by armrests), and continues 5-6 cm distally along the posterior border of the ulna. After fascial division, the distal triceps and periosteum of the distal humerus are elevated subperiosteally, and the joint is opened through the olecranon fossa. For triceps lengthening in flexion contracture, a triangular tendinous flap is fashioned with the base on the olecranon and the apex proximal; after contracture release, the flap is repaired in Z-fashion to lengthen the tendon. In the olecranon osteotomy variant, the borders of the olecranon are freed subperiosteally, the osteotomy is performed at the base (at the "bare area" of the trochlear notch lacking articular cartilage), and the osteomuscular flap is reflected proximally, exposing the posterior joint and supracondylar region widely. The osteotomy is fixed at closure with a screw or tension-band wire (Weber). The radial nerve crossing the lateral intermuscular septum at the junction of the middle and distal thirds of the humerus is the cephalic limit of safe dissection.

## Classical Medial Approach with Medial Epicondyle Osteotomy

The Bulgarian school describes an extended medial approach in which the medial epicondyle is osteotomized and reflected distally and laterally with the attached flexor-pronator mass. With the patient supine and the arm on an arm board, the ulnar nerve is first palpated in its sulcus, the overlying fascia carefully incised, and the nerve isolated with a vessel loop and retracted posteriorly. The medial border of flexor carpi ulnaris is elevated and the underlying ulnar nerve protected. The lateral border of pronator teres is elevated up to the medial epicondyle to expose the median nerve, its flexor branches, and the brachial artery. The medial epicondyle is osteotomized obliquely — preserving the medial collateral ligament attachment on the distal fragment — and reflected distally with the flexor-pronator mass. The approach can be extended subperiosteally under brachialis to expose the anterior capsule and supracondylar region; the brachial vessels and median nerve remain protected by the overlying brachialis. At closure the epicondyle is reattached with a screw. The approach is particularly useful for removing an intra-articular incarcerated medial epicondyle in a "locked" pediatric elbow dislocation, for medial coronoid exposure (Hotchkiss "over the top" modification), and for medially-approached total elbow arthroplasty.

## Medial Approach to the Elbow

The medial approach provides access to the medial epicondyle, medial collateral ligament, ulnar nerve, and medial coronoid process.

## Patient Positioning

The patient is supine with the affected arm on a hand table, or the arm positioned across the chest for medial access.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

The medial epicondyle is the principal landmark. The skin incision extends from approximately 5 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle distally for approximately 8 to 10 cm, centered over the medial epicondyle.


## Approach

The skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised, with attention to the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve (branches typically cross the operative field and should be preserved). The medial intermuscular septum is identified proximally, and the ulnar nerve is identified posterior to the medial epicondyle. The ulnar nerve is mobilized and protected throughout the procedure. For MCL reconstruction (Tommy John surgery), the common flexor mass origin from the medial epicondyle is incised vertically (the “muscle-splitting” approach of Jobe) or the common flexor origin is detached (the “detachment” approach). The native MCL is exposed and the reconstruction proceeds (typically with a palmaris longus or hamstring tendon graft passing through bony tunnels). For medial coronoid fracture fixation (particularly the anteromedial facet pattern of O’Driscoll discussed in [Topic Trauma-15](/en/trauma/15/)), the medial approach with medial Hotchkiss “over the top” modification provides access to the coronoid.

For ulnar nerve transposition, the nerve is mobilized from the cubital tunnel and transposed anteriorly (subcutaneously or submuscularly) anterior to the medial epicondyle.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>On the medial approach the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve crosses the operative field subcutaneously and is the source of painful neuroma if transected. The ulnar nerve must be identified posterior to the medial epicondyle and protected throughout; cubital tunnel decompression releases Osborne's ligament (the arcuate ligament between the two heads of FCU).</p>
</aside>

## Complications

The complications of the medial approach include ulnar nerve injury (the principal concern; the nerve must be identified and protected throughout), medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury (with painful neuroma formation), and flexor mass disruption (with grip strength loss if the common flexor origin is not adequately repaired).

## Anterior Approach to the Elbow (Henry Approach to Elbow)

The anterior approach is used for vascular injury repair (brachial artery), distal biceps tendon repair, and selected exposures of the anterior elbow.

## Patient Positioning

The patient is supine with the affected arm on a hand table, forearm supinated.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

The incision is a lazy S or lazy J that crosses the cubital crease at an angle to avoid scar contracture across the flexion crease.

## Approach

The skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised. The lacertus fibrosus (bicipital aponeurosis) is identified and incised, providing access to the cubital fossa. The brachial artery and median nerve are identified and protected. For distal biceps tendon repair, the distal biceps tendon is identified after disruption of the lacertus and traced to its retracted location. Repair to the radial tuberosity is performed through a single anterior approach (with the additional risk of PIN injury during posterior tuberosity preparation) or through the classical two-incision (Boyd-Anderson) approach with a separate posterior incision for the radial tuberosity. For vascular injury repair, the brachial artery is directly exposed and repaired. For anterior capsulectomy (in stiff elbow surgery), the anterior approach provides access to the anterior capsule and the coronoid fossa.

## Complications

The complications of the anterior approach include median nerve injury, brachial artery injury, lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury, and PIN injury (particularly during distal biceps tendon repair from the anterior approach).

## Surgical Approaches to the Cubital Tunnel and Ulnar Nerve

The cubital tunnel is approached through a longitudinal incision posterior to the medial epicondyle. The Osborne ligament (the arcuate ligament between the two heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris) is identified and released, providing in situ decompression of the ulnar nerve. For anterior transposition, the nerve is further mobilized and brought anteriorly, either: Subcutaneously: The nerve is placed in the subcutaneous tissue anterior to the medial epicondyle, with a fascial sling or tendon turn-up sometimes used to maintain position. Submuscularly (Learmonth, 1942): The flexor pronator origin is detached, the nerve is placed deep to the muscle onto brachialis, and the muscle is reattached over the nerve. The submuscular position provides more protection but requires more dissection. The technique bears the name of J. R. Learmonth, who described it for tardy ulnar nerve palsy after post-traumatic cubitus valgus. Intramuscularly: The nerve is placed within a groove created in the flexor pronator muscle mass. This technique is less commonly used. Medial epicondylectomy is an alternative procedure that removes the medial epicondyle, allowing the nerve to lie naturally anterior; it has fallen out of favor because of concerns about MCL function and persistent pain. The classical Bulgarian technique uses a subcutaneous transposition under a fascia-fat flap for early ulnar paresis from cubitus valgus or scar compression after epicondyle fracture, with division of some fibers of flexor carpi ulnaris distally to free the nerve from Osborne's arcade and dissection deep to the flexor-pronator origin to seat the nerve on brachialis without tension or kinking.

## Tendon Transfers for Elbow Flexion Restoration

In complete loss of elbow flexion (traumatic brachial plexus palsy, post-polio paralysis), a series of classical tendon transfers were developed and remain relevant where modern nerve reconstruction is unavailable. Active shoulder motion (or a preceding shoulder arthrodesis in moderate abduction) and preserved hand grip function are prerequisites for success.

The Clark transfer of the distal portion of pectoralis major (modified by Merle d'Aubigné and Postel) is indicated for brachial plexus palsy with preserved C8-T1 roots, since the distal pectoralis major receives its innervation from these roots and has an independent neurovascular pedicle. In Clark's original description the distal pectoralis major belly is mobilized from the chest wall preserving its humeral insertion; Merle d'Aubigné and Postel showed better function with full transfer on a neurovascular pedicle, sutured to the distal biceps tendon near its insertion on the radial tuberosity with the elbow flexed to 120 degrees and moderate muscle tension. Immobilization is for 3 weeks in full adduction of the shoulder and 150 degrees of elbow flexion, then a further 3 weeks in a sling at 90 degrees.

The Hovnanian transfer of latissimus dorsi on its neurovascular pedicle (thoracodorsal artery and nerve) for biceps function is used for broader paralyses. The latissimus is mobilized from the chest wall preserving its covering fascia, detached from both ends (humerus and lumbodorsal fascia), and transferred through a subcutaneous tunnel to the distal biceps tendon. The Schottstaedt modification attaches the proximal end to the coracoid for stabilization and improved lever arm. The same principle applies to restore extension by transfer of latissimus dorsi to the triceps insertion, particularly important in post-polio paralysis when the patient requires crutches or other walking aids.

The Steindler flexorplasty (A. Steindler, 1918) uses the same principle of converting wrist and finger flexors into secondary elbow flexors by proximal advancement of the common flexor origin from the medial epicondyle to the distal humerus. Through a curved medial incision the flexor mass is detached osteoplastically with a thin epicondylar bone wafer; the bony fragment is reattached to the anterior humerus 7-8 cm above the elbow, preserving the ulnar nerve in its new position. Pre-existing flexion contracture defeats the procedure, so passive extension must be restored first. Reattachment to the anterolateral rather than anteromedial humerus minimizes pronation accompanying flexion — the classic Steindler effect described by Bunnell.

When biceps is non-functional but triceps is preserved, a biceps "rerouting" — converting biceps brachii from supinator to pronator — is performed by releasing the distal biceps tendon from the radial tuberosity, passing it around the radial neck in the reverse direction, and reinserting at the same site. During flexion the muscle now produces pronation rather than supination — particularly useful in children with obstetric brachial plexus palsy and fixed supination contracture, and in polio sequelae. If the forearm is locked in supination, release of the interosseous membrane (with protection of the posterior interosseous nerve), supinator detachment, and distal radioulnar capsulotomy precede the rerouting.

## Distal Biceps Tendon Reinsertion

Distal biceps tendon rupture (avulsion from the radial tuberosity) typically occurs with sudden eccentric loading of a flexed elbow in middle-aged men. The classical two-incision Boyd-Anderson repair (1961) uses an anterior incision to identify and weave the retracted tendon (cross-stitch Bunnell suture) and a second posterolateral incision over the radial tuberosity. A blunt tunnel is created between radius and ulna at the tuberosity level, the elbow flexed, and the tendon sutured to the freshened tuberosity bony bed. Immobilization is at 90 degrees and neutral rotation for 3 weeks. The single-anterior-incision alternative permits direct tuberosity preparation through the anterior approach at the cost of additional PIN risk; modern techniques use cortical buttons or interference screws that reduce the need for the second posterolateral incision. A pre-operative predictor of paralysis-transfer success is preserved active flexion after passive elbow positioning at 90 degrees — indicating residual flexor strength meaningful for function.

## Arthrodesis of the Elbow

Elbow arthrodesis is now rare but retains indications in heavy laborers with severe joint destruction, particularly with bilateral upper extremity involvement where at least one elbow must remain mobile. Bony union is hard to achieve because of the limb's pendulous position, so classical techniques rely on autografts. The Putti-Wittek arthrodesis uses an intramedullary bone graft between humerus and ulna. The Brittain arthrodesis uses a transarticular tricortical tibial graft through the humerus into the proximal ulna. The Campbell arthrodesis uses an angular bone-periosteal graft fixed with cerclage. The Steindler arthrodesis inverts the olecranon into the distal humerus for biomechanical stability and better fusion surfaces. The radial head is routinely resected before arthrodesis to prevent blocking of forearm rotation at the proximal radioulnar joint.

Charnley compression arthrodesis with Steinmann pins has been abandoned because of pin bulk and inadequate stability. The modern standard is Ilizarov-type compression arthrodesis: two parallel wires through the humerus 4-5 cm apart fixed to upper half-rings, two ulnar wires (one along the joint axis, one slightly distal) fixed to lower half-rings, and compression applied via threaded rods. Plaster immobilization is generally unnecessary; bony union typically occurs in approximately 2 months. The functional fusion position is 70-90 degrees of flexion in neutral rotation.

## Interposition Arthroplasty (Pre-Replacement Era)

Before reliable elbow replacement, motion was restored in the stiff elbow by interposition arthroplasty. The classical Hass interposition arthroplasty (and the Bulgarian Konforti-Paneva modification) uses a posterior approach with longitudinal triceps splitting, joint exposure, reshaping of the bony surfaces (distal humerus resected 1-1.5 cm to a cylindrical contour, ulnar trochlear notch deepened), radial head resection, and interposition of a free fascia lata graft (5-7 cm wide, approximately 20 cm long) — half of which wraps the distal humerus and is fixed through transverse cortical channels. Pure resection arthroplasty resects the bony ends transversely (3 cm defect) without interposition, producing an unstable but mobile elbow — indicated only in severe septic or tuberculous destruction where stability is not a priority. Hemiarthroplasty applies when only the distal humeral articular surface is destroyed and the ulna is intact — the distal humerus is reshaped to match the trochlear notch and covered with a fascia lata graft. The Paneva autograft technique for the distal humeral articular surface uses an allograft into which the recontoured proximal fragment is impacted, fixed with two Kirschner wires.

The classical Konforti-Paneva technique for reconstructing the medial collateral ligament in a loose elbow after resection or arthroplasty uses a free tendon graft passed through a transverse drill channel in the distal humeral metaphysis and through two slightly oblique channels in the proximal ulna (anteromedial and posteromedial). Jobe in 1974 for baseball pitchers: reconstruction of the anterior bundle of the MCL with a free graft of palmaris longus or gracilis/semitendinosus, passed in a figure-of-eight configuration through bone tunnels in the medial epicondyle and the sublime tubercle. Success in both approaches depends on locating the isometric anterior bundle insertion — the posteroinferior medial epicondyle, just above the rotation axis. For lateral instability after LUCL injury (posterolateral rotatory instability, O'Driscoll), a free graft is passed from the isometric point on the lateral epicondyle to the supinator crest in a similar figure-of-eight configuration.

## Elbow Endoprosthesis

Elbow replacement developed along two main concepts: unipolar (surface replacement of one articular side) and bipolar (linked or unlinked total replacement).

Unipolar replacement of the distal humerus — the Konforti-Paneva technique (a Bakeen-Vainio modification using a high-density polyethylene component) — uses a posterior Campbell approach, subperiosteal humeral exposure, and placement of a transverse Kirschner wire through the metaphysis at 90 degrees to the diaphyseal axis as a rotation axis. The bone around the wire is shaped into a cylinder slightly smaller than the implant's inner diameter, and the prosthesis is impacted from ulnar to radial. Impaction must be firm enough that no additional fixation is needed. Analogous unipolar replacement of the radial head is used for comminuted Mason III-IV fractures in adults, particularly with Essex-Lopresti or complex elbow instability where simple excision is contraindicated.

Linked semi-constrained hinge implants of the Coonrad-Morrey type are the modern standard for total elbow replacement in rheumatoid arthritis and complex post-traumatic destruction. Both components (humeral and ulnar) have intramedullary stems, usually cemented, linked by a hinge with small varus-valgus and rotational play (semi-constrained) that distributes rotational loads to soft tissues and reduces loosening. The approach is posterior paratricipital or triceps-reflecting; the radial head is resected; the humerus is opened just above the olecranon fossa, the ulna approximately 1 cm from the coronoid. Canals are prepared 2 mm wider than the stems and 1 cm deeper, allowing room for cement. Unlinked surface replacements (Souter-Strathclyde, Kudo) are biomechanically gentler on soft tissues but require competent collateral ligaments and are not suitable for severe destruction with ligament insufficiency.

Tension-band wiring of the olecranon (Weber, AO, 1969) is the standard for simple transverse and short oblique olecranon fractures with displacement. Through a posterior incision over the olecranon tip the fracture is exposed and reduced; two parallel Kirschner wires are driven through the triceps insertion site so that their tips just engage the opposite cortex distal to the fracture. A transverse drill channel is made through the distal fragment approximately 3-4 cm distal to the fracture, and a stainless steel wire is passed through this channel and around the proximal ends of the K-wires in a figure-of-eight configuration. Twisting the wire tightens the construct, converting triceps flexion forces into compression at the articular surface (Pauwels tension principle). K-wires are bent caudally and rotated to prevent skin penetration on flexion. The alternative Nachev technique uses an intramedullary compression screw introduced through the olecranon tip into the ulnar medullary canal — applicable when the distal fragment has sufficient metaphyseal bone. For comminuted fractures or small tip fragments (involving no more than one-third of the olecranon), the fragments are excised and the triceps reattached directly to the freshened bony bed with transosseous sutures or anchors. Resection is contraindicated for distal fragments compromising anteroposterior stability.

## Pediatric Distal Humerus Fractures

Capitellum (eminentia capitata humeri) fractures in children are Salter-Harris type IV epiphyseolyses — the line crosses the growth cartilage obliquely and the fragment typically includes the capitellar ossific nucleus, part of the trochlea, and a small metaphyseal portion. Because the lateral epicondylar muscles and the lateral collateral ligament attach to this fragment, it rotates externally. Surgery is mandatory: lateral Kocher approach, exact reduction, fixation with 2 Kirschner wires or a thin screw introduced through the metaphyseal portion, never crossing the growth plate. If unstable with subluxation (trochlear involvement), Merle d'Aubigné recommends additional medial collateral repair through a separate medial incision. Immobilization at 90 degrees for 25-30 days.

Unlocking of an incarcerated medial epicondyle (a "locked" pediatric elbow dislocation with the epicondyle trapped in the joint) requires a medial incision, gentle ulnar nerve retraction, opening the torn capsule, extraction of the epicondyle with a curved clamp, and screw osteosynthesis.

Cubitus varus (gunstock deformity) is the classic late sequel of malreduced supracondylar or transcondylar fracture in childhood. Cubitus valgus most often arises from nonunion of the lateral condyle or growth arrest of the distal humerus. Corrective supracondylar osteotomy is performed through a lateral approach for varus and a medial approach for valgus. After subperiosteal exposure of the distal humerus (approximately 4 cm) at a standardized point above the olecranon fossa, a wedge osteotomy is fashioned — laterally based open wedge for varus, medially based open (or laterally based closed) wedge for valgus. A slightly broader dorsal wedge corrects accompanying extension deficit. Fixation is with 1-2 Kirschner wires with tips left under the skin (in children, special care to avoid the growth plate). For cubitus valgus correction, the ulnar nerve must be identified, its bed opened, and the nerve retracted before osteotomy — the indication is often tardy ulnar nerve palsy prophylaxis, not just cosmetic correction. Comparative radiographs of both elbows are required for accurate planning.

The classical indication for radial head excision is a comminuted Mason III-IV fracture in an adult, without significant Essex-Lopresti or concomitant instability (which requires preserving the radial buttress through reconstruction or replacement). Through a Kocher approach, the annular ligament is opened or resected and the radial head excised at the level of the neck. Contraindications include interosseous membrane injury (Essex-Lopresti), MCL insufficiency, and skeletally immature patients.

Open reduction of a chronically dislocated radial head (2-6 weeks unreduced) uses a posterolateral approach: the joint capsule is opened, the dislocated head is reduced with gentle elevators while flexing the elbow; if the annular ligament is repairable it is sutured, otherwise reconstructed with a free tendon graft passed through a transverse channel in the posterior border of the proximal ulna at the radial neck level. The classical Hohmann technique uses a pedicled triceps tendon autograft to reconstruct the annular ligament. Beyond 6 months of duration the soft-tissue changes make open reduction impractical — alternatives are head excision or replacement.

## Open Reduction of Chronic Elbow Dislocation

For chronic unreduced elbow dislocations (over 2-3 weeks in adults; in children regardless of duration), open reduction is performed through a posterior Campbell approach. The distal humerus is skeletonized subperiosteally; the ulnar nerve is identified and retracted; scar tissue and heterotopic ossification around and within the joint are excised with care for the cartilage; a significant portion of the fibrotic, retracted capsule is usually resected. The reduction should be easy without redislocation tendency; persistent instability calls for re-inspection of obstructing structures and, if necessary, further soft-tissue release or radial head excision. Immobilization in semi-flexion for 3 weeks followed by active rehabilitation. In combined fracture-dislocations of many months or years' duration, motion recovery is limited and arthroplasty or replacement may be preferred over reduction attempts.

## Anterior Arthrolysis for Flexion Contracture

For fixed flexion contracture of soft-tissue origin (without shortened collaterals or bony block), an anterior Henry approach is used. The lacertus fibrosus is divided, superficial veins ligated, the biceps tendon Z-lengthened, and brachialis incised obliquely or similarly lengthened. The median nerve and brachial artery are identified and protected. If needed the anterior capsule is divided and intra-articular adhesions excised. After extension is restored, the tendons are repaired with appropriate lengthening to permit passive extension. Immobilization is in maximum extension followed by early active rehabilitation. Concurrent excision of heterotopic ossification (HO) is indicated only after lesion "maturation" — clinically with pain resolution, radiographically with sharp dense margins, generally at least 6 months from trauma or prior surgery. Excision of immature HO causes recurrence; postoperative prophylaxis uses indomethacin or single-dose low-energy radiation.

## Rheumatoid Synovectomy

Elbow synovectomy is indicated in chronic rheumatoid inflammation with persistent pain, failed medical management, and relatively preserved articular surfaces (Larsen I-II). The approach is usually lateral via Kocher, supplemented as needed by posterior or medial incisions for complete synovectomy. The radial head is routinely resected — it is often substantially damaged and resection eases access to the radiocarpal gutter and proximal radioulnar joint. Early mobilization (24-48 hours postoperatively) is critical to preserve range of motion.

Volkmann ischemic contracture of the forearm flexor mass is the late sequel of unrecognized or undertreated ischemia after pediatric supracondylar fracture, post-traumatic brachial artery compression, or prolonged tourniquet ischemia. Median nerve decompression at pronator teres and the carpal tunnel, and ulnar nerve transposition for accompanying cubitus valgus, are routine adjuncts. ## Summary and Take-Home Points

The elbow joint, with its complex combination of hinge and pivot articulations, requires careful attention to the surrounding neurovascular structures during surgical approaches. The Kocher approach uses the internervous interval between anconeus (radial nerve) and extensor carpi ulnaris (PIN) to access the lateral elbow, with the posterior interosseous nerve being the principal neurological concern, minimized by forearm supination during dissection. The approach provides access to the radial head, capitellum, and lateral collateral ligament complex. The posterior approaches (triceps-splitting, paratricipital, triceps-reflecting, olecranon osteotomy) provide access to the distal humerus and ulnohumeral joint, with the choice depending on the planned procedure. The olecranon osteotomy provides the best visualization for complex intra-articular distal humerus fractures. The medial approach provides access to the medial epicondyle, MCL, and ulnar nerve, with the ulnar nerve being the principal neurological concern. The approach is used for MCL reconstruction (Tommy John surgery), medial coronoid fixation, and ulnar nerve transposition. The anterior approach uses a lazy-S incision crossing the cubital crease at an angle to avoid scar contracture, with development through the lacertus fibrosus to expose the brachial artery, median nerve, and the cubital fossa contents. The approach is used for vascular injury repair, distal biceps tendon repair, and anterior capsulectomy in stiff elbow surgery.

The principal neurovascular structures at risk in elbow surgery are the ulnar nerve (cubital tunnel), the posterior interosseous nerve (arcade of Frohse), the median nerve and brachial artery (cubital fossa), and the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve (subcutaneous, vulnerable to neuroma formation).

From the classical Bulgarian and Russian schools, the elbow surgeon should remember: the posterior midline approach with triangular tendinous flap for triceps lengthening; the medial approach with medial epicondyle osteotomy for wide anterior and medial exposure; the Hohmann and Bosworth procedures for tennis elbow and the modern Nirschl analogue; the classical anatomic rules for the PIN (radial nerve bifurcation above the supinator's upper margin; deep branch entering the canalis supinatorius 2-3.5 cm distal to the radial head); the flexion-restoration transfers (Clark of pectoralis major, Hovnanian of latissimus dorsi, Steindler flexorplasty) and the biceps supinator-to-pronator rerouting; the classical Learmonth submuscular ulnar nerve transposition; the arthrodeses (Putti-Wittek, Brittain, Campbell, Steindler) and Ilizarov compression arthrodesis; the fascia lata interposition arthroplasty per Hass and Konforti-Paneva; MCL reconstruction per Konforti-Paneva and the modern Tommy John equivalent of Jobe; the Konforti-Paneva polyethylene distal humeral hemiarthroplasty (Bakeen-Vainio modification) and semi-constrained Coonrad-Morrey hinge replacement; tension-band wiring of the olecranon (Weber) and the intramedullary Nachev screw; the Boyd-Anderson two-incision distal biceps reinsertion; open reduction of chronic radial head dislocation with annular ligament reconstruction using a pedicled triceps tendon graft (Hohmann); corrective supracondylar osteotomies for cubitus varus and valgus; and classical Volkmann release. This classical foundation remains relevant both for understanding the evolution of elbow surgery and for everyday revision and non-standard scenarios. The chapter that follows turns to the forearm, where the principles of anatomical reduction for forearm rotation (Topic Trauma-16) are revisited from the anatomical perspective and the principal surgical approaches to the radius and ulna are addressed.
