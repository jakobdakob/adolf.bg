## Overview
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">


The forearm spans the elbow joint proximally to the wrist joint distally and is unique among segments of the upper limb in containing two parallel long bones — the radius and the ulna — whose spatial relationship determines the rotational function of pronation and supination. The surgical concept of forearm approaches demands preservation of the radial bow, integrity of the two radioulnar joints, and prevention of heterotopic synostosis between the bones. Neurovascular risk concentrates in the deep motor branch of the radial nerve in the proximal third, in its superficial branch along the radial border of the distal third, and in the median nerve with its anterior interosseous branch along the depth of the volar compartment. The chapter presents the layered anatomy, the principal volar and dorsal approaches (Henry, Thompson, direct ulnar, posterior Cadenat), decompressive fasciotomy for acute compartment syndrome, the reconstructive operations for established Volkmann's contracture, the catalogue of classical tendon transfers for median, ulnar, and radial nerve palsy, the plating and intramedullary techniques of the Bulgarian school of Boychev, Koncherov, and Paneva, the corrections of chronic Monteggia, Madelung deformity, and congenital radioulnar synostosis, the Krukenberg cineplasty of the amputation stump with the Albrecht and Holevich modifications, and the denervation and arthrodesis procedures of the wrist.

</section>

### Bony Anatomy and Interosseous Membrane
<section class="topic-subsection" id="bony-anatomy">

**Radius.** The lateral and principal load-bearing bone of the upper limb. The proximal end consists of the disc-shaped radial head, the narrow radial neck, and the radial tuberosity (insertion of the biceps tendon). The shaft carries its characteristic lateral bow with maximum prominence at the junction of the proximal and middle thirds — the radial bow, whose anatomical restoration is critical for normal rotation. The distal radius widens and articulates with the proximal carpal row, with a mean radial inclination of 22°, volar tilt of 11°, and radial length of 11 mm — morphometric values used to judge the effect of a distal fracture (Topic Trauma-17).

**Ulna.** The medial, essentially straight bone, providing the stable axis of rotation. Its proximal end carries the olecranon posteriorly and the coronoid process anteriorly, with the trochlear notch between them articulating with the trochlea of the distal humerus. The shaft has a subcutaneous posterior border palpable from the olecranon to the ulnar styloid — the anatomical basis for the direct ulnar approach. The distal end bears the ulnar head with its characteristic styloid, which anchors the central fold of the triangular fibrocartilage complex.

**Interosseous membrane.** Connects the two bones along their length. The fibres of the central band run obliquely from proximal-lateral (radius) to distal-medial (ulna) at a mean of 21°, transmitting normal axial load from the wrist (received by the radius) to the elbow (delivered to the ulna). Additional structures: the dorsal oblique band of Henry (proximal radius to distal ulna), the volar oblique band, and the distal oblique band reaching the triangular fibrocartilage. Loss of the central band in Essex-Lopresti injury (radial head fracture + DRUJ dislocation + membrane rupture) converts the elbow and wrist into an unstable chain and mandates reconstruction of the radial head rather than its resection.

**Proximal and distal radioulnar joints.** The proximal joint — between the radial head and the radial notch of the ulna — is stabilised by the annular ligament, the quadrate ligament, and the upper end of the interosseous membrane. The distal joint — between the ulnar head and the sigmoid notch of the radius — is stabilised by the volar and dorsal radioulnar ligaments (components of the triangular fibrocartilage complex), the membrane itself, pronator quadratus, and the ulnar collateral ligament.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The radial bow must be restored to millimetric precision — deviation of more than 4–5 mm reduces rotation by over 20% and is the principal predictor of functional failure after both-bone diaphyseal fractures.</li>
    <li>The central band of the interosseous membrane is a key stabiliser of radial length — in Essex-Lopresti injury the radial head is NOT resected; it is reconstructed or replaced with a prosthesis.</li>
    <li>The DRUJ is stable only when the volar and dorsal radioulnar ligaments, the triangular fibrocartilage, and pronator quadratus are simultaneously functional — isolated loss of any one structure by more than 50% destabilises the joint.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<section class="topic-subsection" id="compartments">

The forearm has two principal fascial compartments — volar (flexor-pronator) and dorsal (extensor-supinator). The mobile wad of Henry (brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus, extensor carpi radialis brevis) is regarded by many authors as a third, separate compartment along the radial border.

**Volar compartment — three layers.** *Superficial layer* from radial to ulnar border: pronator teres, flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus (absent in about 14% of the population — clinically important when planning a Camitz transfer), flexor carpi ulnaris. They originate from the common flexor tendon at the medial epicondyle. *Intermediate layer*: flexor digitorum superficialis (origin from the medial epicondyle, the proximal radius, and the ulnar coronoid; four tendons to the middle phalanges of the long fingers). *Deep layer*: flexor digitorum profundus (from the ulna and interosseous membrane), flexor pollicis longus (from the middle third of the radius and interosseous membrane), and pronator quadratus (from the distal ulna to the distal radius).

**Dorsal compartment — two layers.** *Superficial layer* from radial to ulnar border: extensor digitorum, extensor digiti minimi, extensor carpi ulnaris — from the common extensor tendon at the lateral epicondyle. *Deep layer*: abductor pollicis longus, extensor pollicis brevis, extensor pollicis longus, extensor indicis, supinator. The deep muscles have origins distributed across the radius, ulna, and interosseous membrane.

**Mobile wad of Henry.** Brachioradialis (innervated by the radial nerve above its division), extensor carpi radialis longus (radial nerve), and extensor carpi radialis brevis (variably from the radial nerve or the posterior interosseous nerve) — three muscles that can be palpated and mobilised as a unit along the radial border. Brachioradialis provides the important muscular shield over the radial artery and the superficial branch of the radial nerve in the proximal two thirds of the forearm.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Palmaris longus is absent in about 14% of the population — clinically important when planning a Camitz transfer; preoperative Schaeffer test (wrist flexion against resistance with thumb opposition) is mandatory.</li>
    <li>The two heads of pronator teres surround the median nerve — surgical release in pronator teres syndrome divides the humeral head and the lacertus fibrosus without sacrificing the motor branches.</li>
    <li>The mobile wad of Henry retracts as a unit along the radial border — the anatomical basis for the Henry (volar) and Thompson (dorsal) intervals, which use its two flanks.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Radial nerve and its branches.** Enters the forearm anterior to the lateral epicondyle, in the interval between brachialis (medial) and brachioradialis (lateral), and at the level of the radial head divides into:

Superficial sensory branch — continues distally beneath brachioradialis, emerges subcutaneously at the junction of the middle and distal thirds (about 9 cm proximal to the radial styloid) and supplies sensation to the dorsoradial half of the wrist and to the dorsum of the thumb, index, middle, and radial half of the ring finger proximal to the distal interphalangeal joint.

Deep motor branch (posterior interosseous nerve, PIN) — enters the supinator through the arcade of Frohse (the fibrous proximal edge of the superficial head of the muscle), describes a 4–5 cm arc within the supinator tunnel, and emerges on the dorsal surface of the interosseous membrane 9–11 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle, or 1–2 cm above the distal border of supinator. PIN innervates the extensors and abductors of the thumb, extensor indicis, and the common extensor of the fingers.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The arcade of Frohse at the proximal edge of supinator is the zone of highest PIN risk during proximal radial exposure. Full supination of the forearm during the Henry volar approach moves the nerve medially, away from the radial neck; full pronation during the Thompson dorsal approach moves it radially.</li>
    <li>The superficial branch of the radial nerve emerges subcutaneously about 9 cm proximal to the radial styloid — skin incisions along the radial border of the distal forearm (including the volar approach to the distal radius) risk a painful neuroma if the branch is divided.</li>
    <li>The muscle fibres of supinator must not be split in their proximal third because this places PIN directly on the blade; access to the radial neck from the dorsal side requires release in the distal third of the muscle or subperiosteal detachment under full pronation.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Median nerve and anterior interosseous nerve.** The median nerve enters the forearm between the two heads of pronator teres (where it may be entrapped in pronator teres syndrome), passes deep to the fibrous arch of flexor digitorum superficialis, and continues between it and flexor digitorum profundus along the midline. At the wrist it passes beneath the flexor retinaculum in the carpal tunnel.

The anterior interosseous nerve branches 5–8 cm distal to the medial epicondyle, runs along the volar surface of the interosseous membrane, and innervates flexor pollicis longus, the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus (index and middle), and pronator quadratus. It is a purely motor nerve — anterior interosseous nerve palsy (Kiloh-Nevin syndrome) is recognised by inability to form the OK sign between the thumb and index finger (loss of flexor pollicis longus and of the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus).

**Ulnar nerve.** Enters the forearm through the cubital tunnel between the humeral and ulnar heads of flexor carpi ulnaris. Runs deep to flexor carpi ulnaris, ulnar to flexor digitorum profundus, innervating both muscles (the humeral head of flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus for the ring and little fingers). It gives off a dorsal sensory branch at the junction of the middle and distal thirds (for the dorsoulnar half of the wrist and the dorsum of the little and ulnar half of the ring finger proximal to the distal interphalangeal joint), and continues into Guyon's canal at the wrist.

**Arterial anatomy.** The brachial artery divides at the level of the radial neck into the radial and ulnar arteries. *Radial artery* — runs along the radial border, deep to brachioradialis in the proximal two thirds and superficial in the distal third (where it is easily palpable for the pulse); the main vessel in the Henry interval. *Ulnar artery* — runs along the ulnar border, deep to flexor carpi ulnaris; in the proximal third the artery and nerve are separated by muscular tissue and converge only distally (practically important when revising ulnar trauma, where the nerve may not accompany the artery in the proximal wound). *Common interosseous artery* — branches from the ulnar, divides into an anterior interosseous trunk (running with the anterior interosseous nerve along the volar surface of the interosseous membrane) and a posterior interosseous trunk (running with PIN in the dorsal compartment).

### Pirogov's Deep Volar Space
<section class="topic-subsection" id="pirogov">
<div class="ts-body">

In the distal third of the volar compartment lies the specific space described by N. I. Pirogov — *spatium antebrachii profundum* — 5–6 cm long, bounded posteriorly by the interosseous membrane and pronator quadratus and anteriorly by flexor digitorum profundus and flexor pollicis longus. The space contains loose areolar tissue continuous proximally with the forearm planes and distally with the contents of the carpal tunnel, forming a continuous channel between hand and forearm.

Its clinical significance is central in suppurative infections: volar palmar phlegmons spread retrograde through the carpal tunnel into Pirogov's space; horseshoe abscess (passage of infection between the radial and ulnar bursae through the space) is the classical mechanism for rapid spread of flexor sheath infection from thumb to little finger and back. Adequate surgical drainage requires an extensive volar forearm incision combined with carpal tunnel release, exposure of the space by lifting flexor digitorum profundus and flexor pollicis longus from the membrane, and curettage of the granulation tissue with drainage for 5–7 days.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Missed drainage of Pirogov's space in palmar phlegmon leads to ascending forearm spread with risk of sepsis and to necrosis of the deep flexors with the functional equivalent of Volkmann's contracture.</li>
    <li>Drainage requires a full-length volar forearm incision combined with carpal tunnel release — not isolated "minimal" palm incisions that do not reach the space.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Surface Anatomy and Landmarks
<section class="topic-subsection" id="landmarks">
<div class="ts-body">

The anterior forearm region extends between a line about 6 cm distal to the humeral epicondyles and a line about 3 cm proximal to the distal wrist crease. The volar skin is thin, mobile, and hair-bearing in its radial half; the subcutaneous tissue contains the cephalic vein in the radial part (accompanied by the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve — terminal branch of the musculocutaneous), the basilic vein in the ulnar part (with the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve), and the median antebrachial vein along the midline. The deep fascia is continuous with the brachial fascia, fuses with the medial border of the ulna, and gives off intermuscular septa that delineate the mobile wad and the anterior fascial compartment.

Palpable landmarks: the radial tuberosity for the level of radial nerve division (approximately the radial neck); the biceps tendon proximally for the start of the Henry approach; the radial styloid distally; the ulnar styloid normally 1–2 mm distal to the radial (positive or negative ulnar variance is judged on a standard posteroanterior radiograph). The subcutaneous border of the ulna is palpable along its entire length between olecranon and ulnar styloid — the line for the direct ulnar approach.

Projection lines: radial artery — from a point 1.5 cm lateral to the midpoint of the intercondylar line to a point 1.5 cm medial to the radial styloid. Ulnar artery in the upper third — from a point 0.5 cm lateral to the midpoint of the antecubital crease to the medial border of the pisiform. Ulnar nerve — line between the medial epicondyle and the pisiform. Median nerve — medial border of the biceps tendon proximally to the midpoint between the two styloids distally.

## Pathophysiology and Mechanism
<section class="topic-section" id="pathophysiology">


**Acute compartment syndrome.** The classical pathogenetic mechanism of Volkmann's ischaemic contracture is acute elevation of tissue pressure within a closed fascial compartment above the level of capillary perfusion pressure (absolute values of 60–80 mmHg, or perfusion pressure within 30 mmHg of diastolic). Causes in the forearm: supracondylar humeral fracture in a child with excessive elbow flexion in a cast, brachial artery rupture, severe crush, circumferential cast or dressing, extravasation of intravenous infusate, reperfusion after prolonged ischaemia, bleeding into the volar compartment in coagulopathy. Primary ischaemia of the superficial flexors and deeply of flexor digitorum profundus with flexor pollicis longus leads to myofibrous necrosis (Seddon's "volar compact core"). Survival of the superficial layers with fibrous replacement of the deep ones creates the characteristic configuration of the established contracture — forearm pronation, wrist flexion, interphalangeal flexion with thenar atrophy when the median and ulnar nerves are simultaneously injured.

**Pronator teres syndrome.** Compression of the median nerve between the two heads of pronator teres, under the lacertus fibrosus, or under the arch of flexor digitorum superficialis. Provoked by repetitive pronator effort, presenting with dysesthesia in the median nerve territory, volar proximal forearm pain, and nocturnal awakenings. Distinguished from carpal tunnel syndrome by preserved sensation in the palmar cutaneous zone (the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve arises proximal to the wrist, distal to pronator teres but proximal to the carpal tunnel).

**Kiloh-Nevin syndrome (anterior interosseous nerve palsy).** Spontaneous or traumatic injury to the anterior interosseous nerve with paralysis of flexor pollicis longus and of the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus (for the index), with preserved sensation. Classical test — inability to form the OK sign between the thumb and index finger; the patient compensates with interphalangeal hyperextension in an attempt at pinch.

**Posterior interosseous nerve syndrome.** Compression of the deep motor branch of the radial nerve at the arcade of Frohse, within supinator itself (especially with a thickened fibrous edge), by a lipoma at the radial neck, or by post-traumatic deformity. Paralyses the extensors of the fingers and thumb with preserved wrist extension (because extensor carpi radialis longus is innervated by the radial nerve proximal to the compression) and preserved sensation (purely motor nerve). Radial deviation of the wrist is characteristic owing to unopposed action of extensor carpi radialis longus.

**Radial tunnel syndrome.** A purely pain-equivalent of compression at the arcade of Frohse, without motor or sensory deficit. Distinguished from lateral epicondylitis by the location of maximum tenderness — 3–5 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle rather than over it.

</section>



## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis
<section class="topic-section" id="clinical">


**Acute compartment syndrome — the five signs.** Systematised in the Bulgarian orthopaedic literature as the "five Ps": disproportionate pain unrelieved by the injury (pain), compartment tension (pressure), pallor or cyanosis (pallor), paresthesia and numbness (paresthesia), and inability to passively extend the fingers (passive stretch pain — the most reliable early sign). Loss of the radial pulse is a LATE sign — the presence of a pulse does not exclude acute ischaemia. Tissue pressure is measured with an electronic transducer (Stryker, Whitesides) by puncture of the three compartments at the level of maximum clinical tenderness; pressure above 30 mmHg in an injured limb or perfusion pressure within 30 mmHg of diastolic is an indication for fasciotomy.

**Established Volkmann's contracture.** Forearm pronation, wrist flexion, distal interphalangeal flexion with metacarpophalangeal flexion ("intrinsic-minus" position). Sensory deficit in median and ulnar territory in severe forms. The Volkmann test (finger extension with the wrist flexed — possible; with the wrist extended — not possible) distinguishes fixed finger flexion from free flexion due to flexor tightness.

**Classical nerve tests.**
- OK sign — impossible in anterior interosseous nerve palsy.
- Froment's test (pinch on a sheet of paper between thumb and index) — positive in ulnar nerve palsy (compensation by flexor pollicis longus).
- Phalen's test and Tinel's test — provoke symptoms in carpal tunnel syndrome.
- Spurling's test — for discogenic radiculopathy as a cause of distal symptoms.
- Hoffmann-Tinel sign along the course of the nerve — for neuroma at the site of injury or regeneration.

**Imaging.** Standard posteroanterior and lateral radiograph of the forearm including the elbow and wrist joints (to exclude a missed Monteggia or Galeazzi — the law of the two joints). Computed tomography for complex intra-articular fractures and to plan osteotomy of malunion. Magnetic resonance imaging for suspected tumour, infection, pronator teres syndrome, radial tunnel syndrome, and to assess soft tissue after trauma. Electrodiagnostic studies (electromyography and nerve conduction) for suspected nerve syndromes, post-traumatic injury, and when planning a tendon transfer — to assess the motor units of the potential donor muscles.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Loss of the radial pulse is a LATE sign of acute compartment syndrome — the presence of a pulse does not exclude the diagnosis and does not justify delay of fasciotomy.</li>
    <li>A missed radial head dislocation in Monteggia is a frequent error — a radiograph including the elbow joint is mandatory for every ulnar fracture, with assessment of the radiocapitellar line (a line through the centre of the radius passes through the capitellum in every projection).</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Classification
<section class="topic-section" id="classification">


**Volkmann's contracture — Tsuge.** Tsuge's classification (1975) divides the established ischaemic contracture into three severities and dictates operative tactic. *Mild* — focal necrosis involving part of flexor digitorum profundus (usually for the ring and middle fingers) with preserved flexor pollicis longus and flexor digitorum profundus for the index; usually no neurological deficit; functional reconstruction by neurolysis with or without flexor slide of Page-Scaglietti. *Moderate* — necrosis of a larger portion of flexor digitorum profundus and of flexor pollicis longus, usually with median and/or ulnar deficit; neurolysis, excisional Seddon procedure, transfer of flexor digitorum superficialis to flexor digitorum profundus (Epstein-Rozov transfer). *Severe* — necrosis of both the superficial and deep flexor layers with severe fibrosis; functional reconstruction by radical excision of necrotic muscle with free functional muscle transfer (gracilis in modern practice) or wrist arthrodesis with active pinch function from preserved elements.

**Monteggia — Bado.** Bado's classification (1967) describes four types according to the direction of radial head dislocation in association with an ulnar fracture. *Type 1* — diaphyseal ulnar fracture with anterior dislocation of the radial head (60% of adult cases). *Type 2* — diaphyseal ulnar fracture with posterior dislocation (15%; characteristic of older patients and osteoporosis). *Type 3* — metaphyseal ulnar fracture with lateral dislocation (20%; characteristic of childhood). *Type 4* — diaphyseal fractures of both bones with anterior radial head dislocation (rare, severe). The principle of treatment: anatomical osteosynthesis of the ulna automatically reduces the radial head, unless capsular or annular ligament interposition prevents it.

**Galeazzi.** A diaphyseal (typically distal third) fracture of the radius with dislocation at the distal radioulnar joint; called the "fracture of necessity" because the instability mandates internal fixation even in adults. Classification: *stable type* — DRUJ reducible and stable after plate osteosynthesis of the radius (about 60%); *unstable type* — requires transfixation of the DRUJ with a Kirschner wire or repair of associated stabilisers (ulnar styloid with triangular fibrocartilage, volar or dorsal radioulnar ligament).

**Madelung deformity.** A bilateral (more commonly) or unilateral partial agenesis of the volar-ulnar epiphyseal plate of the distal radius with compensatory dorsal subluxation of the distal ulna. Classical clinical-radiographic criteria: radial inclination greater than 30°, volar tilt greater than 25°, negative ulnar variance. Vickers severity classification — mild (symptomatic Vickers ligament on MRI with minimal radiographic deformity), moderate (radiographic deformity without arthritis), severe (degenerative DRUJ arthritis).

**Classification of approaches.** Volar (Henry); dorsal (Thompson, dorsal distal radius between third and fourth extensor compartments, posterior Cadenat to the proximal radius); medial subcutaneous (direct ulnar); combined (volar approach to the distal radius for volar plating of distal radius fractures with a watershed-line landmark).

</section>



## Treatment
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">



### Nonoperative
<section class="topic-subsection" id="nonoperative">
<div class="ts-body">

Nonoperative treatment of closed forearm fractures occupies a limited niche in adults — stable, undisplaced, or minimally angulated (under 10°) distal Colles fractures, isolated ulnar (nightstick) fractures with angulation under 10° and no shortening, and Mason type 1 radial head fractures without rotational block.

In children, nonoperative treatment remains the principal approach for most diaphyseal fractures thanks to the strong remodelling potential — accepted shortening up to 1 cm, accepted sagittal-plane angulation up to 15° under age 8 and 10° between 8 and 12 years; remodelling of rotational deformity is minimal, so any rotational malalignment is an indication for correction. Immobilisation is in an above-elbow cast with the elbow at 90° and the forearm in neutral for both-bone fractures, in supination for an isolated distal radial fracture (to counteract the pull of brachioradialis), and in pronation for a proximal isolated radial fracture (to counteract supinator and biceps pull). Follow-up radiographs at weeks 1, 2, and 4 — displacement beyond the accepted range mandates operative correction.

For acute Colles fractures with accepted reduction, the cast is maintained for 4–6 weeks in neutral wrist position with slight ulnar deviation; early functional finger exercises to avoid stiffness; early elbow mobilisation.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>An above-elbow cast is mandatory for any diaphyseal forearm fracture in adult and child — a below-elbow cast does not control rotation.</li>
    <li>A circular cast for an acute fracture is split lengthwise (univalve or bivalve) to allow for swelling in the first 48–72 hours; the risk of acute compartment syndrome from an overly tight cast is real and unacceptable.</li>
    <li>The law of the two joints — a radiograph of the elbow and wrist is mandatory for every forearm fracture to exclude a missed Monteggia or Galeazzi.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>

<summary>Henry Volar Approach to the Radius</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Described by A. K. Henry (1927) and established by *Extensile Exposure* (1945) as the universal volar approach to the radius. Covers the full length of the bone in one incision; standard for compression plating of diaphyseal fractures, for volar plating of distal radius fractures, for biopsy, and for tumour resection in the forearm region.

**Position.** Supine with the upper limb on an arm board, shoulder abducted to about 70°, elbow in full extension, forearm in full supination. Tourniquet on the proximal arm at 250 mmHg after exsanguination of the limb (exception: emergency fasciotomy for acute compartment syndrome, where a tourniquet is not used).

**Internervous plane.** Between brachioradialis (radial nerve) and flexor carpi radialis (median nerve) — a true internervous plane extensible along the full length.

**Landmarks and incision.** A line from the biceps tendon proximally (or from the antecubital crease) to the radial styloid distally. The skin incision follows the line for the required length — short for open reduction of an isolated middle-third fracture, long for tumour resection or extensive plating.

**Technique.** Skin and subcutaneous tissue — attention to the cephalic vein laterally and to the superficial radial nerve branches in the distal third. The deep fascia is opened along the incision. Brachioradialis is retracted laterally, flexor carpi radialis medially. The radial artery is identified in the interval and protected; in the proximal third it gives off the radial recurrent artery, which is ligated for high mobilisation of brachioradialis if wide exposure is required. *Proximal third.* Identification of supinator covering the radial neck. Access to the bone requires release of supinator from its radial insertion under FULL SUPINATION of the forearm, which moves the posterior interosseous nerve medially, away from the field. Dissection proceeds subperiosteally from the radial edge of the bone toward the ulnar. *Middle third.* Release of the pronator teres tendon from its radial insertion at the diaphyseal midpoint; subperiosteal mobilisation. *Distal third.* Release of pronator quadratus from its radial insertion in the distal third, reflection ulnarly to expose the volar surface of the distal radius up to the volar cortical smoothness at the wrist joint projection.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>FULL SUPINATION of the forearm when working on the proximal third — moves the posterior interosseous nerve medially, away from the radial border of the radial neck.</li>
    <li>Subperiosteal release of supinator from its radial insertion, NOT splitting of the muscle — the latter places the posterior interosseous nerve directly on the blade.</li>
    <li>The radial recurrent artery (branch of the radial) crosses the field in the proximal third and is ligated for high mobilisation of brachioradialis when wide exposure is required.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The superficial branch of the radial nerve emerges subcutaneously about 9 cm proximal to the radial styloid — a skin incision along the radial border of the distal forearm risks a painful neuroma if branches are divided; careful subcutaneous dissection protecting all visible branches.</li>
    <li>Wrong position (pronation instead of supination) when working on the proximal radius places the posterior interosseous nerve directly in the field of dissection — one of the most common causes of iatrogenic finger drop.</li>
    <li>The radial artery in the distal third is superficial — careful retraction without aggressive self-retaining retractors.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Described by J. E. Thompson (1918) for posterior exposure of the proximal half of the radius. Preferred over the Henry approach for proximal radial fractures, for selected tumour cases, and for conditions in which the volar surface is compromised by soft tissue (burns, skin defects).

**Position.** Supine with the upper limb on an arm board, shoulder abducted to 90°, elbow flexed to 90°, forearm in full pronation. Alternative — limb across the chest with the dorsal surface up. Tourniquet on the proximal arm.

**Internervous plane.** Between extensor carpi radialis brevis (radial nerve) and extensor digitorum (posterior interosseous nerve) — a true internervous plane in the proximal half; in the distal half the dissection is between extensor carpi radialis brevis and the common extensor along their natural fascial border.

**Landmarks and incision.** A line from the lateral epicondyle to Lister's tubercle on the distal radius. The skin incision follows the line.

**Technique.** Skin and subcutaneous tissue. The deep fascia is opened and the interval between extensor carpi radialis brevis (radial) and extensor digitorum (ulnar) is developed bluntly. *Proximal third.* Supinator is exposed covering the proximal radius. The posterior interosseous nerve enters supinator at the arcade of Frohse and emerges on the dorsal surface of the interosseous membrane 9–11 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle. Identification of the nerve is mandatory — it is traced upward, retracted radially, and only then is supinator released subperiosteally from the radial border of the radial neck under FULL PRONATION of the forearm (which moves the nerve further radially). *Middle third.* Release of abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis from their oblique radial origin and ulnar retraction with extensor digitorum. *Distal third.* The dorsal approach to the distal radius (between the third and fourth compartments) is a separate procedure — see below.

  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Length of the posterior interosseous nerve course within supinator: about 4–5 cm; exit on the dorsal surface — 9–11 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle or 1–2 cm above the distal border of the muscle.</li>
    <li>Full pronation of the forearm when working on the proximal third by the dorsal approach — moves the posterior interosseous nerve radially, away from the field of dissection.</li>
    <li>Identification of the posterior interosseous nerve is mandatory before release of supinator — a nerve in the field must be seen, not assumed, before it is protected.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Direct Ulnar Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The subcutaneous posterior border of the ulna allows a straight surgical corridor without the need for intermuscular dissection. Standard for plate osteosynthesis of isolated diaphyseal ulnar fractures, for shortening osteotomy in ulnar impaction syndrome, for tumour resection, and for biopsy.

**Position.** Supine with the upper limb on an arm board, forearm in neutral or slight supination — a position that brings the subcutaneous border uppermost.

**Landmarks and incision.** The subcutaneous border of the ulna from olecranon to ulnar styloid. The skin incision is straight, directly over the border, of the appropriate length.

**Technique.** Skin and subcutaneous tissue. The deep fascia is opened directly over the border. Flexor carpi ulnaris (with the ulnar nerve in its depth) is retracted ulnarly; extensor carpi ulnaris is retracted radially. Subperiosteal dissection from both sides of the border exposes the ulnar diaphysis.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve crosses the ulnar side of the forearm at the junction of the middle and distal thirds — protecting it prevents a painful neuroma over the dorsoulnar wrist.</li>
    <li>For simultaneous osteosynthesis of both forearm bones, the direct ulnar approach is used in parallel with the Henry approach through SEPARATE incisions — not through a single unified posterior incision of Cadenat, because of the risk of radioulnar synostosis.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Posterior Cadenat Approach to Both Bones</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Described for simultaneous access to the radius and ulna in severe both-bone injuries, gunshot wounds, and tumour resections with extensive involvement. Rarely used in modern practice because of the elevated risk of radioulnar synostosis, but recognised in the Bulgarian school and relevant for segmental defects of both bones.

**Position.** Supine with the forearm in pronation.

**Technique.** The skin incision follows the posterior border of the ulna; the subcutaneous position allows direct subperiosteal exposure. The muscles inserting on the dorsal surface of the ulna are released subperiosteally up to the interosseous membrane, which together with the distal border of supinator serves as the landmark for crossing to the dorsal surface of the radius. Supinator is released from the radius and carefully retracted to protect the posterior interosseous nerve; abductor pollicis longus, extensor pollicis brevis, and extensor pollicis longus are released subperiosteally and lifted from the radius.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Simultaneous plate osteosynthesis of both bones through a SINGLE dorsal incision (Cadenat) carries a real risk of radioulnar synostosis — two separate incisions (Henry for the radius and direct ulnar for the ulna) are the biomechanically safer tactic.</li>
    <li>In multilevel or segmental fractures requiring shared exposure, the radius is osteosynthesised first to restore the radial bow, and then the ulna is reduced to the correct length.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Approaches to the Neurovascular Trunks</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Radial artery.** Classical projection — from a point 1.5 cm lateral to the midpoint of the intercondylar line to a point 1.5 cm medial to the radial styloid. Used for direct vascular access for haemodialysis fistula, for radial artery graft harvest for coronary bypass (preoperative Allen test to assess collateral circulation), for exploration of penetrating trauma, and for tumour biopsy. The approach is performed in supination along the projection line; the fascia is opened with particular care in the distal third, where the artery is superficial. In the interval between brachioradialis (radial) and flexor carpi radialis (ulnar) the vessel is exposed together with its two venae comitantes and the bundle is mobilised over the required length.

**Ulnar artery and ulnar nerve.** Projection of the ulnar nerve — line between the medial epicondyle and the pisiform. Projection of the ulnar artery in the upper third — from a point 0.5 cm lateral to the midpoint of the antecubital crease to the medial border of the pisiform. The skin incision is along the lateral border of flexor carpi ulnaris; the fascia is opened, the muscle is retracted medially, and in the depth the ulnar artery (medial) and the ulnar nerve (slightly ulnar to the artery) are exposed.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>In the upper third of the forearm the ulnar artery and ulnar nerve are separated by considerable muscular interposition and converge into a common bundle only distally — practically important during revision for ulnar arterial trauma, where the nerve may not accompany the artery in the proximal wound.</li>
    <li>A preoperative Allen test before radial artery harvest for coronary bypass or arteriovenous fistula is mandatory to exclude isolated radial-dominant circulation.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Decompressive Fasciotomy for Acute Compartment Syndrome</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Acute forearm compartment syndrome is an emergency with a 4–6 hour window of reversibility from the onset of ischaemia. The indication for fasciotomy is clinical or based on pressure monitoring — persistence of the characteristic symptoms beyond 30–60 minutes after release of external pressure (circular cast, dressings), elbow extension, and limb elevation, or measured pressure above 30 mmHg in an injured limb or perfusion pressure within 30 mmHg of diastolic.

**Position.** Supine with the upper limb on an arm board in supination. A tourniquet is NOT used — it compromises residual perfusion and masks clinical assessment.

**Volar fasciotomy (Bulgarian technique after Boychev).** The skin incision begins above the antecubital crease medial to the biceps tendon, continues volar across the forearm along a curvilinear (zigzag) line that avoids straight lines across joints, and ends at the level of the radiocarpal crease, crossing obliquely through the projection of the carpal tunnel. The tense deep fascia is divided with sufficient lateral extent so that the anterior muscle bed is fully decompressed. Beneath, the lacertus fibrosus is identified and divided. The epimysial sheaths of the individual muscles are progressively released — superficial and deep flexor layers, flexor pollicis longus, and pronator quadratus — and haematoma from the anterior bed is evacuated. The brachial artery is exposed under the elbow; if a local anaesthetic block fails to restore the pulse, the artery is followed distally and revised for compression by bone fragments or intravascular thrombosis (indication for thrombectomy or resection of the thrombosed segment).

**Dorsal and mobile-wad fasciotomy.** An additional straight or slightly curvilinear incision down the middle of the dorsal surface decompresses the dorsal compartment and the mobile wad. Carpal tunnel release is added at the distal end of the volar incision if needed.

**Closure.** The wound is NOT closed primarily — the skin is left open for delayed closure on day 5–10 or covered with split-thickness skin graft if soft-tissue tension is severe. Postoperatively the limb is immobilised with the wrist in dorsiflexion and the fingers extended.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>A tourniquet is NOT used during fasciotomy for acute compartment syndrome — it masks clinical assessment and compromises marginal perfusion.</li>
    <li>Median nerve decompression (lacertus fibrosus proximally, fibrous arch of flexor digitorum superficialis, pronator teres arcade, and carpal tunnel distally) is a mandatory component — nerve-involved compartment syndrome is not solved by compartment fasciotomy alone.</li>
    <li>The wound is left open for delayed closure; primary closure after acute fasciotomy is a mistake leading to recompression and failure of the procedure.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Reconstruction for Established Volkmann's Contracture</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

When diagnosis is delayed or primary decompression is inadequate, the established Volkmann's contracture develops — fibrous replacement of the flexors with retraction, forearm pronation, wrist flexion, interphalangeal flexion, and sometimes deficit of the median and ulnar nerves. The choice of operative tactic is determined by Tsuge severity.

**Flexor slide of Page-Scaglietti.** In an established contracture with still viable muscle, the classical technique of Page (1923), modified by Scaglietti, releases the common flexor origin subperiosteally from the medial epicondyle (together with the origin of pronator teres and the origins of flexor digitorum profundus and flexor pollicis longus from the radial and ulnar diaphyses) and slides the entire flexor mass caudally under clinical control of the stretch until full wrist and finger extension is possible. The median and ulnar nerves are identified and protected throughout; at the end of the procedure Z-lengthening of any residually shortened tendons is performed. The advantage is preservation of flexor function with elimination of the static component of deformity.

**Seddon excisional procedure.** For established contracture with infarction (necrosis) of the deep flexor group and severe scarring, the first step is a curvilinear or 5-shaped volar incision from the antecubital crease to the wrist crease with excision of the thickened fascia. The necrotic muscles (typically flexor digitorum profundus, flexor pollicis longus, sometimes partially flexor digitorum superficialis) are excised; the nerves are freed from the scar conglomerate — neurolysis is central to the functional result and itself restores significant sensory and motor function in moderate cases.

**Transfer of flexor digitorum superficialis to flexor digitorum profundus (Epstein-Rozov).** Bulgarian technique described by Epstein and Rozov: in a case with relatively preserved superficial flexors and severe degeneration of the deep ones, the superficial finger flexor is transposed by side-to-side suture to the deep finger flexor tendons. The flexor digitorum superficialis tendons are Z-lengthened until each finger sits in physiological position, and the palm and wrist are immobilised in wrist dorsiflexion and metacarpophalangeal flexion for 21 days, followed by prolonged functional rehabilitation.

**Pronator teres rerouting as supinator (Bunnell, Bulgarian adaptation).** For mixed forms with pronator contracture. Through a 5–6 cm volar-radial incision the fascia is opened and brachioradialis with the superficial branch of the radial nerve is retracted; in pronation the pronator teres tendon is released maximally distally from the radial surface of the radius. The muscle belly is mobilised proximally, looped around the radius (around the posterior surface of the radius), and reinserted with reversed vector so that its contraction now produces supination. A Bunnell concept adapted in the Bulgarian school for spastic or post-poliomyelitic pronator contracture.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Tsuge algorithm for established Volkmann's: mild — neurolysis of the median and ulnar nerves ± Page-Scaglietti flexor slide; moderate — flexor slide with excision of the necrotic deep flexors and Epstein-Rozov side-to-side transfer of flexor digitorum superficialis to flexor digitorum profundus; severe — radical excision with free functional gracilis transfer (modern option) or wrist arthrodesis with active pinch from preserved elements.</li>
    <li>Decompression of the nerves during the excisional procedure is central — often undervalued as part of the Seddon operation, yet alone it restores significant function in moderate cases.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Tendon Transfers for Median Nerve Palsy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**High palsy.** In high median nerve palsy (traumatic injury above the take-off of the anterior interosseous nerve), of the volar muscles only the deep flexors for the ring and little fingers (from the ulnar branch to flexor digitorum profundus) and flexor carpi ulnaris are preserved. For adequate grip the deep flexor for the index and middle fingers, flexor pollicis longus, and thumb opposition must be replaced.

The classical retrograde transposition of the superficial flexors after Epstein-Rozov (with similar descriptions by Bunnell) joins the preserved flexor digitorum profundus for the ring and little fingers with the paralysed ones for the index and middle fingers by side-to-side suture, so that the driver of the preserved side (ulnar nerve) activates all four long fingers together. The flexor pollicis longus tendon is lengthened with a preserved protein-hydrolysate allograft passed through a subcutaneous tunnel to the thumb metacarpophalangeal joint. Alternatively flexor carpi ulnaris with a graft is used. Restoration of thumb opposition is performed by a transferred muscle in the same operation.

**Low palsy (opponensplasty).** Restoration of opposition in low median palsy (injury distal to the anterior interosseous nerve) is the central surgical task and the subject of many classical techniques:

*Bunnell technique* (Z. T. Bunnell). Flexor digitorum superficialis of the ring finger is used as the motor. The tendon is divided over the proximal interphalangeal joint, withdrawn retrograde through a volar incision on the distal forearm, looped around the distal end of flexor carpi ulnaris (in the classical description together with a suture around the pisiform), and then guided through a subcutaneous tunnel across the thenar crease to the abductor pollicis brevis insertion.

*Merle d'Aubigné technique.* Through an incision over the proximal crease of the ring finger the flexor digitorum superficialis slips are exposed and divided as distally as possible; through a second small incision proximal to the radiocarpal joint the tendon is withdrawn retrograde across flexor carpi ulnaris. The third incision is on the dorsoulnar aspect of the thumb metacarpophalangeal joint, where the slips are sutured with appropriate tension so that the thumb adopts a position of opposition and palmar abduction.

*Camitz and the Bulgarian Stoycheva modification.* Palmaris longus with its palmar aponeurosis is transposed through a subcutaneous tunnel to the thenar — particularly suitable in carpal tunnel syndrome with severe thenar atrophy and in childhood paralysis, because it is technically simple and does not sacrifice a strong flexor. The Bulgarian Stoycheva modification uses flexor carpi radialis, divided at the musculotendinous junction and reversed 180° distally, passed subcutaneously across the palm along the diagonal from the thumb metacarpophalangeal joint to the pisiform and anchored at the opponens insertion. Additional stabilisation by a Kirschner wire between the first and second metacarpals, removed on day 45; supportive bandaging is recommended up to 6 months.

*Littler technique.* Abductor digiti minimi is mobilised on its own neurovascular pedicle and rotated palmar through a subcutaneous tunnel to the thenar — valuable when the superficial flexors cannot be sacrificed (e.g., concurrent ulnar nerve palsy) and when a "living" muscular bulk in the thenar is sought for cosmesis.

*Paneva technique.* Through two longitudinal volar incisions on the distal forearm, palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis are divided proximal to the radiocarpal joint; both muscles are lengthened with preserved allografts harvested with paratenon and weave-sutured. Lengthened palmaris longus is looped around flexor carpi ulnaris as a pulley and then through a subcutaneous tunnel in the direction of opponens; lengthened flexor carpi radialis is routed toward abductor pollicis brevis. The two grafts are anchored to the metacarpal and proximal phalanx with the thumb in maximum opposition, palmar abduction, and radial rotation, with the wrist at about 20° flexion.

*Matev technique.* A functionally elegant solution that restores not only opposition but also active abduction; through the thenar crease a double anchoring is placed — one slip of the transfer to the extensor pollicis brevis insertion (for proximal phalangeal extension), the other to adductor pollicis (for palmar arch stabilisation).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Choice of opponensplasty in low median palsy: preserved palmaris longus and a young patient — Camitz (simple, fast); thenar atrophy with intact sensory loss and need for strong function — Bunnell with ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis; concurrent ulnar nerve palsy — Littler (abductor digiti minimi) to avoid sacrificing flexor digitorum superficialis; childhood and post-poliomyelitic cases — Stoycheva (flexor carpi radialis tenodesis reversed 180°).</li>
    <li>The Matev technique provides a double anchorage to extensor pollicis brevis and adductor pollicis for simultaneous stabilisation of the proximal phalanx and the palmar arch.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>

<details>
<summary>Tendon Transfers for Ulnar Nerve Palsy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The aim in high or low ulnar palsy is restoration of metacarpophalangeal flexion, thumb adduction, and index abduction for precision pinch — a combination whose simultaneous restoration prevents the progressive claw deformity and grip weakness.

**Bunnell-Stiles correction of claw deformity.** Flexor digitorum superficialis of the ring and little fingers (or of all four ulnar fingers in total claw) is divided near its insertions and withdrawn proximally; each slip is passed lateral to its own finger and sutured to the radial side of the dorsal hood at the proximal phalanx (lateral band). When all four fingers are deformed, the middle-finger flexor digitorum superficialis tendon supplies the radial side of the middle and ring fingers, and the ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis tendon — the ulnar side of the ring and little fingers. Immobilisation in a cast with flexed metacarpophalangeal joints and extended interphalangeal joints for 21 days.

**Brand technique.** Extensor carpi radialis brevis is detached from the base of the third metacarpal, lengthened with a palmaris longus autograft (or a combination of two grafts if needed), passed through the interosseous spaces to the lateral bands of the four fingers, and sutured with the metacarpophalangeal joints in 75° flexion and the interphalangeal joints neutral. Brand is particularly useful in low ulnar palsy with associated weakness of the radial slip of flexor digitorum profundus, because it provides a strong synergist.

**Restoration of index abduction.** When the first dorsal interosseous is non-functional (signing one's name is impossible), extensor pollicis brevis or extensor indicis is transposed to the radial lateral band of the index (Royle-Thompson and modifications). When index abduction and thumb adduction are simultaneously lost, the extensor indicis tendon is divided into two slips, one to the radial lateral band of the index, the other to the adductor pollicis insertion.

**Matev technique for restoration of thumb proximal-phalangeal flexion.** Through a series of three small incisions the ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis tendon is divided and withdrawn proximal to the carpal tunnel, then re-routed through the tunnel along the path of flexor pollicis longus and anchored to the thumb proximal phalanx through a transverse bone tunnel — an original Bulgarian modification for claw deformity combined with thumb adduction deficit.

**Restoration of the palmar arch and thumb adduction (Littler/Matev).** Through an incision proximal to the flexor retinaculum the middle- or ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis is withdrawn; one slip is anchored at the adductor pollicis insertion, the other is passed transversely across the palm to the base of the thumb proximal phalanx or to the neck of the fifth metacarpal, reconstructing the palmar arch.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Tendon Transfers for Radial Nerve Palsy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

High radial nerve palsy means division of the nerve proximal to the muscular branches for the wrist extensors — typically in the middle third of the arm within the radial groove (where the nerve lies in immediate contact with the humerus and is frequently injured by gunshot wounds, open fractures, or extensive scar adherence). When primary repair or graft is impossible (extensive defect, poor soft-tissue bed), tendon transfers are performed without prolonged delay — free nerve graft is rarely justified because of the large time interval to reinnervation and the limited functional result.

The historical development begins with Franke (1898), who transposed flexor carpi ulnaris to the common finger extensor and flexor carpi radialis to the long thumb extensor after preliminary shortening of the wrist extensors. The concept was developed by Perthes, Dzhanelidze, Tonchev, Tikhonovich, Jaschar (Chaklar), and Merle d'Aubigné.

**Classical principles (formulated by Jaschar/Chaklar):**

1. At least one wrist flexor must be preserved as a stabiliser of the radiocarpal joint during finger extension.
2. Active wrist extension must be restored — its synergistic extension with finger flexion is the condition for stable fist grip.
3. Active extension of the proximal phalanges of the long fingers and of the distal phalanx of the thumb must be restored — extensor digitorum and extensor pollicis longus can be driven by a common motor.
4. The long and short extensors of the thumb are synergists for extension but antagonists for abduction-adduction; therefore separate motors are recommended for the short extensor and the abductor.

**Transfers after Jaschar/Chaklar.** Pronator teres → wrist extensors (extensor carpi radialis longus/brevis); flexor carpi ulnaris → extensor digitorum + extensor pollicis longus; palmaris longus (or a middle- or ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis if palmaris longus is absent) → abductor pollicis longus + extensor pollicis brevis.

**Transfers after Boyes.** Pronator teres → extensor carpi radialis brevis (wrist extension); flexor carpi radialis → abductor pollicis longus + extensor pollicis brevis; middle-finger flexor digitorum superficialis (through a window in the interosseous membrane) → extensor digitorum communis; ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis (also through the interosseous window) → extensor pollicis longus + extensor indicis. Boyes is technically more demanding but provides independent thumb extension and segmental fine control — particularly useful for trained patients and musicians.

**Low palsy (posterior interosseous nerve syndrome).** Most commonly flexor carpi radialis (or flexor carpi ulnaris) → extensor digitorum + extensor pollicis longus through the classical dorsal transverse transfer plane. Because the wrist extensors are synergists of finger flexors rather than finger extensors, some authors prefer in low palsy to transpose flexor carpi ulnaris to the extensors in the typical way and extensor carpi radialis longus to the thumb abductors.

  <strong>NB</strong>

When palmaris longus is absent, the second motor is sought in middle- or ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis.</li>

<summary>Compression Plating of Both Bones</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Laboratoires Servier, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Forearm_fracture_-_Radius_fracture_1_--_Smart-Servier.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>

Standard for diaphyseal forearm fractures in adults. AO principles (Müller, Allgöwer, Schneider, 1969) include anatomical reduction with restoration of the radial bow, interfragmentary compression by lag screw or dynamic compression plate with oval holes, absolute stability for primary bone healing, and early functional mobilisation.

**Classical indications in the Bulgarian orthopaedic tradition (Paneva et al.):** transverse and slightly oblique "difficult to reduce and hold" acute diaphyseal fractures, neglected unreduced fractures, malunions, and atrophic non-unions. The approach to the radius is most commonly Henry (volar-lateral); Thompson (dorsal) is preferred for middle-third fractures when the volar side is compromised by soft tissues.

**Self-compression plate principle (Paneva).** The plate has oval holes oriented toward the fragment with the larger compact bone mass; screws are placed first through the round holes, then a screw in the terminal oval hole is tightened until its head slides along the oblique slope of the hole by about 50% of its length (generating axial compression); only then is a second compression screw placed and the two are tightened alternately to full compression. Periosteal stripping is economical — only on the surface where the plate will lie. Postoperatively the limb is immobilised in mid-rotation (about 20° supination for an isolated radial plate, neutral for two plates) until union (mean 12 weeks).

**Atrophic non-union.** Osteosynthesis is combined with bone autografting — spongious chips from the iliac crest are placed around the fragment ends; for a bone defect an autograft is interposed between the fragments before compression. In significant radial shortening (loss of the radial bow and DRUJ asymmetry) a Darrach distal ulna resection is performed before compression to balance the two-bone length — the "balance manoeuvre."

**Simultaneous osteosynthesis.** The preferred mode in the Bulgarian school is intramedullary nailing of the ulna with reaming + compression plating of the radius, but plate osteosynthesis of both bones through two separate incisions is acceptable. Two plate approaches through ONE incision (Cadenat) are not recommended because of the risk of radioulnar synostosis.

    <li>The radial bow is restored first — anatomical radial reduction determines the possibility of anatomical ulnar reduction, not the other way around.</li>
    <li>Plate length — at least six cortical screw purchases on each side of the fracture (best practice), or at least eight in oblique and complex fractures.</li>
    <li>Two plate osteosyntheses through SEPARATE incisions — NOT through a common dorsal incision (Cadenat), because of the risk of radioulnar synostosis.</li>

<details>
<summary>Intramedullary Nailing with Reaming</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For diaphyseal ulnar fractures the classical Bulgarian approach (Boychev, Koncherov) is open intramedullary nailing with reaming and a Koncherov nail. The dorsal surface of the ulna is exposed; through the olecranon tip the guide wire is brought out orthograde with a drill or gentle tapping, and a nail of appropriate diameter is inserted after reaming with progressively larger reamers.

**Errors to avoid:**

1. Splitting of fragments when the nail diameter mismatches the canal diameter.
2. Creation of a false path when the reamer is used without a previously placed guide.
3. Penetration of the nail close to the articular cartilage of the olecranon (requires bending of the distal nail end against the dorsal cortex).
4. Heterotopic ossification around the olecranon tip after retrograde reaming.

**Closed method** (without opening the fracture) is applicable in acute diaphyseal fractures with precisely reducible fragments and in non-union after osteosynthesis with a thin nail or Kirschner wires — a ball-tipped guide is passed along the canal, the fragments are aligned under fluoroscopy, and only then is reaming and definitive nailing performed.

**Radial nailing.** Performed relatively rarely because of the natural radial bow, which makes a straight canal path impossible; when used, entry into the distal fragment is via a small dorsoradial incision over the wrist, a cortical window is opened, and the guide is passed retrograde across the fracture into the proximal fragment. The modern equivalent is a locked intramedullary radial nail with a bowed shape that permits intramedullary fixation without sacrificing the bow.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Radioulnar Superposition for Bone Defects</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In an extensive segmental defect of the radius with preserved distal fragment (typically after gunshot trauma or tumour resection), the classical Bulgarian technique is radioulnar superposition or "centring" osteosynthesis.

**Radial defect.** The distal fragment is mobilised and aimed at the proximal end of the ulna, which is resected to the required level to open the medullary canal; the ulna is inserted into the canal of the distal radius (intramedullary nailing with a Koncherov nail after reaming), and the forearm is set in mid-rotation. In children superposition can be performed without osteosynthesis — by impaction of the fragments alone or with thin Kirschner wires, in order not to injure the growing cartilage of the distal radius.

**Extensive ulnar defect.** The proximal end of the radius is mobilised subperiosteally, resected to the required level, and the radius is laid along the ulnar axis as a single central segment — a position that stabilises the elbow and offers at least minimal prosthetic utility, although both pronation and supination are lost. This "one-bone forearm" construct is a last-resort technique described in the Bulgarian tradition by Boychev. The modern equivalent is a one-bone forearm with microvascular fibular transfer in tumour resections.

**Congenital absence of the ulna** (with proximal radial head dislocation and progressive growth retardation) — two-stage treatment in childhood: stage one — Ilizarov distraction of the radius against the ulna (two rings + distraction frames); stage two — Cadenat posterior approach to the proximal third, freshening of the distal end of the proximal ulnar fragment, radial head resection, alignment of the radius along the ulnar axis, and osteosynthesis with a Kirschner wire. Cast immobilisation for 45–60 days; at a later stage, in a sufficiently preserved ulna, synostosis between the two bones can be created by freshening the lateral surfaces and fixation with a compression screw.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Distal Radius Malunion Correction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In neglected, unreduced, or malunited Colles fractures with the typical dorsoradial deformity, residual shortening, and altered radioulnar angle, operative correction is indicated in young or middle-aged patients with significant functional disturbance. Correction takes into account three parameters: dorsal displacement of the distal fragment, change in the radioulnar angle, and the relative length of the two forearm bones.

**Osteoplastic correction (Holevich).** Through a 6–7 cm gently curved incision on the lateral aspect of the forearm beginning at the level of the radial styloid, dissection enters between brachioradialis and abductor pollicis longus to the bone; the sensory branch of the radial nerve is identified and retracted volarly. An oscillating saw performs the osteotomy at the level of the deformity. The fragments are aligned; from the lateral side a wedge-shaped bone defect is cut and filled with autologous spongious graft from the iliac crest, providing simultaneous correction and axial stability.

**L-shaped nail-plate (Yankov-Popova).** The same approach is used; the incision is extended proximally to the middle third of the forearm. After mobilisation of the fragments and correction of the deformity, osteosynthesis is performed with an L-shaped (Γ-shaped) compression plate with a special spear-like transverse arm driven into the distal fragment parallel to the articular surface through a pre-shaped guide bed. The proximal arm of the plate is fixed to the proximal fragment as a self-compression plate. When the two bones are length-mismatched, the ulnar head is resected through a small additional incision (Darrach).

**Acute intra-articular fractures.** When the dorsal surface is involved (dorsal die-punch fragment), Ellis suggested a small plate buttressing the distal fragment as a shovel without fixation through it — the buttress plate. In unstable fractures after closed reduction with impaction-related defect or marked oedema, percutaneous fixation with Kirschner wires or an external fixator is an alternative.

**Acute Monteggia.** Anatomical plate osteosynthesis of the ulna via the direct ulnar approach; in adults — a compression plate with at least six cortical screw purchases on each side. **Chronic Monteggia — Boychev operation.** A pedagogically classical technique. The first oblique incision begins at the lateral epicondyle of the humerus and reaches the dorsal border of the ulna about three fingers below the olecranon tip; the deep fascia is divided, the dissection passes between extensor carpi ulnaris (retracted radially) and anconeus (retracted ulnarly), and the dislocated radial head is exposed. Through a second dorsal incision the dissection reaches the ulnar fracture site and the proximal radius; obstacles to reduction are removed (including interposed scar tissue and any heterotopic ossification around the annular ligament). After repositioning of the radial head into the radial notch of the ulna, annular ligament reconstruction is performed: a pedicled fascial graft 10–12 cm long and 1–1.5 cm wide is harvested from the forearm fascia, left proximally attached to the ulna, is wrapped around the radial neck, and the free end is sutured back to itself with moderate tension that does not permit redislocation. Alternatively a free graft of fascia lata or preserved tendon is used.

The classical correction in the Bulgarian tradition combines resection of the distal ulna (to decompress the DRUJ and restore rotation) and a wedge osteotomy of the distal radius fixed with Kirschner wires. The distal fragment is mobilised by division of the DRUJ capsule until it hangs from the ulnar collateral ligament; the styloid is divided so as to remain attached to the lateral ligamentous complex, and the capsule with the periosteum and periosteotendinous attachments is reconstructed over the distal end of the remaining ulna for stabilisation.

**Vickers physiolysis.** An alternative in a young patient with an active growth plate — resection of the abnormal physeal "bar" combined with an ulnar shortening osteotomy to equalise the lengths. The Vickers ligament (an abnormal ligament from the volar-ulnar pole of the distal radius to the lunate) is excised to decompress the medial physis.

<details>
<summary>Radial Club Hand Correction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In small children, treatment is performed in two or even three stages with the aim of avoiding additional injury to the remaining growth potential (Max Lange). The first stage is performed up to age 2–3 and consists of progressive soft-tissue mobilisation on the radial side (including an Ilizarov distractor when needed) and centring of the hand over the distal end of the ulna. The cartilaginous surface of the distal ulna is isolated suprapeiosteally (ensuring that the physis is not compromised), a Kirschner wire is passed through the cartilage and out through the dorsal cortex of the proximal third of the ulna; the wrist is tensioned and set in moderate dorsiflexion with slight ulnar deviation, perfusion and innervation are checked, and the wire is withdrawn until its end is buried in the cartilage. The soft tissues are bluntly separated.

When two-stage treatment is insufficient because of a congenital ulnar bow, a corrective ulnar osteotomy is added after wrist stabilisation. In combined pathology with proximal radial head dislocation (residual radius), this is corrected as well. The long-term adult task is reconstruction of a functional opposing thumb (pollicisation in absent first ray — discussed in the hand topic).

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Congenital Radioulnar Synostosis Correction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Congenital proximal radioulnar synostosis produces a fixed pronation of the forearm with severe functional limitation. The classical operative correction (Boychev) is performed in childhood before skeletal maturity: through the anterolateral Henry approach an osteotomy of the radius is performed at the required level. If forearm rotation remains restricted despite the osteotomy, release of pronator teres and pronator quadratus is added, and if needed — longitudinal division of the interosseous membrane and resection of the ulnar head (when the deformity also involves the radial bow). Operative correction has a high recurrence rate due to repeated synostosis; therefore in modern practice severe cases are managed by derotation osteotomy of the ulna in neutral or slightly supinated position (10–20° supination) without an attempt to restore rotation — acceptance of a fixed functional position rather than restoration of motion.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>DRUJ Ligament Reconstruction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

DRUJ instability due to disruption of the ligamentous apparatus (typically after a subacute Galeazzi injury or after triangular fibrocartilage resection) is corrected operatively relatively early, before degenerative changes appear. A T-shaped incision is made with a longitudinal arm on the ulnar side of the forearm (beginning several centimetres above the wrist) and a transverse arm on the dorsal surface. A palmaris longus autograft or preserved tendon allograft is taken; the ulna is denuded subperiosteally proximal to its head. The graft is looped around the ulnar neck and its ends are anchored to the radius with a pull-out suture or anchor. The reconstruction recreates both components of stability — the palmar and dorsal radioulnar ligament systems. Immobilisation in supination (for dorsal instability) or neutral (for bidirectional instability) in a long-arm cast for 6 weeks, followed by 4 weeks in a short-arm cast. This technique is the conceptual predecessor of modern anatomical reconstructions after Adams-Berger (double tenodesis of the dorsal and palmar radioulnar ligaments with a graft through a radial bone tunnel).

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Darrach Distal Ulna Resection</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Distal ulna resection (W. Darrach, 1912) is one of the most enduring procedures in the forearm surgical repertoire. Indications: painful ulnar impaction syndrome with advanced DRUJ arthritis, rigid DRUJ instability after complex trauma, inoperable adult Madelung deformity, correction of radioulnar dysmetry after a malunited Colles.

**Technique.** A dorsal incision over the distal end of the ulna, dissection between the tendons of extensor carpi ulnaris and extensor digiti minimi, opening of the DRUJ, transverse or slightly oblique division of the ulna 1.5–2 cm proximal to the ulnar styloid. The distal fragment is grasped with a bone-holder and mobilised by division of the DRUJ capsule; the styloid is divided carefully and left attached to the ulnar collateral ligament. The capsule with the periosteum is sutured over the distal end of the remaining ulna for stabilisation and to prevent painful radioulnar convergence under load.

**Complications and alternatives.** Radioulnar convergence with collapse of the distal ulna toward the radius under load, instability of the remnant with "painful clunking" on rotation — the principal motives for development of alternatives: hemiresection-interposition arthroplasty (Bowers), preserving the ulnar styloid as triangular fibrocartilage anchor with interposition of a capsular flap; Sauvé-Kapandji (DRUJ arthrodesis with proximal ulnar pseudarthrosis); DRUJ arthroplasty. The choice depends on grip-strength demands, expectations for heavy work, and patient age.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Wrist Denervation (Wilhelm)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Wrist denervation is an elegant analgesic procedure for painful degenerative arthrosis of various aetiologies (scaphoid non-union, Kienböck's disease, post-traumatic arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis) when relief of pain is sought without sacrificing motion. Wilhelm's anatomical study established that the wrist is innervated by twigs from the median, ulnar, and radial (superficial branch) nerves, as well as by the articular branches of the anterior and posterior interosseous nerves. The classical technique requires selective division of the articular twigs — principally the distal branches of the two interosseous nerves (the posterior nerve distal to its exit from supinator for dorsal denervation, the anterior nerve distal to pronator quadratus for volar denervation) — through two small incisions. Outcomes are favourable when selection is appropriate: more than 70% of patients report significant relief without noticeable loss of proprioception or muscular function.

</div>
</section>

<details>
<summary>Wrist Arthrodesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

A salvage operative method for severe degenerative arthritis with failed nonoperative therapy or after failed motion-preserving procedures. The optimal functional position is 15–20° of dorsiflexion with slight ulnar deviation — a position that on a standard radiograph aligns the second metacarpal with the radius. The operation is performed after age 16, when the distal radial physis is closed.

**Bulgarian Tsaneva technique.** Compression arthrodesis through a dorsal 5-shaped incision with ulnar retraction of the common finger extensor and radial retraction of the wrist extensors with the long thumb extensor. After opening the radiocarpal joint (sometimes together with the intercarpal joint), the articular cartilage is removed and compression is provided by a screw from the iliac crest into the distal radius. Whether the intercarpal joint must always be arthrodesed is debated — for isolated radiocarpal pathology, preservation of midcarpal motion is theoretically advantageous.

**Adams technique.** A 5-shaped incision between the second and third dorsal compartments; the common finger extensor is retracted ulnarly, the wrist extensors radially (if needed temporarily detached for better exposure). The radiocarpal joint is opened; a corticocancellous graft of appropriate length and about 1.5 cm width is taken from the iliac crest. The graft is set in a bone trough engaging the distal radius, the carpus, and the bases of the second and third metacarpals, shaped with an oscillating saw. Immobilisation in a padded cast for 2 weeks, the sutures are removed, the cast is changed, and immobilisation is maintained until union (mean 3 months). The DRUJ is not affected in this operation — forearm rotation is preserved.

**Alternative techniques.** A 5-shaped incision between the third and fourth compartments with a 180°-reversed graft from the dorsal surface of the distal radius (own-bone graft avoids iliac crest donor morbidity); compression arthrodesis with a Mannerfeldt rod for rheumatoid disease with Sauvé-Kapandji ulnar resection.

</section>

For severe traumatic injury, tumour, or gangrene, forearm amputation should be performed as distally as possible to preserve functional potential of the stump and the option of future cineplasty. Very short stumps (under 6 cm from the antecubital crease) are particularly unfavourable — prosthetic fitting is technically problematic and cineplasty is impossible.

**Classical amputation technique.** Volar and dorsal flaps of approximately equal size (larger in proximal amputations because the soft-tissue mass is greater). The radial, ulnar, and common interosseous arteries are ligated; the three nerves (median, ulnar, radial) are identified and sharply divided, then released to retract into the soft tissue — preventing a painful subcutaneous neuroma. The flexor and extensor tendons are distributed roughly equally between the two bones.

**Krukenberg cineplasty (1917, F. Krukenberg).** One of the most elegant functional reconstructions of an amputated forearm: the stump is split into two branches (radial and ulnar), each with autonomous muscular control, forming a functional pincer without a prosthesis. The original indication was a blind amputee (bilateral amputation in a patient with impaired vision), in whom the tactile feedback from the patient's own branches provides proprioceptive function inaccessible to a prosthesis. The available muscles — pronator teres, brachialis, flexor carpi radialis, supinator (radial side), and flexor carpi ulnaris, flexor digitorum (ulnar side) — are distributed between the two branches. The radial branch is covered by the volar flap; the defect on the volar side of the ulnar branch is covered by a full-thickness skin graft.

**Albrecht modification.** Indicated for amputation around the middle of the forearm or even more proximal; allows fashioning of relatively thin, mobile branches. For long forearm stumps (under 6 cm from the elbow), the Albrecht technique is not optimal because of circulatory difficulties.

**Holevich modification.** A volar incision starting 6–8 cm below the antecubital crease; proximally the incision runs on the radial side, courses about 2.5–3.5 cm proximally and ulnarly at 45° to the forearm axis, then continues 5–7 cm at 90° to the first in a distal and ulnar direction. In depth the dissection passes between the flexor digitorum superficialis tendons, the median nerve is exposed, the superficial flexor is split into two halves so that the tendons for the ring and little fingers go to the ulnar branch; the median nerve is divided at the level of pronator teres. Flexor digitorum profundus and flexor pollicis longus are distributed between the two branches; the radial and ulnar nerves are pulled and shortened only anatomically; one branch is covered by the volar flap, the other with a free graft. This modification provides better contouring and control of the two branches.

<details>
<summary>Distal Radius Approaches (Volar and Dorsal)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Standard volar approach.** A modified Henry with a longitudinal incision over the flexor carpi radialis tendon. The flexor carpi radialis sheath is opened, the tendon is retracted ulnarly together with the median nerve and the contents of the tendon sheath, and pronator quadratus is released from the radius. The distal radius is then exposed for plate placement. The watershed line (the transverse ridge marking the volar prominence of the distal radius) is identified and the plate is positioned on it or proximal to it to avoid flexor tendon irritation.

**Dorsal approach to the distal radius.** Used for selected indications (dorsal Barton fracture, dorsal die-punch fragments, specific dorsal plating). The interval is between the third extensor compartment (extensor pollicis longus) and the fourth (extensor digitorum), with extensor pollicis longus rerouted dorsally outside its sheath at closure to avoid attritional rupture over the plate. Detailed considerations of distal radius fixation are presented in the distal radius topic.

<details>
<summary>Postoperative Care</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

After plate osteosynthesis of both bones — a functional bandage or light wrist immobilisation in neutral for 7–10 days (oedema control), followed by early functional mobilisation of elbow, wrist, and fingers. Full forearm loading after 12 weeks with radiographic confirmation of union.

After distal radius volar locking plate osteosynthesis — early functional mobilisation of the fingers from postoperative day one; a functional bandage for 4–6 weeks with stable fixation. A cast is not used after locking-plate osteosynthesis with eight cortical screw purchases.

After tendon transfer — immobilisation in the position relieving tension on the transfer (see the specific operation) for 4 weeks with a well-sutured transfer; progressive increase of active and passive range of motion under hand therapy supervision. Full loading and pinch with full strength after 12 weeks.

After wrist arthrodesis — a padded cast for 2 weeks with suture removal, followed by a full cast for 10 weeks. Follow-up radiographs at 6, 12, and 16 weeks to confirm consolidation.

## Outcomes and Complications
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">


**Henry approach.** Posterior interosseous nerve injury (principal concern; minimised by forearm supination and careful subperiosteal release of supinator); superficial radial nerve injury with painful neuroma; radial artery injury (rare but possible with aggressive retraction); median nerve injury at proximal extension.

**Thompson approach.** Posterior interosseous nerve injury (principal concern; particular risk during supinator dissection); superficial radial nerve injury; elbow stiffness after extensive exposure.

**Direct ulnar approach.** Dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve injury with dysesthesia over the dorsoulnar wrist.

**Posterior Cadenat approach.** Radioulnar synostosis — the principal complication driving the abandonment of a single posterior incision for both-bone fractures.

**Compression plating.** Non-union (5–10% in isolated ulnar plate osteosynthesis); refracture after plate removal (risk during the first 2 years); infection (1–3%); posterior interosseous nerve palsy via dorsal approach; synostosis.

**Intramedullary nailing.** Cortical splitting from mismatch in diameter; false path; tendon irritation at the olecranon; reduced rotational stability compared to plate osteosynthesis.

**Volkmann reconstruction (Page-Scaglietti).** Residual flexor weakness; recurrent contracture from insufficient slide; injury to the median or ulnar nerve during dissection.

**Tendon transfers.** Adhesions and limited tendon excursion; insufficient strength of the transferred muscle; non-uniform distribution of force across adjacent fingers; failure of synthesis with low biomechanical strength of the donor. Outcomes in experienced hands — restoration of functional grip in 70–90% of cases.

**Krukenberg cineplasty.** Skin necrosis with inadequate graft coverage (especially in the Albrecht modification and in proximal amputations); insufficient pincer strength when the muscles are damaged; psychosocial considerations — in the modern era of electronic prostheses the indications narrow to a blind amputee and bilateral amputation in developing countries.

**Wrist arthrodesis.** Non-union (5%); suboptimal fusion position with functional limitations; pain in adjacent joints (DRUJ, carpal joints); infection (1–2%).

**Darrach distal ulna resection.** Radioulnar convergence under load; painful clunking on rotation; reduced grip on heavy loading — driving the development of alternatives (Bowers, Sauvé-Kapandji).

## Local Contributions
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">


**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the orthopaedic school in Bulgaria; the eponymous USBALO "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains the curriculum reference and contains descriptions of the classical forearm approaches, of the one-bone construct for severe segmental defects of both bones, of operative correction of congenital radioulnar synostosis, of the two-stage treatment of congenital absence of the ulna with the Ilizarov frame, and of the two-stage operation for chronic Monteggia with annular ligament reconstruction by a pedicled fascial graft.

**Pirogov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** The classical deep volar osteofascial cavity in the distal third of the forearm (*spatium antebrachii profundum*), bounded by the interosseous membrane and pronator quadratus posteriorly, flexor digitorum profundus and flexor pollicis longus anteriorly. Anatomical substrate for the spread of palmar phlegmons and horseshoe tenosynovitis from hand to forearm; reproduced and taught in the Bulgarian curriculum of topographical anatomy and operative surgery.

**Epstein-Rozov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Side-to-side transfer of flexor digitorum superficialis to flexor digitorum profundus in established Volkmann's contracture with deep-flexor infarction — a modification of the Seddon excisional procedure providing grasping function from the preserved superficial flexors. Embedded in the Bulgarian school as a single-stage tactic for moderate Tsuge cases.

**Karagyozov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian modification of flexor slide for established Volkmann's contracture with emphasis on simultaneous decompression of the median and ulnar nerves and single-stage restoration of pronation via pronator teres rerouting. Cited in the Bulgarian orthopaedic literature as a compromise tactic for moderate Tsuge forms.

**Stoycheva <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Modification of opponensplasty in low median palsy in childhood: flexor carpi radialis divided at the musculotendinous junction and reversed 180°, passed subcutaneously across the palm to the opponens insertion. Particularly applicable in post-poliomyelitic palsy and in children where the superficial flexors are not the optimal donor.

**Ivan Matev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Distinguished Bulgarian upper-extremity surgeon. Classical transfers in his name: opponensplasty with double anchoring to extensor pollicis brevis and adductor pollicis for simultaneous stabilisation of the proximal phalanx and palmar arch; restoration of thumb proximal-phalangeal flexion in ulnar palsy via ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis re-routed through the carpal tunnel along the path of flexor pollicis longus and anchored through a transverse bone tunnel of the proximal phalanx.

**Paneva <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Two-graft opponensplasty with palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis lengthened by preserved allografts and anchored to opponens and abductor pollicis brevis. Defined the modern principles of compression plate osteosynthesis in the Bulgarian tradition with emphasis on the self-compression plate and the sequence of compression-screw tightening.

**Holevich <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Osteoplastic correction of malunited Colles with wedge osteotomy and an iliac-crest cancellous graft; modification of Krukenberg cineplasty with a discreet volar incision and optimal distribution of the superficial flexors between the two branches; contribution to the operative school of post-war Bulgarian hand surgery.

**Yankov-Popova <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** L-shaped (Γ-shaped) compression nail-plate for distal-radius malunion correction with a transverse spear-like arm driven into the distal fragment parallel to the articular surface through a pre-shaped guide bed. A compression-based technical solution for complex adult deformity.

**Tsaneva <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Compression arthrodesis of the wrist with a screw from the iliac crest to the distal radius via a dorsal 5-shaped incision — a Bulgarian classical technique described before the dorsal locking plate era of wrist fusion.

**Konforti <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> and Paneva <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Median nerve neurolysis in pronator teres syndrome through a low approach via the fascial tunnel, sparing the motor branches to the deep flexors. A classical description in the Bulgarian hand surgery literature.

**Koncherov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian intramedullary nailing system for diaphyseal ulnar fractures with reaming; describes open and closed (fluoroscopy-guided) methods and a standardised technique for avoiding the typical errors (false path, cortical splitting, tendon irritation at the olecranon).

**Krukenberg <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> (1917).** Cineplasty of the amputated forearm stump by splitting it into two functional branches (radial and ulnar) for pincer function with intrinsic tactile feedback — particularly in a blind amputee. In the Bulgarian tradition the technique remains widely recognised owing to the Holevich modification with a discreet volar incision and optimal distribution of flexor digitorum superficialis between the two branches.

**Bunnell <sup class="natflag">US</sup>.** *Surgery of the Hand* (1944) — opponensplasty with ring-finger flexor digitorum superficialis; transfers for ulnar palsy (Bunnell-Stiles); the conceptual rerouting of pronator teres as a supinator in pronator contracture. Included in the Bulgarian hand surgery curriculum as a reference standard for tendon transfers.

**Chaklar (Jaschar) <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Classical principles for tendon transfers in high radial palsy, formulated in the Bulgarian literature on the basis of the Russian descriptions of Perthes, Dzhanelidze, Tonchev, and Tikhonovich. Preservation of at least one wrist flexor, synergistic wrist extension with finger flexion, a common motor for extensor digitorum and extensor pollicis longus, and separate motors for extensor pollicis brevis and abductor pollicis longus.
