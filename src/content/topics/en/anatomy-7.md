## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The pelvis presents the orthopaedic surgeon with one of the most complex regional anatomical environments: the bony pelvic ring — the load-transmitting structure between the spine and the lower extremities — is interwoven with the pelvic viscera, the iliac vessels and their branches, the lumbosacral plexus, and the abdominal wall musculature. Surgical approaches must navigate this complexity while providing access to specific bony regions for fracture fixation, tumour resection, arthroplasty, and periacetabular correction. The principal approaches — the ilioinguinal approach of Letournel, the anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa), the posterior Kocher-Langenbeck, the extended iliofemoral, and the anterior Pfannenstiel approach — each provide access to specific regions with characteristic risks and benefits. This chapter presents the bony and soft-tissue anatomy, the principal approaches with their indications, dangers, and modern instrumented constructs, and the classical Bulgarian and Soviet operative school (Zatsepin, Berglezov, Selig-Stoffel, Kocher, Roux, B. Boychev) that remains part of the orthopaedic curriculum in Bulgaria.

</div>
</details>
</section>

The pelvic ring is formed by the two innominate bones (each the product of fusion of the ilium, ischium, and pubis at the triradiate cartilage around age 16) and the sacrum, joined anteriorly at the pubic symphysis (a secondary cartilaginous joint) and posteriorly at the sacroiliac (SI) joints. Stability of the ring depends 70–80% on the posterior pelvic ligaments — the anterior SI ligament, the short posterior SI ligaments, the long posterior SI ligament, the iliolumbar ligament, and the sacrotuberous–sacrospinous complex (Tile, 2003 — the biomechanical basis of the A/B/C stability classification).

**Bony landmarks for the operative approaches.** Iliac crest — the superior margin of the ilium, palpable from the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) anteriorly to the posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS) posteriorly; the classical bone graft donor site. ASIS — the anterior projection at the front end of the crest; origin of sartorius and lateral attachment of the inguinal ligament. Anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS) — just below the ASIS; origin of the direct head of rectus femoris. PSIS — the posterior projection at the back end of the crest; usually marked by a skin dimple (surgical landmark for posterior approaches). Posterior inferior iliac spine (PIIS) — just below the PSIS. Greater sciatic notch — the large posterior incisure between the PIIS and the ischial spine, transmitting the sciatic nerve, the superior gluteal vessels and nerve (above piriformis), and the inferior gluteal vessels and nerve plus the pudendal nerve (below piriformis). Lesser sciatic notch — between the ischial spine and the ischial tuberosity, transmitting obturator internus, the pudendal nerve (re-entering from Alcock's canal), and the perineal vessels. Ischial spine — the bony projection at the junction of the body and ramus of the ischium; landmark for the pubic-rectal line and the pudendal nerve. Ischial tuberosity — the "sit bone", the weight-bearing portion of the ischium; origin of the hamstrings. Pubic tubercle — the projection on the superior pubic ramus, lateral to the symphysis; medial attachment of the inguinal ligament. Iliopectineal eminence — the prominence at the junction of the ilium and superior pubic ramus, marking the iliopectineal line on the pelvic brim.

**Linea terminalis (pelvic brim).** The continuous line from the sacral promontory along the arcuate line, the pectineal line, the pubic crest, to the pubic symphysis, separating the true pelvis (lesser pelvis) below from the false pelvis (greater pelvis) above. The key surgical landmark in the ilioinguinal and modified Stoppa approaches.

**Quadrilateral surface.** The flat plate of bone forming the medial acetabular wall, facing into the true pelvis. Visualised through the anterior intrapelvic approach — Stoppa provides the best exposure of medial protrusion of the femoral head (Letournel-Judet, 1993).

**Column architecture of the acetabulum (Letournel-Judet).** The classical two-column concept — anterior and posterior — rests on the observation that the acetabulum lies at the base of an inverted "Y": an anterior column running from the iliac crest through the iliopectineal eminence to the upper symphysis, and a posterior column running from the greater sciatic notch through the posterior acetabular wall and the ischial tuberosity to the inferior ischial ramus. The anterior column is longer and shorter in cross-section; the posterior column is shorter and stouter. Since the two columns meet at an angle of about 60°, a single approach rarely exposes both fully — the basis for choosing a single (Kocher-Langenbeck for posterior, ilioinguinal/Stoppa for anterior) or a staged combined approach in both-column fractures.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The posterior pelvic ligaments (sacrotuberous, sacrospinous, iliolumbar, SI complex) carry 70–80% of ring stability — their disruption (Tile C) requires posterior reconstruction.</li>
    <li>Letournel's column concept — two pillars meeting at about 60° — determines whether a single or combined approach will achieve reduction in a given fracture pattern.</li>
    <li>The quadrilateral surface is accessible only through the modified Stoppa (or the third window of the ilioinguinal) — medial protrusion of the femoral head is therefore Stoppa territory.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Topography — Greater and Lesser Pelvis
<details id="topography">
<summary>Topography — Greater and Lesser Pelvis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian topographic-anatomical concept, as set out in B. Boychev's *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology*, divides the pelvis into the greater pelvis (pelvis major) and the lesser pelvis (pelvis minor), separated by the linea terminalis. The greater pelvis is bounded by the iliac wings and the last lumbar vertebra; the lesser pelvis is bounded by the bodies of the pubis, ilium, and ischium, the sacrum, and the coccyx. The soft tissues covering the pelvis externally belong to the gluteal and femoral regions. The external muscles of the pelvic girdle (glutei, tensor fasciae latae) insert on the proximal femur and drive gait, while the internal muscles — iliopsoas (in the greater pelvis), piriformis, and obturator internus (in the lesser pelvis) — pass through pelvic foramina and also insert on the proximal femur.

Between the posterior surface of iliopsoas and the hip capsule lies the iliopectineal bursa, which communicates with the joint cavity in about 10–15% of cases — anatomically important in infection and in joint aspiration. Piriformis has a triangular form within the pelvis and on emerging through the greater sciatic foramen forms two classical openings — foramen suprapiriforme (above) and foramen infrapiriforme (below) — transmitting the superior gluteal nerve with the superior gluteal vessels, and the complex of sciatic, inferior gluteal nerve with inferior gluteal vessels, pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels, respectively. Obturator internus exits the lesser pelvis through the lesser sciatic foramen, bending at a right angle, accompanied by the two gemelli.

The pelvic vessels and nerves lie in the subperitoneal fat between the peritoneum and the pelvic fascia. The internal iliac artery arises from the common iliac at the level of the SI joint and runs down, outward, and backward along the posterolateral wall of the lesser pelvis, medial to iliopsoas. Its stem is relatively short (3–4 cm), creating a technical challenge for vascular control in depth. The transversalis fascia, on entering the lesser pelvis, covers the internal muscles and neurovascular structures and is termed fascia endopelvina; it also covers the pelvic floor. The space between fascia endopelvina and the peritoneum — filled with loose or fatty connective tissue — is most prominent lateral to the visceral organs and defines the plane of blunt dissection for the anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa) approach (spatium Retzii — the prevesical space; spatium retroinguinale of Bogros — its lateral continuation).

</div>
</details>


### Muscular Anatomy
<details id="muscular">
<summary>Muscular Anatomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Abdominal wall muscles.** External oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis, and rectus abdominis. Encountered in the anterior pelvic approaches (ilioinguinal, Pfannenstiel). The lower edges of the three flat abdominal muscles form the conjoined tendon (falx inguinalis) inserting on the pubic crest — the weak point through which the indirect inguinal hernia passes; meticulous repair of the inguinal canal at closure of the ilioinguinal approach is mandatory.

**Iliacus and psoas (iliopsoas).** Iliacus arises from the iliac fossa and psoas from the lumbar vertebrae; they join in a common tendon inserting on the lesser trochanter. Iliacus is innervated by the femoral nerve (L2–L4), which lies between the two heads of the muscle in its lower third. Encountered in approaches entering the iliac fossa from above. Between iliopsoas and the hip capsule lies the iliopectineal bursa — an anatomical plane for blunt dissection.

**Gluteus maximus.** The principal posterior pelvic muscle, providing hip extension. Innervated by the inferior gluteal nerve (L5–S2), which enters from the medial side. Encountered in the Kocher-Langenbeck and extended iliofemoral approaches. When split along its fibres the line is biased laterally to preserve innervation.

**Gluteus medius and minimus.** The principal hip abductors, originating from the outer ilium. Innervated by the superior gluteal nerve (L4–S1), exiting through the foramen suprapiriforme. Preservation is critical to gait — superior gluteal nerve injury produces a Trendelenburg gait.

**Piriformis.** From the anterior sacrum, through the greater sciatic notch, to the greater trochanter. The principal landmark for identifying the sciatic nerve in posterior pelvic surgery — the nerve usually exits just below piriformis (88% of the population; Beaton & Anson, 1937).

**Obturator internus.** From the inner pelvic surface around the lesser sciatic notch to the greater trochanter.

**Sartorius.** From the ASIS to the medial tibia. Encountered in the ilioinguinal approach.

**Rectus femoris.** From the AIIS and the supra-acetabular ilium to the patella through the quadriceps tendon. Encountered in the anterior hip and pelvis approaches.

</div>
</details>


### Iliac Vessels and Corona Mortis
<details id="iliac-vessels">
<summary>Iliac Vessels and Corona Mortis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The common iliac arteries branch from the abdominal aorta at approximately the level of L4 and divide into the internal iliac (hypogastric) and external iliac arteries at approximately the SI joint. The external iliac continues across the pelvic brim and becomes the femoral artery beneath the inguinal ligament; it gives off the inferior epigastric artery just above the inguinal ligament. The internal iliac has numerous branches supplying the pelvic viscera, the gluteal region (superior and inferior gluteal arteries), the obturator region (obturator artery), and the perineum (internal pudendal artery).

The iliac veins parallel the arteries. The common iliac veins join to form the inferior vena cava. The left common iliac vein is particularly vulnerable in anterior approaches because of its medial position relative to the artery — in several series it is the most commonly injured venous structure in the ilioinguinal and Stoppa approaches (Mast, *Pelvic Reconstruction*, 1989).

**Corona mortis ("crown of death").** The vascular anastomosis between the obturator artery (a branch of the internal iliac) and the external iliac / inferior epigastric system, crossing the superior pubic ramus. Present in 30–80% of individuals (median 40–50% in recent CT-angiography series), arterial variant 10–30%, venous 70–80%, with both vessels in about 20%. At substantial risk during the ilioinguinal (third window) and the anterior intrapelvic Stoppa approach; injury causes significant bleeding that is difficult to control because the vessel retracts into the obturator foramen and is anterior to the obturator artery, which is at the pelvic roof on retraction.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Corona mortis crosses the superior pubic ramus in 30–80% — arterial variant 10–30%, venous variant 70–80%; in either case identify and ligate before retracting the iliac vessels in the modified Stoppa or the ilioinguinal third window. The stump retracts into the obturator foramen and haemostasis becomes technically difficult.</li>
    <li>The left common iliac vein is the most vulnerable venous structure in the anterior pelvic approaches because of its medial position relative to the artery — particularly at L4–L5 and in the modified Stoppa.</li>
    <li>The internal iliac stem is short (3–4 cm) — massive bleeding from the pelvic depth requires proactive access to the common iliac for rapid proximal control rather than "chasing" the bleeder in depth.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>

The lumbosacral plexus is formed from the anterior rami of L1 through S4.

**Femoral nerve (L2–L4).** Descends through the iliac fossa between the two heads of iliacus beneath the iliac fascia and passes beneath the inguinal ligament lateral to the femoral artery. Innervates iliopsoas, quadriceps femoris, sartorius, and pectineus. In the second window of the ilioinguinal approach it lies on the surface of iliopsoas and is retracted laterally with the muscle.

**Obturator nerve (L2–L4).** Descends along the lateral wall of the lesser pelvis on obturator internus and passes through the obturator foramen with the obturator vessels. Innervates obturator externus and the adductor compartment of the thigh. In the anterior intrapelvic approach the nerve is identified at its entry into the obturator foramen and preserved.

**Sciatic nerve (L4–S3).** The largest nerve in the body. Exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, typically inferior to piriformis (88% — Beaton-Anson type I; about 12% show anatomical variants, including a divided nerve straddling piriformis). Innervates the hamstrings and (via the peroneal and tibial divisions) all muscles of the leg and foot. The principal neurological hazard in the Kocher-Langenbeck approach — postoperative dysfunction in 10–30% of cases, predominantly the peroneal division.

**Superior gluteal nerve (L4–S1).** Exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen ABOVE piriformis (the only nerve to do so). Runs between gluteus medius and minimus, supplying both muscles and tensor fasciae latae. Iatrogenic injury in the lateral hip approach is a recognised cause of postoperative abductor weakness and Trendelenburg gait.

**Inferior gluteal nerve (L5–S2).** Exits inferior to piriformis. Supplies gluteus maximus.

**Pudendal nerve (S2–S4).** Exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis, wraps around the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament, and re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen in Alcock's canal (canalis pudendalis) along the medial aspect of obturator internus. Supplies the perineum and sphincters.

**Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (L2–L3).** Crosses anterior to iliacus and passes beneath the inguinal ligament approximately 1–2 cm medial to the ASIS. Supplies the lateral thigh. Frequently encountered (and at risk) in anterior pelvic approaches and in iliac crest graft harvesting; injury produces meralgia paresthetica.

**Superior cluneal nerves.** Dorsal rami of L1–L3 cross the iliac crest approximately 7–8 cm lateral to midline — injury in posterior iliac crest graft harvest produces chronic pain in the donor area.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve crosses under the inguinal ligament 1–2 cm medial to the ASIS — meralgia paresthetica is a common (20–60%, often transient) complication of the ilioinguinal approach and of tricortical iliac crest graft harvest.</li>
    <li>The superior gluteal nerve is the only nerve exiting ABOVE piriformis through the foramen suprapiriforme — at risk in the lateral hip approach (Trendelenburg gait).</li>
    <li>The sciatic nerve exits below piriformis in 88% (Beaton-Anson I); the 12% anatomical variants (types II–VI) include a divided nerve around or above piriformis — preoperative imaging and intraoperative monitoring in complex cases.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Lumbar plexus within psoas.** The lumbar plexus lies within the substance of psoas and is at particular risk in lateral transmuscular (transpsoas) approaches to the lumbar spine (XLIF/LLIF; see [Anatomy-1](/en/anatomy/1/)). The L4 root is the most caudal and at greatest risk at the L4–L5 level.

### Pelvic Viscera and Pelvic Floor
<details id="viscera">
<summary>Pelvic Viscera and Pelvic Floor</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The bladder (vesica urinaria) lies in a prepubic position in the lesser pelvis; empty it is entirely subperitoneal, full it bulges above the pubic symphysis into the abdominal cavity. For all pelvic approaches (Pfannenstiel, Stoppa, ilioinguinal medial window) a Foley catheter is mandatory pre-operatively for decompression and intraoperative haematuria monitoring — the indicator of iatrogenic injury.

The ureters cross the bifurcation of the common iliac vessels at the pelvic brim (linea terminalis), descend along the lateral wall of the lesser pelvis medial to the internal iliac artery, and enter the bladder about 2 cm proximal to the trigone. They are at risk in anterior and lateral pelvic approaches; the uretero-vesical junction is particularly vulnerable in the modified Stoppa with aggressive lateral retraction.

The rectum lies in the posterior midline of the lesser pelvis; its anterior wall is closely related to the presacral fascia (Denonvilliers in males). In sacrectomy and pelvic tumour resection, preoperative MRI assessment of rectal fascia involvement is critical for determining invasion and for choosing between low-radical resection and anterior exenteration.

The pelvic floor is formed by levator ani (pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, puborectalis) and coccygeus. Levator ani arises from the tendinous arch of obturator internus and from the ischial spine, forming a funnel around the rectum, urethra, and (in females) the vagina. The inferior hypogastric (pelvic) plexus lies lateral to the rectum on the pelvic visceral vessels — injury produces sexual and sphincter dysfunction. In sacrectomy for chordoma, bilateral preservation of S3 is critical for normal sphincter continence (see below).

The superior hypogastric plexus lies in front of the promontory as a delicate nerve mesh — the principal neurological cause of retrograde ejaculation (1–5% of males) in anterior lumbosacral and high Stoppa approaches; see also [Anatomy-1](/en/anatomy/1/).

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Foley catheter is mandatory before Pfannenstiel, Stoppa, and the ilioinguinal medial window — a full bladder is injured in 1–3% of Stoppa approaches.</li>
    <li>Ureter on the lateral pelvic wall medial to the internal iliac artery — preoperative stenting in large pelvic tumours and in revisions with fibrosis.</li>
    <li>The superior hypogastric plexus in front of the promontory is the cause of retrograde ejaculation (1–5% of males) in anterior lumbosacral and pelvic approaches — blunt dissection only, no cautery.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Pathophysiology and Mechanism
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="patho">
<details>
<summary>Pathophysiology and Mechanism</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Pelvic ring and acetabular fractures are the product of high-energy trauma in adults (motor-vehicle, fall from height, crush, motorcycle) and low-energy injury on osteoporosis in the elderly. The classical work of Pennal and Tile (1980) identified three principal vectors of force that determine the typical patterns of injury and the corresponding Tile A/B/C classification: anteroposterior compression (APC), lateral compression (LC), and vertical shear (VS). Young-Burgess (1990) added the combined mechanism (CMI).

**Anteroposterior compression (APC).** A force in the sagittal plane — typically a crush injury or a motorcycle frontal impact. It opens the symphysis (open book — Tile B1, APC I); with greater force it tears the sacrospinous ligament and the anterior SI complex (APC II); with maximum force it tears the posterior SI ligaments, creating a totally unstable, rotationally and translated hemipelvis (Tile C1, APC III). External rotation of the hemipelvis.

**Lateral compression (LC).** A force in the coronal plane — typical lateral motor-vehicle impact or sideways fall. It compresses the ring with internal rotation of the hemipelvis. LC I — stable compression fracture of sacrum and pubis; LC II — iliac wing fracture with "crescent" fragment in the posterior ring (Tile B2); LC III — windswept pelvis with compression on one side and external rotation on the other (Tile C2).

**Vertical shear (VS).** A sagittal-vertical force — fall from height onto a straight leg, head-on motor accident with axial loading. The hemipelvis is translated proximally (Tile C3). The high energy frequently produces concomitant visceral and vascular injuries — mortality 10–25%.

**Acetabular fractures.** Letournel and Judet (1964, 1981, 1993) showed that the vector of impact force on the femoral head determines the anatomical distribution of the fracture. Impact on the forward-flexed femur → anterior column or wall; impact on a posteriorly-directed femur (controlled by hip position — 90° flexion plus adduction gives the "dashboard" mechanism) → posterior wall or column; axial impact → transverse or T-shaped fracture; mixed vector → both-column. The classical dashboard injury in unbelted car crashes produces posterior wall fracture with posterior dislocation of the head.

**Pelvic haemorrhage.** The presacral venous plexus and the branches of the internal iliac artery (especially the superior gluteal, internal pudendal, and obturator) are the sources of massive bleeding in unstable Tile B/C fractures. Arterial bleeding (10–20%) requires selective embolisation; venous (60–80%) is controlled by external fixation or pelvic binder (the shock-room standard from the 2000s onwards). Mortality from haemodynamic instability in open pelvic fractures remains 25–40%.

</div>
</details>
</section>

## Clinical Evaluation and Imaging
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="clinical">
<details>
<summary>Clinical Evaluation and Imaging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Primary survey.** The pelvic ring is assessed within the ATLS protocol. Haemodynamic instability in a patient with a pelvic fracture demands immediate placement of a pelvic binder (or an improvised wrap at the level of the greater trochanters) and reassessment. Clinical signs of pelvic instability include limb-length asymmetry (shortening and external rotation in VS), open/closed rectal examination, periurogenital bleeding, high-riding prostate (urethral injury), scrotal/perineal haematoma ("butterfly sign"), and pain on stress compression of the iliac crests ("pelvic spring test"). The stress test is performed only once — repeated manoeuvres dislodge any nascent thrombus.

**Radiography.** Standard AP pelvis in every polytrauma patient in the shock room (Tile, Tornetta). For an acetabular fracture, Judet views are added: the iliac oblique (45° external rotation of the affected side) shows the anterior wall and posterior column; the obturator oblique (45° internal rotation) shows the posterior wall and anterior column. Letournel-Judet (1993) identified six radiographic lines: ilio-pectineal (anterior column), ilio-ischial (posterior column), the radiographic "U" (medial wall), anterior lip, posterior lip, and the radiographic "teardrop" — an indicator of the integrity of the acetabular floor.

**Computed tomography.** The standard for definitive evaluation of pelvic and acetabular fractures, with 3D reconstruction. CT shows fragment orientation and volume, posterior wall as axial volume (important for the percent-circumference of the involved wall — over 50% → hip instability), impacted articular surface, marginal impaction of the acetabular dome, SI disruption, and sacral fractures by Denis (zones 1–3). In the haemodynamically stable patient, CT angiography identifies arterial bleeding and embolisation candidates.

**MRI.** Reserved for ligamentous evaluation (posterior pelvic complex), occult sacral fractures on osteoporosis, and assessment of pelvic tumours before resection (invasion of rectal fascia, lumbosacral plexus involvement, major vascular involvement).

**Classical clinical eponyms.** Destot's sign — fluctuant haematoma above the inguinal ligament or in the scrotum; Roux's sign — asymmetric distance from symphysis to trochanter; Earle's sign — palpable displaced fragment on rectal examination (in sacral and lower-pelvic fractures).

</div>
</details>
</section>

## Classification
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classification">
<details>
<summary>Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Tile (1980, revised 1988, 2003) — pelvic ring classification.** Based on stability and mechanism; adopted by AO/OTA.

*Type A — stable.* A1 — avulsions without ring disruption; A2 — stable wing fracture (isolated iliac wing or Duverney); A3 — transverse sacral/coccygeal fracture without posterior involvement.

*Type B — partially unstable (rotational).* B1 — open book, symphyseal opening (APC); B2 — lateral compression with internal rotation of the hemipelvis; B3 — bilateral B-type.

*Type C — completely unstable (vertical plus rotational).* C1 — unilateral total instability (Malgaigne); C2 — unilateral C with contralateral B; C3 — bilateral C; includes acetabular involvement.

**Young-Burgess (1990) — mechanism-based classification.** APC I–III, LC I–III, VS, CMI. Correlates with volume of haemorrhage and type of vascular injury — the basis for shock-room decisions.

**Letournel-Judet (1964, 1981, 1993) — acetabular fracture classification.** Based on the column concept.

*Elementary (5 types):* anterior wall; anterior column; posterior wall (the commonest type, about 30%); posterior column; transverse.

*Associated (5 types):* posterior column + posterior wall; transverse + posterior wall; T-shaped; anterior column + posterior hemitransverse; both-column (the pathognomonic "spur sign" on the obturator oblique).

The classification dictates the operative approach: posterior wall/column/transverse → Kocher-Langenbeck; anterior wall/column → ilioinguinal or modified Stoppa; T-shaped and both-column → combined two-stage (or extended iliofemoral in historical series).

**Denis (1988) — sacral fracture classification.** Zone 1 — lateral (alar fractures); zone 2 — foraminal (with risk to S1); zone 3 — central (transverse sacral with risk to conus and cauda). The "U-shape" sacral fracture (Roy-Camille) is a special variant of zone 3 with bilateral vertical components and a horizontal component.

**Enneking-Dunham (1986) — innominate bone resection classification.** Type I — ilium (wing); type II — periacetabular zone; type III — pubic-pubic segment (pubic rami); type IV — sacrum. Type II is the technically most demanding case for reconstruction (see below).

</div>
</details>
</section>

## Treatment
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Nonoperative
<details id="nonoperative">
<summary>Nonoperative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Nonoperative treatment of pelvic and acetabular injury is determined by ring stability and articular surface displacement.

**Stable pelvic fractures (Tile A).** Avulsion fractures in young athletes (ASIS avulsion with sartorius, AIIS with rectus femoris, ischial tuberosity with hamstrings) — rest, protected weight-bearing on crutches for 4–6 weeks, progressive rehabilitation; surgery only for displacement over 2 cm or for elite athletes facing functional inadequacy at healing. Isolated wing fractures (Duverney) — rest, progressive loading by pain tolerance. Sacral/coccygeal transverse fractures without posterior involvement — symptomatic care (ring cushion, analgesia); most heal in 6–8 weeks.

**Pelvic binder in the shock room.** Standard intervention in unstable pelvic fracture with haemodynamic instability — strap or dedicated device at the level of the greater trochanters (not above the iliac crest), tightened to compress the ring and provide temporary stabilisation before definitive treatment or angioembolisation. Reduces pelvic volume and tamponades the venous plexus. A temporary measure — not more than 24 hours because of skin ischaemia risk.

**Skeletal traction.** Classical intervention for VS-type fracture for initial reduction of proximal displacement — distal-femoral or calcaneal traction with 8–15 kg for 24–48 hours before definitive operative stabilisation. Largely replaced by early percutaneous or open fixation in current practice.

**Acetabular fractures — unacceptable displacement.** Letournel: more than 2 mm displacement of the articular surface in the dome region is an indication for open reduction and plate fixation. Exceptions: large fragmentation of the posterior wall with more than 50% of the articular surface (instability regardless of displacement → always operate); both-column fracture with secondary congruence (the two columnar fragments remain congruent with the head — a relative indication for nonoperative care in elderly patients).

</div>
</details>

The ilioinguinal approach (Letournel, first described 1961, developed in *Fractures of the Acetabulum* 1974/1993) is the classical anterior approach to the pelvis and acetabulum, providing access to the entire anterior column of the acetabulum, the iliac wing, the pelvic brim, the quadrilateral surface (from anterior), and the pubic symphysis.

**Indications.** Acetabular fracture fixation with anterior dominance: anterior column, anterior wall, anterior column with posterior hemitransverse, both-column patterns; iliac wing and symphyseal fixation; tumour resection; selected revision pelvic procedures.

**Positioning.** Supine on a radiolucent table. Foley catheter. The legs draped to permit hip flexion as needed.

**Landmarks and incision.** Iliac crest, ASIS, inguinal ligament, and the midline of the pubic symphysis. The skin incision follows the iliac crest from about 2 cm posterior to the highest point, curves around the ASIS, and continues medially along the inguinal ligament to the midline at the symphysis.

**The three windows of Letournel.**

*Lateral (first) window* — between the ASIS and iliopsoas. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve crosses here; it is identified and protected. Iliacus is reflected medially from the inner table of the ilium, providing access to the iliac wing, the iliac fossa, and the anterior column of the acetabulum.

*Middle (second) window* — between iliopsoas with the femoral nerve (laterally) and the iliac vessels (medially). The iliopectineal fascia (separating the neuromuscular and vascular compartments) is divided along its length — the critical step in developing this window. Gives access to the pelvic brim and the upper portion of the quadrilateral surface.

*Medial (third) window* — between the iliac vessels (laterally) and the spermatic cord (or round ligament in females) and rectus abdominis (medially). Gives access to the superior pubic ramus, the symphysis, and the anteroinferior quadrilateral surface. The corona mortis is identified here — mandatory ligation before retracting the vascular bundle.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Letournel's three windows: lateral (between ASIS and iliopsoas, to iliac fossa and anterior column), middle (between iliopsoas and iliac vessels, to pelvic brim and upper quadrilateral surface), medial (between iliac vessels and spermatic cord/rectus abdominis, to superior pubic ramus and symphysis).</li>
    <li>The iliopectineal fascia divides the neuromuscular compartment (femoral nerve in iliopsoas) from the vascular compartment — opening it along its length is the technical key to developing the middle window.</li>
    <li>The third window is "corona mortis territory" — identification and ligation of the anastomosis before aggressive retraction is mandatory.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Critical structures.** Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve beneath the inguinal ligament near the ASIS; femoral nerve, artery, and vein in the femoral sheath beneath the inguinal ligament; iliopsoas containing the femoral nerve; the inguinal ligament (reconstructed at closure); the corona mortis crossing the superior pubic ramus; the inferior epigastric vessels ascending behind rectus abdominis; the spermatic cord (or round ligament) traversing the inguinal canal.

**Closure.** Meticulous repair of the inguinal canal floor and the inguinal ligament to prevent inguinal hernia.

**Complications.** Inguinal hernia (3–8%); meralgia paresthetica from injury to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (20–60%, often transient); vascular injury (corona mortis, inferior epigastric vessels, left common iliac vein); deep vein thrombosis; infection; postoperative abdominal wall instability with inadequate closure.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Anterior Intrapelvic (Modified Stoppa) Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Hirvensalo (1993) and Cole and Bolhofner (1994) described the anterior intrapelvic approach as a modification of the original Stoppa approach for incisional and recurrent inguinal hernia repair (R. Stoppa, 1973). It provides access to the pelvic brim, the quadrilateral surface, and the posterior column of the acetabulum from anterior, without the morbidity of the three-window ilioinguinal approach.

**Indications.** Acetabular fractures with medial protrusion (posterior column with medial translation); both-column fracture as part of a combined approach; fractures with dominant quadrilateral component; revision pelvic procedures with complex anatomy. Better visualisation of the quadrilateral surface than the ilioinguinal — Stoppa is therefore preferred for acetabular fractures with a medial component in current practice (Tile, 2003 III ed.).

**Positioning.** Supine on a radiolucent table with Foley catheter.

**Landmarks and incision.** A Pfannenstiel-type transverse incision approximately 2 cm above the pubic symphysis; midline vertical along the linea alba for extended exposure.

**Technique.** The skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised. Rectus abdominis is split in the midline (linea alba is the avascular plane). The transversalis fascia and peritoneum are retracted cephalad to enter the preperitoneal space (spatium Retzii). The dissection continues laterally along the pelvic brim. The corona mortis is identified and ligated when present. The iliac vessels are identified and retracted laterally. Iliopsoas with the femoral nerve is retracted laterally. The obturator nerve and vessels are identified entering the obturator foramen and preserved. The pelvic brim, the quadrilateral surface, the medial acetabular wall, and the posterior column from anterior can be visualised and reduced; plating along the brim or the posterior column is feasible.

**Complications.** Vascular injury (corona mortis, iliac vessels, especially the left common iliac vein); bladder injury (with inadequate decompression); obturator nerve injury; inguinal/femoral hernia (less common than after the ilioinguinal).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Kocher-Langenbeck (Posterior) Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Kocher-Langenbeck approach (a combination of the upper part of Kocher's approach with the lower part of Langenbeck's hip approach) is the classical posterior approach to the pelvis and acetabulum, providing access to the posterior wall, the posterior column, and the ischial region.

**Indications.** Posterior wall and posterior column acetabular fractures; transverse and T-shaped fractures when the posterior column is dominant; open reduction of posterior hip dislocations (particularly with impacted posterior wall); posterior pelvic tumour resection; posterior SI fixation.

**Positioning.** Prone or lateral decubitus with the affected side up. Lateral decubitus is generally preferred — it allows easier sciatic nerve monitoring and easier conversion to anterior or extended approaches if needed. The knee is flexed to 60° to relax the sciatic nerve.

**Landmarks and incision.** The greater trochanter, the PSIS, and the midline of gluteus maximus. The skin incision begins at the PSIS, curves down and laterally over the greater trochanter, and extends distally about 5–8 cm into the proximal thigh.

**Technique.** Skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised. The fascia lata and gluteal aponeurosis are opened in line with the skin incision. Gluteus maximus is split along its fibres; the inferior gluteal nerve enters from the medial side, so the splitting is biased laterally to preserve innervation. Beneath gluteus maximus the short external rotators are encountered (from superior to inferior: piriformis, gemellus superior, obturator internus, gemellus inferior, quadratus femoris). Piriformis is identified — the critical landmark for the sciatic nerve, which typically exits just inferior to piriformis. The nerve is identified and protected for the rest of the procedure (vessel loop around the nerve for retraction). The short external rotators are then incised at their trochanteric insertions and reflected medially; the posterior hip capsule is exposed. If required, trochanteric osteotomy or trochanteric flip osteotomy (Ganz, 1995) extends the exposure proximally to the supra-acetabular region and permits safe surgical dislocation of the hip.

  <strong>Cave</strong>

    <li>Split gluteus maximus with a lateral bias — the inferior gluteal nerve enters medially, and its preservation determines abductor function.</li>

  </ul>
</aside>

**Complications.** Sciatic nerve injury (10–30%, predominantly peroneal division); heterotopic ossification (10–90% without prophylaxis); infection (3–7%); wound complications; abductor dysfunction with extensive proximal extension or trochanteric osteotomy nonunion.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Extended Iliofemoral Approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The extended iliofemoral approach (Letournel, 1965; in Letournel-Judet 1993) provides the most comprehensive access to both columns of the acetabulum from a single incision. Substantial morbidity (heterotopic ossification 50–90%, abductor dysfunction, higher infection rate) has caused it to be largely abandoned in favour of combined two-stage approaches (Kocher-Langenbeck plus ilioinguinal) for the most complex acetabular fractures in current practice.

**Technique.** The patient is in lateral decubitus. The skin incision combines a posterior limb (along the iliac crest from the PSIS) with an anterior limb (curving down to the greater trochanter). Gluteus maximus is reflected posteriorly; gluteus medius and minimus anteriorly (with trochanteric osteotomy preserving their attachment to the bone fragment). Tensor fasciae latae is separated along the internervous plane between the superior gluteal nerve (to the two glutei) and the femoral nerve (to tensor). The approach gives access to both columns of the acetabulum from outside the pelvis.

Current indications are exceptional — extensive both-column fractures in older patients where combined approaches are technically unacceptable; revision procedures with extensive periacetabular osteotomy.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Pfannenstiel Approach to the Symphysis and Symphyseal Fixation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Pfannenstiel approach (Hermann Johannes Pfannenstiel, 1900 — originally an obstetric approach for caesarean section) provides direct access to the pubic symphysis for symphyseal disruption fixation, anterior pelvic ring stabilisation, and selected exposure of the inferior pubic region.

**Technique.** A transverse skin incision about 2 cm above the symphysis, similar to but smaller than the obstetric Pfannenstiel. Rectus abdominis is split or divided in the midline (care for the bladder — Foley catheter mandatory). The anterior aspect of the symphysis is exposed and the symphyseal plate is applied.

**Symphyseal fixation for traumatic diastasis.** Traumatic symphyseal diastasis (a component of open-book pelvic injury, AO/Tile B1; more than 2.5 cm of diastasis is a reliable sign of sacrospinous and anterior SI ligament disruption) is the indication for osteosynthesis when closed reduction cannot be obtained or maintained. For fresh diastasis the patient is placed in lateral or semi-lateral decubitus, aiding gravity-assisted reduction. For chronic diastasis with interposed scar tissue, supine with a slight pelvic tilt is preferred.

Fixation may use a double (superior and anterior) reconstruction plate, or a more rigid LCP plate with screws extending into the inferior pubic ramus. The classical technique in the absence of dedicated instrumentation uses a transpubic cerclage wire or screw circling the two pubic tubercles. Regardless of method, adjunctive plaster spica immobilisation for 6–8 weeks is recommended in patients without posterior pelvic injury; with concomitant posterior injury, additional posterior stabilisation (SI screw or plate) is needed because isolated anterior fixation is biomechanically inadequate in Tile C.

**Complications.** Bladder injury (with inadequate decompression or aggressive retraction); infection; wound complications; mechanical failure with isolated anterior fixation in a posteriorly injured ring (Tile C) — biomechanical analysis by Tile shows symphyseal plating contributes less than 30% of stability in Tile C, the rest must come from posterior fixation.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Combined Approach to Inferior Pubic Ramus, Ischium, and Body of Ilium (Zatsepin)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Zatsepin combined approach <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> is a classical Soviet concept providing wide exposure of the inferior pubic ramus, the body of the ischium, and the body of the ilium in a single operative session. It was developed for resection of tumours (benign or low-grade malignant) involving more than one anatomical zone of the innominate bone, where neither standard anterior nor standard posterior approaches alone provides adequate field. Conceptually Zatsepin combines an anterior perineal approach to the pubic bone and inferior ramus with a modified posterior Kocher-Langenbeck exposure of the body of the ilium and upper ischium.

**Stage 1 (anterior perineal).** The patient is supine in lithotomy with hips and knees flexed and abducted. The skin incision starts lateral to the base of the scrotum along the proximal edge of the superior pubic ramus, continues along the inferior ramus, and reaches the ischial tuberosity along the gluteal crease. The adductor insertions are divided; the spermatic cord is retracted medially; the corpus cavernosum is bluntly mobilised at its base. Obturator externus and internus are elevated with the obturator membrane. The inferior pubic ramus is divided with a Gigli saw at the two endpoints of the planned resection.

**Stage 2 (posterior gluteal).** The patient is turned prone. A vertical incision is made 4 cm posterior to the greater trochanter. The tendinous part of gluteus maximus is divided, while in its upper pole the muscle fibres are split longitudinally by blunt technique — the critical step that permits high reflection of gluteus maximus without compromising its blood supply from the inferior gluteal artery and its innervation by the inferior gluteal nerve. The first incision is extended from the ischial tuberosity along the gluteal crease to meet the midpoint of the second. To avoid necrosis of the inferior triangular flap, Zatsepin recommends that the outer part of the horizontal incision be made not exactly along the gluteal crease but horizontally, meeting the vertical limb at a right angle. The combined exposure gives access to the body of the ilium and the upper ischium from a posterior angle in which the sciatic nerve can be looped with a vessel loop and protected throughout the dissection.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>A two-stage procedure — anterior perineal plus posterior gluteal — for tumours involving the inferior pubic ramus, ischium, and body of the ilium together, in zones that no single approach covers.</li>
    <li>The posterior stage splits the tendinous and muscular parts of gluteus maximus by blunt dissection at the upper pole, preserving the inferior gluteal artery and nerve; without this the muscle is devitalised and persistent abductor weakness results.</li>
    <li>The horizontal limb of the posterior incision — not along the gluteal crease but horizontally at a right angle to the vertical — prevents necrosis of the inferior triangular flap.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Modified Posterior Approach to Ischium and Posterior Acetabular Wall (Berglezov)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For tumours of the ischium involving the posterior wall of the acetabulum, V. G. Berglezov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> modified the posterior stage of the Zatsepin approach to provide safe exposure of the retroacetabular bone without sacrificing gluteus maximus as a whole.

**Technique.** The patient is prone with the pelvis elevated. The skin incision begins 2–3 cm lateral to the PSIS, runs in an arc downward between the anterior border of gluteus maximus and the posterior border of tensor fasciae latae, crosses the gluteal crease, and continues as an S-shape for 7–8 cm down the posterior thigh. The tendinous part of gluteus maximus inserting on the iliotibial tract and the gluteal tuberosity is divided. The entire muscle is reflected superiorly and medially, with selective blunt division of upper-pole fibres as needed to preserve the neurovascular pedicle. Tensor fasciae latae is retracted laterally and anteriorly to reveal the sciatic nerve and the short external rotators (gemelli, piriformis, quadratus femoris, obturator externus and internus). The sciatic nerve is taken on a vessel loop for monitoring throughout the procedure.

**Berglezov's principle.** If the extent and type of tumour permits strictly subperiosteal bony dissection, the risk of injury to the inferior gluteal artery and the pudendal vessels is virtually eliminated. The approach reveals the posterior acetabular wall from outside, the body and upper ramus of the ischium, and may be extended distally for access to the sciatic nerve in its distal course.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Posterior Approach to the Sacroiliac Joint</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The posterior SI joint approach is used for arthrodesis in tuberculous sacroiliitis, for arthrodesis in chronic fracture-dislocation with persistent pain beyond 1.5 years, for biopsy and resection of posterior pelvic tumours, and for open reduction and fixation of posterior pelvic injuries when percutaneous SI screw fixation is unsuitable (anatomical dysplasia, osteoporosis, extensive swelling).

**Technique.** The skin incision runs along the posterior third of the iliac crest and curves down and back 5–7 cm below the PSIS. The subcutaneous tissue and thoracolumbar fascia are divided. The fibres of gluteus maximus are split bluntly or its origin is reflected from the iliac crest. The aponeurosis of erector spinae is divided and retracted laterally and inferiorly, exposing the posterior surface of the ilium down to the SI joint. A rectangular bone block is cut with an osteotome from the ilium immediately over the joint, directed laterally; the inferior border of the block is roughly parallel to the greater sciatic notch. The joint cleft itself is opened by removing articular cartilage from both surfaces (iliac and sacral) down to cancellous bone. For arthrodesis, the bone block is impacted into the prepared trough with its cancellous surface inward; bone chips from the iliac crest are packed around it. Immobilisation in a plaster body cast from shoulder girdle to knees for 3 months.

**Contemporary alternative.** Percutaneous or mini-open SI screw fixation under fluoroscopic/3D navigation (S1 and/or S2 trajectory) provides comparable fusion rates (90–95%) with much shorter immobilisation (6 weeks crutched ambulation) and significantly less surgical morbidity.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The superior gluteal artery emerges just above piriformis through the foramen suprapiriforme — if injured it retracts into the pelvis and produces an uncontrollable haematoma; the bone block is cut strictly lateral to the joint.</li>
    <li>Lateral cutaneous branches of S1–S3 dorsal rami — their loss produces local cutaneous dysaesthesia.</li>
    <li>Overly medial dissection threatens the superior cluneal nerves (chronic pain over the iliac crest).</li>
    <li>The bone block never crosses the anterior sacral cortex, which abuts the lumbosacral plexus and the internal iliac artery.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Intrapelvic Obturator Neurectomy (Selig-Stoffel)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Intrapelvic obturator neurectomy (the Selig-Stoffel technique <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>, described in the late 1910s and 1920s) is a classical neurectomy for releasing the hip adductors.

**Indications.** Severe adductor contractures in cerebral palsy refractory to botulinum toxin and conservative therapy; historically the procedure was used as a partial denervation for painful coxarthrosis with adductor contracture — an indication superseded by hip arthroplasty.

**Technique (intrapelvic Selig-Stoffel).** The patient is supine; the bladder is emptied by catheter pre-operatively. A transverse suprapubic incision starts 2–3 cm lateral to rectus abdominis and ends in the midline. The abdominal wall is divided and the preperitoneal space is entered — the same principle as the modified Stoppa. The peritoneum is retracted cephalad; the nerve is identified on the lateral wall of the lesser pelvis, where it passes along obturator internus toward the obturator foramen. A 2 cm segment of the nerve is resected, with clips or ligatures on both ends to discourage regrowth.

**Alternative — extrapelvic Stoffel.** Performed at thigh level: an incision along the medial thigh 2 cm below the inguinal crease, dissection between pectineus and adductor longus; the anterior and posterior branches of the obturator nerve are identified at their emergence from the obturator foramen beneath obturator externus and selectively resected. The anterior branch supplies adductor longus, brevis, and gracilis; the posterior branch supplies obturator externus and part of adductor magnus. Selective anterior-branch resection (Adams <sup class="natflag">US</sup>, 1947) preserves adductor magnus as an active hip extensor — critical in ambulators, while complete trunk resection is acceptable in non-ambulators where hygiene positioning is the only goal.

</div>
</details>

<summary>Innominate Bone Resections (Enneking I–III)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Resection of the pubis and ischium (Enneking III).** Indications — benign bony tumours (osteochondroma, osteoid osteoma, aneurysmal bone cyst) and low-grade malignant tumours of limited size (chondrosarcoma G1, low-grade osteosarcoma after neoadjuvant therapy). The patient is in lithotomy. The skin incision begins 0.5 cm below the midpoint of the inguinal ligament, runs medially parallel to it, curves down over the inferior pubic ramus, and ends at the ischial tuberosity. The adductor insertions and obturator externus are reflected subperiosteally, exposing the lower border of the obturator foramen with its membrane, part of the body and outer margin of the inferior pubic ramus, the ischial ramus, and the tuberosity. Bone cutting with osteotome or Gigli saw at the two endpoints; the ischial cut rarely exceeds the ischial spine (protecting the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels in the lesser sciatic notch); the pubis is divided from the symphysis with a resection knife.

**Resection of the ischium.** Isolated ischial resection for primary tumours (chondrosarcoma, chordoma, low-grade Ewing) of limited size. The patient is in lithotomy. A gently curved incision over the inferior pubic ramus and ischium, passing over the tuberosity and ending lateral to the sacrum. Release of the ischial ramus from its medial side is the risky step: the internal pudendal vessels and the pudendal nerve run in Alcock's canal along the medial surface of obturator internus. The hazard is mitigated by strict subperiosteal release of ischiocavernosus and transversus perinei. Subperiosteal release continues posteriorly and superiorly to the ischial spine or just above; cutting with Gigli saw or osteotome.

**Resection of the iliac wing (Enneking I).** The patient is supine with the relevant pelvic side gently elevated. The skin incision runs over the iliac crest from the PSIS, curves anteriorly toward the ASIS, and extends distally over tensor fasciae latae as needed. Iliacus is reflected from the inner surface and the glutei from the outer, both subperiosteally. Wing resection may be economical (sparing the crest as an apophyseal structure with attached muscles) or extensive (extending to the greater sciatic notch).

If the wing must be resected as far as the greater sciatic notch, the pelvic ring is disrupted — a substantial biomechanical compromise requiring posterior reconstruction in two-level resections. Particular care for the superior gluteal artery, which emerges below the upper margin of the greater sciatic notch covered by piriformis: iatrogenic avulsion retracts the vessel into the pelvis and produces a rapidly expanding haematoma that is technically demanding to control and often requires percutaneous embolisation.

  <strong>NB</strong>

    <li>Enneking-Dunham classification of innominate resection: I — ilium (wing); II — periacetabular zone; III — pubic-pubic segment; IV — sacrum.</li>
    <li>Type II is the technically demanding case — reconstruction options include saddle prosthesis (Mark), pelvic megaprosthesis (LUMiC, Stanmore), allograft-prosthetic composite, or resection without reconstruction ("flail hip").</li>
    <li>The Zatsepin approach covers combinations I+III with part of II; the Berglezov modification is optimised for II plus posterior wall.</li>

<details>
<summary>Partial Sacrectomy for Chordoma (Enneking IV)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Sacral chordoma is the most common indication for partial sacrectomy in orthopaedic practice. Other indications include giant cell tumour and low-grade chondrosarcoma in the lower sacral segments. The operation is performed by a mixed team — orthopaedic/spine surgeon, general surgeon, and (with rectal involvement) colorectal surgeon. The patient is prone with the pelvis elevated by a bolster, or in Kraske position (prone with the legs hanging off the table).

**Classical technique of B. Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** A transverse arcuate incision over the midline of the sacrum, strongly convex superiorly, spanning the distance between the two gluteus maximi. This incision has the advantage of a large distal flap sweeping down toward the anus — protecting the wound from contamination by the anal opening, important for patients with potential bacterial contamination. A midline longitudinal incision is an alternative, but Boychev preferred the transverse for its lower infection rate.

The insertions of gluteus maximus into the sacrum are divided. The coccyx is freed from soft tissue, disarticulated, and removed. The rectum is carefully separated from the presacral tumour mass — a step where preoperative MRI assessment of rectal fascia invasion is critical. The sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments are divided bilaterally, as is coccygeus. The S4 and S5 roots are identified and divided bilaterally, preserving S2 and S3 (the pudendal nerve and parasympathetic innervation of the rectum and genitourinary functions). Blunt dissection along the anterior surface of the sacrum with the tumour separates loose connective tissue down to the lower pole of both SI joints. The sacrum is divided with an osteotome between the 2nd and 3rd segments; the iliolumbar ligament and lateral sacral artery are divided. The resected bone with the tumour is removed en bloc.

**High sacrectomies.** Above S3 with sacrifice of S2 bilaterally — severe neurological loss; pelvic stability is impossible without posterior reconstruction, and bladder and rectal incontinence are almost inevitable. For more extensive sacrectomies, pelvic reconstruction with pedicle screws in L4–L5 linked to bilateral iliac screws (lumbopelvic fixation) is performed, with a vascularised fibular graft between L5 and the ilium added for very extensive resections.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Neurological inventory by level of section: bilateral S5 — clinically silent; bilateral S4 — mild anal hypoaesthesia; bilateral S3 — partial loss of filling sensation with stress incontinence risk; bilateral S2 — complete sphincter incontinence, pelvic floor paralysis, impotence; bilateral S1 — also gastrocnemius weakness and pelvic instability.</li>
    <li>Unilateral preservation of even one S3 root preserves significant sphincter function, even with total contralateral resection.</li>
    <li>Informed consent must include an anatomically specific description of the expected neurological outcome at the planned level of resection.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Coccygectomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Indications.** Persistent pain (coccydynia) after coccygeal fracture or dislocation, refractory to 6 months of conservative care; coccygeal chordoma (rare); infection (pilonidal cyst with chronic course).

**Technique.** The patient is prone with a pelvic bolster or in Kraske position. A 10 cm longitudinal incision over the coccyx, or a horseshoe (Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>) incision convex superiorly — again to protect the wound from anal contamination. The coccyx is released from soft tissue (gluteus maximus, anococcygeal ligament, levator ani medially, coccygei laterally) subperiosteally with a chisel and removed with its periosteum. Haemostasis; subcutaneous and skin closure. A small drain may be used at the surgeon's discretion.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Interilioabdominal Resection (Internal Hemipelvectomy by Kocher)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Interilioabdominal resection (internal, limb-sparing hemipelvectomy) is the en bloc removal of the pubis, ischium, and ilium with the acetabulum and the proximal end of the femur, with the femoral stump positioned against the residual ilium to form a "flail hip" or a forced arthrodesis. The operation was first performed by the Swiss surgeon Theodor Kocher <sup class="natflag">CH</sup> in 1884 for a chondrosarcoma of the ilium and described by him in 1897; subsequently performed by Roux <sup class="natflag">CH</sup> and entered the classical European tumour-orthopaedic armamentarium.

**Indications.** Primary malignant bony tumours around the hip with low malignancy grade (chondrosarcoma G1–G2, low-grade osteosarcoma after chemotherapy, well-differentiated Ewing sarcoma after induction), where surgical radicality is achievable and the highly mutilating external interilioabdominal amputation can be avoided.

**Preparation.** Psychological preparation, full cardiovascular and renal assessment, and at least 2000–2500 ml of matched fresh blood (modern equivalent: ROTEM-guided transfusion protocol, cell saver, and proactive fibrinogen replacement). The patient is supine with provision for prone repositioning during the procedure.

**Approach.** The skin incision begins over the middle of the iliac crest, reaches the ASIS, and continues along the inguinal crease to the symphysis; it may cross the midline. The inguinal ligament is divided from ASIS to pubis. External and internal oblique are divided from the inner side of the iliac crest, and gluteus medius is divided externally with the insertion of tensor fasciae latae. The insertion of one or both recti abdominis with their sheaths is divided and reflected superiorly together with the inguinal ligament, with the spermatic cord retracted medially. This gives wide access to the pelvic viscera and neurovascular bundle.

**Neurovascular bundle.** The most demanding step. Meticulous separation, ligation, and division of all branches from common iliac artery and vein down to the upper third of the femoral vessels; longitudinal incision of vessel adventitia eases the dissection. A rubber loop around the common iliac artery provides rapid haemorrhage control. The femoral nerve is dissected and looped. Iliopsoas is divided above the joint. The periosteum of the ilium is divided transversely well clear of the tumour, down to the greater sciatic notch. The symphysis is exposed by dividing the adductor insertions to the pubis. The obturator artery, vein, and nerve are divided as they enter the obturator membrane. A Gigli saw is passed through the greater sciatic notch, brought up over the iliac crest just anterior to the SI joint, and the ilium is divided. The inferior pubic ramus and ischial ramus are divided likewise. The hip joint with the tumour and the surrounding soft tissues is removed en bloc.

**Reconstruction.** The proximal femur is positioned against the residual ilium and fixed with wire for a neoarthrosis or, for fusion, with cancellous screws and additional cancellous grafts harvested from the iliac crest remnant. The inguinal ligament is sutured to the adductor tendons and fascia lata; the oblique abdominal muscles are sutured to the proximal end of gluteus medius; drains are placed; spica plaster immobilisation is applied.

**Classical Boychev variant <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** If the tumour involves more of the iliac body or wing with extension into the acetabular floor, the entire ilium with the acetabulum, part of the superior pubic ramus, the body of the ischium, and the femoral head are resected; the residual femoral neck is positioned against the resected ends of pubis and ischium for bony union; in this type of resection the entire weight-bearing axis is transferred to the symphysis and the contralateral hemipelvis.

**Modern equivalent.** Periacetabular (Enneking type II) resection with saddle prosthesis (Mark, 1979), pelvic megaprosthesis (LUMiC, Stanmore), or modular periacetabular endoprosthesis. In all cases the functional outcome is markedly better than external hemipelvectomy (limb preserved, ambulation with crutch or cane), at the cost of a longer operation, higher infection risk (15–30%), and late mechanical reconstruction failures.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Sacroiliac Arthrodesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Indications in current practice.** Tuberculous SI joint disease (rare in the developed world but retained as an indication in the Bulgarian context); chronic fracture-dislocation with persistent pain beyond 1.5 years; degenerative sacroiliitis refractory to conservative care; severe inflammatory sacroiliitis.

**Classical technique of B. Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** A posterior approach with a curved skin incision from the midpoint of the iliac crest, curving down over the PSIS and continuing 5–7 cm parallel to the origin of gluteus maximus. Subcutaneous tissue and thoracolumbar fascia are divided; gluteus maximus and medius are reflected subperiosteally from the outer ilium; the superficial layer of thoracolumbar fascia is divided; erector spinae is reflected medially from the sacrum. A rectangular bone block is cut from the ilium immediately over the joint, opening it. Articular cartilage is removed from both surfaces (iliac and sacral) down to cancellous bone, in which a trough is fashioned. The bone block is impacted cancellous-surface-in into the trough; cancellous chips are packed around it. Layered closure; plaster body cast immobilisation shoulder-to-knee for 3 months.

**Contemporary standard.** Percutaneous or mini-open fixation with 1–2 cannulated screws in sacroiliac trajectories (S1 and/or S2) under fluoroscopic/3D navigation, providing comparable fusion rates (90–95%) with markedly reduced immobilisation (6 weeks crutched ambulation) and surgical morbidity.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Interilioabdominal Amputation (Hemipelvectomy)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Interilioabdominal amputation (hemipelvectomia) is removal of the entire lower limb together with the corresponding half of the pelvis. First described by Charles Hawkins <sup class="natflag">UK</sup> in 1851 and popularised as a systematic procedure by Pringle <sup class="natflag">UK</sup> (1909) and Gordon-Taylor <sup class="natflag">UK</sup> (1935).

**Indications.** High-grade malignant tumours of the innominate or proximal femur where the limb cannot be salvaged (Ewing sarcoma with lumbosacral plexus invasion, osteosarcoma with extensive soft-tissue component, recurrence after prior internal hemipelvectomy); massive infections with limb gangrene; catastrophic injuries with limb non-viability.

**Approach.** A three-part incision.

*Abdominal part.* From the iliac crest along the inguinal crease to the symphysis; the inguinal ligament is divided; the external iliac artery and vein are isolated for high ligation (transfixion and double security) at the level of the common iliac vessels; the obturator artery, vein, and nerve are divided at the obturator membrane. A moist pack is left in place and attention turns to the perineal part.

*Perineal part.* With the limb maximally abducted, the incision is continued from the symphysis down and laterally along the inferior pubic ramus and ischial ramus to the tuberosity; from the medial side the ischiocavernosus and transversus perinei superficialis are released subperiosteally; the symphyseal ligaments and fibrocartilage are divided with an osteotome, or the two pubic rami are divided with a Gigli saw 4–5 cm from the symphysis.

*Posterior part.* The incision continues posteriorly along the iliac crest to the PSIS, then turns sharply toward the greater trochanter and continues distally along the gluteal crease to the perineum, where it meets the perineal incision. The posterior and inferior margins of gluteus maximus are exposed, the aponeurosis is divided along the incision — forming a large skin-subcutaneous-muscular flap.

The superior and inferior gluteal vessels are isolated, ligated well proximally, and divided. Iliopsoas is divided at the SI joint level. A Gigli saw is passed through the greater sciatic notch, brought up over the iliac crest just anterior to the SI joint, and the ilium is divided. The sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments are divided, freeing the pelvic bones. Levator ani is divided close to its origin on the medial pubic surface, completely isolating the limb and its half of the pelvis. Closure is with a posterior (gluteal) myocutaneous flap (most common) or with an anterior (quadriceps) flap when a posterior tumour involves the gluteal zone; drains for 48–72 hours.

**Complications.** Operative mortality 5–10% in contemporary series (higher in the elderly and the anaemic); flap necrosis 10–20%; deep infection 15–25%; phantom pain over 80%; significant psychosocial sequelae with high rates of postoperative depression.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Periacetabular and Pelvic Osteotomies (notes)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical periacetabular osteotomies for residual developmental hip dysplasia (DDH) are executed from the pelvic side and are systematically reviewed in Topic Ortho-1. From the pelvic anatomy perspective the key concepts are:

**Salter innominate osteotomy <sup class="natflag">CA</sup> (1961).** A single transverse cut from the greater sciatic notch to the iliopectineal eminence, rotating the distal fragment anterolaterally around the symphysis as a hinge. Yields 15–25° of lateral coverage; ideal in children 18 months to 6 years.

**Pemberton osteotomy <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1965).** Incomplete (sparing posterior cortex) pericapsular osteotomy hinging on triradiate cartilage, yielding 25–35° of lateral coverage by remodelling the acetabular cavity itself; applicable until triradiate closure (around 10 years).

**Steel osteotomy <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1973).** Triple osteotomy (ilium + superior pubic ramus + inferior ischial ramus), permitting full acetabular rotation in adolescents and young adults with mature or near-mature skeleton.

**Dega osteotomy <sup class="natflag">PL</sup>.** Incomplete inferiorly-based osteotomy hinging on the posterior cortex, conceptually analogous to Pemberton and favoured in the Polish school.

**Chiari osteotomy <sup class="natflag">AT</sup>.** Medially-displacing osteotomy above the joint capsule, forming a bony roof over the head as a joint replacement — a salvage in skeletally mature adults where PAO is unsuitable.

**Ganz periacetabular osteotomy <sup class="natflag">CH</sup> (1988).** Quadruple osteotomy (superior pubic ramus, inferior ischial ramus, inferior ilial ramus, posterior columnar cut), allowing full acetabular rotation while preserving the posterior pelvic ring — the modern gold standard in skeletally mature adults with symptomatic dysplasia.

From the pelvic anatomy perspective, all these osteotomies traverse three interrelated zones: the anterosuperior zone containing the femoral nerve within iliopsoas (at risk during retraction); the middle zone containing the obturator nerve at its emergence from the obturator foramen; and the posteroinferior zone containing the sciatic nerve at its emergence below piriformis. The Ganz PAO demands intra-operative fluoroscopy to confirm that the posterior columnar cuts remain in the safe zone (at least 1 cm from the greater sciatic notch to protect the superior gluteal artery and sciatic nerve).

</details>

The iliac crest is the classical autograft donor site in orthopaedics. The principal technical points: the incision lies along the crest, at least 2 cm posterior to the ASIS — to avoid the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (passing under the inguinal ligament 1–2 cm medial to ASIS, injury → meralgia paresthetica) and the ilioinguinal nerve. With an anterior approach, a tricortical block (for reconstructive pelvic or spinal procedures) or cortico-cancellous chips may be harvested; with a posterior approach (for spinal fusion), the incision runs along the crest from the PSIS, protecting the superior cluneal nerves (crossing the crest approximately 7–8 cm lateral to midline). Resection depth is limited to about 2 cm below the crest surface, preserving the pelvic ring and avoiding breach into the lesser pelvis; the inner table harvest is typically more problematic owing to iliacus and the neurovascular structures. ## Outcomes and Complications
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Complications of pelvic surgical approaches vary by anatomical region and procedure type.

**Ilioinguinal (Letournel).** Inguinal hernia (3–8%); meralgia paresthetica from lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury (20–60%, often transient); vascular injury (especially corona mortis and left common iliac vein); deep vein thrombosis (5–15% without prophylaxis, under 3% with LMWH); infection (3–5%); femoral nerve palsy with aggressive middle-window retraction (under 2%, usually transient).

**Anterior intrapelvic (Stoppa).** Vascular injury (corona mortis, iliac vessels); bladder injury (1–3%); obturator nerve injury (2–5%, usually transient); inguinal/femoral hernia (less common than after ilioinguinal); infection.

**Kocher-Langenbeck.** Sciatic nerve injury (10–30%, predominantly peroneal); heterotopic ossification (10–90% without prophylaxis; 15–25% with indomethacin/radiation); infection (3–7%); wound complications; abductor dysfunction with extensive proximal extension or trochanteric osteotomy nonunion.

**Extended iliofemoral.** Heterotopic ossification (50–90%); abductor dysfunction (30–50%); infection (7–15%); largely abandoned in favour of combined two-stage approaches.

**Pfannenstiel and symphyseal fixation.** Bladder injury (under 2%); infection (under 5%); mechanical failure with isolated anterior fixation in a posteriorly injured ring (Tile C).

**Tumour-resective pelvic surgery.** Zatsepin/Berglezov — gluteus maximus necrosis with improper dissection (under 5% in experienced hands); inferior gluteal artery and nerve injury; local tumour recurrence (10–30% for chondrosarcoma G1–G2). Internal hemipelvectomy (Kocher) — infection 15–30%; mechanical failure of "flail hip" in long-term follow-up (megaprostheses carry a 30–50% five-year revision risk); functional outcome — ambulation with cane or unaided in 60–70%. External hemipelvectomy — operative mortality 5–10%; flap necrosis 10–20%; deep infection 15–25%; phantom pain over 80%.

**Sacrectomy.** Neurological loss by level of resection (S5 silent; S4 mild anal hypoaesthesia; S3 stress incontinence; S2 complete sphincter incontinence; S1 plus proximal pelvic instability); infection (15–25% because of anus proximity); wound dehiscence; 30-day mortality 3–8%.

**Long-term outcomes of acetabular fractures.** Letournel (1993) series: anatomical reduction (< 1 mm) in anterior column 70–80%, posterior wall 60–70%, both-column 40–60%; post-traumatic arthrosis at 10 years — 15–25% with anatomical reduction, 40–60% with non-anatomical (> 2 mm). Prognosis is determined by age, time from injury to surgery (< 21 days optimal), associated sciatic nerve injury at presentation, and quality of reduction.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school; the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya, Sofia. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curriculum reference and contains detailed descriptions of pelvic approaches, including:

- *Topographic-anatomical concept of greater and lesser pelvis*, separated by the linea terminalis, with the internal muscles (iliopsoas, piriformis, obturator internus) passing through the pelvic foramina, and the iliopectineal bursa with its 10–15% communication with the hip joint.
- *Classical technique of partial sacrectomy for chordoma* with a transverse arcuate posterior incision strongly convex superiorly, with a large distal flap protecting the wound from anal contamination; preservation of S3 bilaterally as a critical sphincter-function rule.
- *Coccygectomy with a horseshoe incision* convex superiorly, with the same intent.
- *Sacroiliac arthrodesis* through a posterior approach, cutting a rectangular bone block over the joint, impaction into a prepared trough with additional cancellous chips, and plaster cast immobilisation shoulder-to-knee for 3 months.
- *Classical interilioabdominal resection (internal hemipelvectomy)* with a variant in which extensive tumours with acetabular floor invasion call for en bloc removal of the entire ilium, part of the pubis, the body of the ischium, and the femoral head, with the residual femoral neck positioned against the resected ends of pubis and ischium for bony union — a conceptual predecessor of the saddle prosthesis.
- *Interilioabdominal amputation (hemipelvectomy)* — systematic presentation of indications, operative tactic, and reconstructive flap variants.

**P. Tivchev, P. Tanchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian contributions to percutaneous iliosacral screw fixation (adaptation and translation of the Routt-Simonian technique into the Bulgarian curriculum, with modifications for patients with smaller canals or dysmorphic sacrum).

From the classical Soviet school, integrated into the Bulgarian curriculum:

**S. T. Zatsepin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Two-stage combined approach (anterior perineal plus posterior gluteal) for tumours involving the inferior pubic ramus, the ischium, and the body of the ilium together. Key technical points: blunt separation of tendinous and muscular components of gluteus maximus at the upper pole to preserve the inferior gluteal artery and nerve; horizontal limb of the second incision at a right angle to the vertical to prevent inferior triangular flap necrosis.

**V. G. Berglezov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Modified posterior approach to the ischium and the posterior acetabular wall for tumours involving the retroacetabular zone. Arc-shaped incision between the anterior border of gluteus maximus and the posterior border of tensor fasciae latae with S-shaped extension down the thigh; principle of strict subperiosteal release of gluteus maximus to preserve the inferior gluteal artery and the pudendal vessels.

From the German and Swiss schools:

**Theodor Kocher <sup class="natflag">CH</sup> (1841–1917).** First interilioabdominal resection (internal hemipelvectomy) for chondrosarcoma of the ilium, 1884; systematic description in 1897. With Langenbeck, his name remains attached to the posterior pelvic approach (Kocher-Langenbeck).

**César Roux <sup class="natflag">CH</sup>.** Independent descriptions of variants of interilioabdominal resection in the European tumour-orthopaedic curriculum.

**E. Selig and P. Stoffel <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>.** Intrapelvic obturator neurectomy (1910s–1920s) — classical neurectomy for adductor release, with an extrapelvic variant (Stoffel) and selective anterior-branch resection after Adams.

**R. Stoppa <sup class="natflag">FR</sup>.** Original preperitoneal anterior approach for incisional and recurrent inguinal hernia (1973), which Hirvensalo (1993) and Cole/Bolhofner (1994) modified into the anterior intrapelvic approach for acetabular fractures.

**Emile Letournel and Robert Judet <sup class="natflag">FR</sup>.** Acetabular column concept, ilioinguinal and extended iliofemoral approaches, acetabular fracture classification; *Fractures of the Acetabulum* (1974; 2nd ed. 1993) remains the definitive reference.

**Marvin Tile <sup class="natflag">CA</sup>.** Stability-based classification of pelvic fractures (1980, revised 1988, 2003); *Fractures of the Pelvis and Acetabulum* (3rd ed., 2003) — the contemporary operative reference.

**Reinhold Ganz <sup class="natflag">CH</sup>.** Periacetabular osteotomy (PAO, 1988); trochanteric flip osteotomy with safe surgical hip dislocation (Ganz et al., 2001).

</div>
</details>
</section>

## Classical Sources

<section class="topic-section" id="classical">
<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical line — pelvic and acetabular surgery</strong>
  <p>Pelvic surgery in its modern sense is a product of the second half of the 20th century, when Robert Judet and Emile Letournel in Paris developed the acetabular column concept and the systematic operative approaches. Earlier pelvic fractures and tumours were treated more often in a general-surgical and transabdominal context — an evolution from Antonio de Gimbernat's *Nuevo método de operar en la hernia crural* (Madrid, 1793; the founding anatomical description of the ligamentum lacunare/Gimbernat ligament), through Joseph-François Malgaigne's *Traité des fractures et des luxations* (1847; classification of the Malgaigne fracture — today's Tile C1), to the first systematic pelvic resections by Theodor Kocher in Bern (1884) and to the modern Letournel-Judet and Tile era.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Gimbernat 1793</strong> — *Nuevo método de operar en la hernia crural* (Madrid); anatomical description of the ligamentum lacunare and its surgical significance (<a href="https://archive.org/details/b28757257_0001">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Larrey 1812</strong> — *Mémoires de chirurgie militaire et campagnes*, vol. 1; military observations on pelvic gunshot injuries (<a href="https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdechiru01larr">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Malgaigne 1847</strong> — *Traité des fractures et des luxations*, vol. 1; classification of pelvic fractures, eponymous Malgaigne fracture (<a href="https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_32985x01">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Kocher 1884/1897</strong> — first interilioabdominal resection for chondrosarcoma of the ilium; systematic description in *Chirurgische Operationslehre* (5. Auflage, 1907) (<a href="https://archive.org/details/chirurgischeope00koch">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Pfannenstiel 1900</strong> — the transverse suprapubic approach, borrowed in contemporary pelvic surgery for symphyseal osteosynthesis and the modified Stoppa.</li>
    <li><strong>Hawkins 1851 → Pringle 1909 → Gordon-Taylor 1935</strong> — evolution of hemipelvectomy from military to systematic tumour-orthopaedic procedure.</li>
    <li><strong>Letournel-Judet 1964–1993</strong> — column concept, ilioinguinal and extended iliofemoral approaches; *Fractures of the Acetabulum* (2nd ed. 1993) — the definitive reference.</li>
    <li><strong>Tile 1980 → 2003</strong> — stability-based pelvic fracture classification; *Fractures of the Pelvis and Acetabulum* (3rd ed. 2003).</li>
    <li><strong>Hirvensalo 1993 → Cole-Bolhofner 1994</strong> — modification of the anterior preperitoneal Stoppa approach (1973) for acetabular surgery.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Classical descriptions of pelvic osteotomies: Salter 1961 (innominate); Pemberton 1965 (incomplete pericapsular); Steel 1973 (triple); Chiari (medialising); Ganz 1988 (PAO). Classical descriptions of salvage procedures: Mark 1979 (saddle prosthesis); Brown et al. 1980 (allograft-prosthetic composite for periacetabular tumours). Sacral fracture classification: Denis 1988 (zones 1–3); Roy-Camille (U-shape).

  (function () {
    function openByHash(hash) {

      var id = hash.replace(/^#/, "");
      if (!id) return;
      var el = document.getElementById(id);
      if (!el) return;
      if (el.tagName === "DETAILS") el.open = true;
      var inner = el.querySelector(":scope > details");
      if (inner) inner.open = true;
      var node = el.parentElement;
      while (node && node !== document.body) {
        if (node.tagName === "DETAILS") node.open = true;
        node = node.parentElement;
      }
      requestAnimationFrame(function () {
        el.scrollIntoView({ behavior: "smooth", block: "start" });
      });
    }
    function wire() {
      openByHash(window.location.hash);
      document.addEventListener("click", function (e) {
        var t = e.target;
        while (t && t !== document) {
          if (t.tagName === "A" && t.getAttribute && t.getAttribute("href") && t.getAttribute("href").charAt(0) === "#") {
            openByHash(t.getAttribute("href"));
            break;
          }
          t = t.parentNode;
        }
      });
      window.addEventListener("hashchange", function () { openByHash(window.location.hash); });
    }
    if (document.readyState === "loading") {
      document.addEventListener("DOMContentLoaded", wire);
    } else {
      wire();
    }
