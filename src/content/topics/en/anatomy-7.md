## Introduction

The pelvis presents the orthopedic surgeon with one of the most complex regional anatomical environments, combining the bony pelvic ring (the load-transmitting structure between the spine and the lower extremities) with the pelvic viscera, the iliac vessels and their branches, the lumbosacral plexus, and the abdominal wall musculature. The surgical approaches to the pelvis must navigate this complexity while providing access to specific bony regions for fracture fixation, tumor resection, joint replacement, or other procedures. The principal approaches — the ilioinguinal approach (Letournel), the anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa) approach, the Kocher-Langenbeck (posterior) approach, the extended iliofemoral approach, and the anterior (Pfannenstiel) approach to the symphysis — each provide access to specific regions of the pelvis with characteristic risks and benefits. This chapter, drawing on Orthopaedic Surgical Approaches, AO Principles of Fracture Management, Netter’s Concise Orthopaedic Anatomy, and the classical Bulgarian Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology of Boycho Boychev (B. Boychev, ed.; Sofia) — the namesake of the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya, Sofia — supplemented with the classical procedures of Zatsepin (two-stage combined approach to the inferior pubic ramus, ischium, and body of the ilium), Berglezov (modified posterior approach to the ischium and posterior wall of the acetabulum), Selig-Stoffel (intrapelvic obturator neurectomy), interilioabdominal resection by Kocher (1884/1897) and Roux, the partial sacral and coccygeal resections of B. Boychev, and the classical interilioabdominal amputation (hemipelvectomy), addresses the surgical anatomy and principal approaches to the pelvis.

The pelvic ring comprises two innominate bones (each formed by the fusion of the ilium, ischium, and pubis at the triradiate cartilage) and the sacrum, joined anteriorly at the pubic symphysis and posteriorly at the sacroiliac (SI) joints. The detailed anatomy was addressed in Topics Trauma-19 (pelvic ring) and Trauma-20 (acetabulum). The principal bony landmarks relevant to surgical approaches include: Iliac crest: The superior margin of the ilium, palpable from the ASIS anteriorly to the PSIS posteriorly. The crest provides the bony graft donor site classically used for bone harvesting. Anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS): The anterior projection at the anterior end of the iliac crest. The origin of the sartorius and the inguinal ligament. The lateral end of the inguinal ligament attaches here. Anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS): Just below the ASIS. The origin of the direct head of the rectus femoris. Posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS): The posterior projection at the posterior end of the iliac crest. Often marked by a dimple in the overlying skin. Posterior inferior iliac spine (PIIS): Just below the PSIS. Greater sciatic notch: The large posterior notch between the PIIS and the ischial spine, transmitting the sciatic nerve and other structures. Lesser sciatic notch: Between the ischial spine and the ischial tuberosity, transmitting the obturator internus, pudendal nerve, and pudendal vessels.

Ischial spine: The bony projection at the junction of the body and ramus of the ischium. Ischial tuberosity: The “sit bone”, the inferior weight-bearing portion of the ischium. The origin of the hamstrings. Pubic tubercle: The bony projection on the superior pubic ramus, lateral to the symphysis. The medial end of the inguinal ligament attaches here. Iliopectineal eminence: The bony prominence at the junction of the ilium and the superior pubic ramus, marking the location of the iliopectineal line on the pelvic brim. Pelvic brim (linea terminalis): The continuous line from the sacral promontory across the iliopectineal line to the pubic tubercle, separating the true pelvis (below) from the false pelvis (above). Quadrilateral surface: The flat bone of the medial acetabular wall facing into the true pelvis. Visualized through the anterior intrapelvic approach.

## Pelvic Topography in the Classical Bulgarian School

The classical Bulgarian topographic-anatomical concept, as set out in B. Boychev's *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology*, divides the pelvis into the greater pelvis (pelvis major) and the lesser pelvis (pelvis minor), separated by the linea terminalis. The greater pelvis is bounded by the iliac wings and the last lumbar vertebra; the lesser pelvis is bounded by the bodies of the pubis, ilium, and ischium, the sacrum, and the coccyx. The soft tissues covering the pelvis externally belong to the gluteal and femoral regions. The external muscles of the pelvic girdle (glutei, tensor fasciae latae) insert on the proximal femur and drive gait, while the internal muscles — iliopsoas (in the greater pelvis), piriformis, and obturator internus (in the lesser pelvis) — pass through pelvic foramina and also insert on the proximal femur.

Between the posterior surface of iliopsoas and the hip capsule lies the iliopectineal bursa, which communicates with the joint cavity in about 10% of cases — anatomically important in infection and in joint aspiration. Piriformis has a triangular form within the pelvis and on emerging through the greater sciatic foramen forms two classical openings — foramen suprapiriforme (above) and foramen infrapiriforme (below) — transmitting the superior gluteal nerve and the complex of sciatic, inferior gluteal, and pudendal nerves and vessels respectively. Obturator internus exits the lesser pelvis through the lesser sciatic foramen, bending at a right angle and accompanied by the two gemelli.

The pelvic vessels and nerves lie in the subperitoneal fat between the peritoneum and pelvic fascia. The internal iliac artery arises from the common iliac at the level of the SI joint and runs down, outward, and backward along the posterolateral wall of the lesser pelvis, medial to the iliopsoas. Its stem is relatively short (3–4 cm), creating a technical challenge for vascular control in depth. The transversalis fascia, on entering the lesser pelvis, covers the internal muscles and neurovascular structures and is termed fascia endopelvina; it also covers the pelvic floor. The space between fascia endopelvina and the peritoneum, filled with loose or fatty connective tissue, is most prominent lateral to the visceral organs and defines the plane of blunt dissection for the anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa) approach.

## Muscular Anatomy

The principal muscles relevant to pelvic approaches include: Abdominal wall muscles: External oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis, rectus abdominis. These are encountered in anterior pelvic approaches. Iliacus and psoas (iliopsoas): Originate from the iliac fossa and the lumbar vertebrae respectively, joining to insert on the lesser trochanter. Encountered in approaches that enter the iliac fossa from above. Gluteus maximus: The principal posterior pelvic muscle, providing hip extension. Encountered in the Kocher-Langenbeck and extended iliofemoral approaches. Gluteus medius and minimus: The principal hip abductors, originating from the outer iliac wing. Their preservation is critical to gait function after pelvic surgery. Piriformis: From the anterior sacrum through the greater sciatic notch to the greater trochanter. The principal landmark for identifying the sciatic nerve in posterior pelvic surgery. Obturator internus: From the inner pelvic surface around the lesser sciatic notch to the greater trochanter. Sartorius: From the ASIS to the medial tibia. Encountered in the ilioinguinal approach. Rectus femoris: From the AIIS and the supra-acetabular ilium to the patella via the quadriceps tendon. Encountered in anterior hip and pelvis approaches.

## Iliac Vessels

The common iliac arteries branch from the abdominal aorta at approximately the level of L4 and divide into the internal iliac (hypogastric) and external iliac arteries at approximately the SI joint. The external iliac artery continues across the pelvic brim to become the femoral artery beneath the inguinal ligament; it gives off the inferior epigastric artery just above the inguinal ligament. The internal iliac artery has numerous branches supplying the pelvic viscera, the gluteal region (superior gluteal artery), the obturator region (obturator artery), and the perineum (pudendal artery). The iliac veins parallel the arteries. The common iliac veins join to form the inferior vena cava. The left common iliac vein is particularly vulnerable to injury in the anterior approaches because of its medial position relative to the artery. The corona mortis (“crown of death”) is a vascular anastomosis between the obturator artery (from the internal iliac) and the external iliac/inferior epigastric system, crossing the superior pubic ramus. The corona mortis is present in 30 to 80 percent of individuals and is at substantial risk during ilioinguinal and anterior intrapelvic approaches; injury produces significant bleeding that may be difficult to control because the vessel retracts into the obturator foramen.

The lumbosacral plexus is formed from the anterior rami of L4 through S4. The principal nerves include: Femoral nerve (L2-L4): Descends through the iliac fossa anterior to the iliacus muscle, then passes beneath the inguinal ligament lateral to the femoral artery. Innervates the iliopsoas, quadriceps, sartorius, and pectineus. Obturator nerve (L2-L4): Descends through the pelvis on the lateral wall, passing through the obturator foramen. Innervates the obturator externus and the adductor compartment of the thigh. Sciatic nerve (L4-S3): The largest nerve in the body. Exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, typically inferior to the piriformis muscle. Innervates the hamstrings and (via its peroneal and tibial divisions) all the muscles of the leg and foot. Superior gluteal nerve (L4-S1): Exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, superior to the piriformis (the only nerve to do so). Runs between the gluteus medius and minimus, supplying both muscles and the tensor fasciae latae. Iatrogenic injury during the lateral approach to the hip is a recognized cause of postoperative abductor weakness. Inferior gluteal nerve (L5-S2): Exits inferior to the piriformis. Supplies the gluteus maximus. Pudendal nerve (S2-S4): Exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below the piriformis, then re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen. Supplies the perineum.

Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (L2-L3): Crosses anterior to the iliacus muscle and passes beneath the inguinal ligament approximately 1 to 2 cm medial to the ASIS. Provides sensation to the lateral thigh. Frequently encountered (and at risk) in anterior pelvic approaches; injury produces meralgia paresthetica.

## Lumbar Plexus Within the Psoas

The lumbar plexus lies within the substance of the psoas muscle and is at particular risk in lateral transpsoas approaches to the lumbar spine (XLIF/LLIF, see [Topic Anatomy-1](/en/anatomy/1/)). The L4 nerve root is the most caudal and is at greatest risk at the L4-L5 level.

## Ilioinguinal Approach (Letournel)

The ilioinguinal approach (Letournel, 1960s) is the classical anterior approach to the pelvis and acetabulum, providing access to the entire anterior column of the acetabulum, the iliac wing, the pelvic brim, the quadrilateral surface (from anterior), and the symphysis pubis. The approach is used for acetabular fracture fixation (anterior column, anterior wall, anterior column with posterior hemitransverse, both-column patterns), iliac wing fracture fixation, pelvic ring fracture fixation (combined with other approaches), tumor resection, and selected revision pelvic procedures.

## Patient Positioning

The patient is supine on a radiolucent table. The bladder is catheterized. The legs are draped to permit hip flexion as needed during the procedure.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

The principal landmarks are the iliac crest, ASIS, inguinal ligament, and the midline of the pubic symphysis. The skin incision follows the iliac crest from approximately 2 cm posterior to the iliac crest highpoint, curves around the ASIS, and continues medially along the inguinal ligament to the midline at the symphysis.

## Approach — The Three Windows

The classical Letournel approach uses three “windows” through the abdominal wall, with the inguinal canal traversed in the process: The lateral (first) window: Between the ASIS and the iliopsoas. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve crosses this region and is identified and protected. The iliacus is reflected medially from the inner table of the ilium, providing access to the iliac wing, the iliac fossa, and the anterior column of the acetabulum. The middle (second) window: Between the iliopsoas medially (containing the femoral nerve and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve) and the external iliac vessels (artery and vein) medially. The window provides access to the pelvic brim and the superior portion of the quadrilateral surface.

The medial (third) window: Between the external iliac vessels laterally and the spermatic cord (or round ligament in females) and the rectus abdominis medially. The window provides access to the superior pubic ramus and the symphysis.

## Critical Structures

The structures crossing the operative field that must be identified and protected include: The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve beneath the inguinal ligament near the ASIS. The femoral nerve, artery, and vein in the femoral sheath beneath the inguinal ligament. The iliopsoas muscle containing the femoral nerve. The inguinal ligament itself, which is reconstructed at closure. The corona mortis vascular anastomosis crossing the superior pubic ramus. The inferior epigastric vessels ascending behind the rectus abdominis. The spermatic cord (or round ligament) traversing the inguinal canal.


## Closure and Complications

The closure includes meticulous repair of the inguinal canal floor and the inguinal ligament to prevent inguinal hernia (a recognized complication). Other complications include inguinal hernia (3 to 8 percent), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury with meralgia paresthetica (very common, 20 to 60 percent, but often transient), vascular injury (the principal feared complication, particularly to the corona mortis or the inferior epigastric vessels), deep vein thrombosis, and infection.

## Anterior Intrapelvic (Modified Stoppa) Approach

The anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa) approach was developed by Hirvensalo and Cole (1993) as a modification of the original Stoppa hernia approach, providing access to the pelvic brim, the quadrilateral surface, and the posterior column of the acetabulum from anterior, without the morbidity of the three-window ilioinguinal approach.

## Patient Positioning

The patient is supine on a radiolucent table, with the bladder catheterized.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

A Pfannenstiel-type transverse incision is made approximately 2 cm above the pubic symphysis, or a midline vertical incision can be used for extended exposure.

## Approach

The skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised. The rectus abdominis is divided in the midline (with the linea alba being the avascular plane). The transversalis fascia and peritoneum are encountered; the peritoneum is retracted cephalad to enter the preperitoneal space (the space of Retzius). The dissection then proceeds laterally along the pelvic brim. The corona mortis is identified and ligated when present. The iliac vessels are identified and retracted laterally. The iliopsoas muscle with the femoral nerve within it is retracted laterally. The obturator nerve and vessels are identified entering the obturator foramen. The pelvic brim, the quadrilateral surface, the medial wall of the acetabulum, and the posterior column from anterior can all be visualized through this approach. Reduction and plating of fractures in these regions can be performed. The modified Stoppa is increasingly preferred over the classical ilioinguinal for many acetabular fracture patterns because of better visualization of the quadrilateral surface and the medial wall, with reduced overall morbidity compared with the three-window approach.

## Complications

The complications include vascular injury (corona mortis, iliac vessels), bladder injury (if the bladder is not adequately drained), obturator nerve injury, and inguinal/femoral hernia (less common than with the ilioinguinal approach).

## Kocher-Langenbeck (Posterior) Approach

The Kocher-Langenbeck approach is the classical posterior approach to the pelvis and acetabulum, providing access to the posterior wall, posterior column, and the ischial region. The approach is used for posterior wall and posterior column acetabular fracture fixation, transverse and T-shaped fracture fixation (when the posterior column is dominant), open reduction of posterior hip dislocations, and tumor resection of the posterior pelvis.

## Patient Positioning

The patient is prone or lateral decubitus with the affected side up. The lateral decubitus position is more commonly used because it allows easier sciatic nerve monitoring and easier conversion to anterior or extended approaches if needed.

## Surface Landmarks and Incision

The greater trochanter, the PSIS, and the midline of the gluteus maximus mark the line of the incision. The skin incision begins at the PSIS, curves down and laterally over the greater trochanter, and extends distally for approximately 5 to 8 cm into the proximal thigh.

## Approach

The skin and subcutaneous tissue are incised. The fascia lata and gluteal aponeurosis are opened in line with the skin incision. The gluteus maximus is split in line with its fibers; the principal innervation (inferior gluteal nerve) enters from the medial side, so the splitting is typically biased laterally to preserve innervation. Beneath the gluteus maximus, the short external rotators are encountered (from superior to inferior: piriformis, gemellus superior, obturator internus, gemellus inferior, quadratus femoris). The piriformis is identified — this is the critical anatomical landmark for the sciatic nerve, which typically exits the pelvis just inferior to the piriformis. The sciatic nerve is identified and protected throughout the remainder of the procedure (with some surgeons placing a vessel loop around the nerve for retraction). The short external rotators are then incised at their trochanteric insertions and reflected medially. The posterior hip capsule is exposed. Trochanteric osteotomy or trochanteric flip osteotomy (Ganz) can be performed to extend the exposure proximally to the supra-acetabular region and to permit safe surgical hip dislocation.

  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>

## Complications

The complications of the Kocher-Langenbeck approach include sciatic nerve injury (the principal concern, 10 to 30 percent of patients have some degree of postoperative sciatic nerve dysfunction, predominantly peroneal-division injury; many recover but residual deficits are common), heterotopic ossification (10 to 90 percent, substantially reduced by indomethacin or low-dose radiation prophylaxis), infection, wound complications, and abductor dysfunction (with extensive proximal extension or trochanteric osteotomy nonunion).

## Extended Iliofemoral Approach

The extended iliofemoral approach (Letournel) provides the most comprehensive access to both columns of the acetabulum from a single approach. However, the substantial morbidity (heterotopic ossification rates of 50 to 90 percent, abductor dysfunction, infection) has caused this approach to be largely abandoned in favor of combined approaches (Kocher-Langenbeck plus ilioinguinal in stages) for the most complex acetabular fractures.


## Approach

The patient is in lateral decubitus. The skin incision combines a posterior portion (along the iliac crest from the PSIS) with an anterior portion (curving down to the greater trochanter). The gluteus maximus is reflected posteriorly, and the gluteus medius and minimus are reflected anteriorly (with trochanteric osteotomy to preserve their attachment to the bone fragment). The approach provides access to both columns of the acetabulum from outside the pelvis. The principal complications limit the use of this approach to selected severe injuries where other approaches are inadequate.

## Pfannenstiel Approach to the Pubic Symphysis

The Pfannenstiel approach provides direct access to the pubic symphysis for symphyseal disruption fixation, anterior pelvic ring stabilization, and selected exposures of the inferior pubic region.

## Approach

The skin incision is transverse approximately 2 cm above the pubic symphysis, similar to the obstetric Pfannenstiel incision but typically smaller. The rectus abdominis is split or divided in the midline (with care for the bladder). The anterior aspect of the symphysis is exposed and the symphyseal plate is applied.

## Complications

The complications include bladder injury (with inadequate decompression or aggressive retraction), infection, and wound complications.

## Symphyseal Fixation for Traumatic Diastasis

Traumatic symphyseal diastasis (a component of open-book pelvic injury, AO/Tile type B1) is the indication for symphyseal osteosynthesis when closed reduction cannot be obtained or maintained. For fresh diastasis the patient is placed in lateral or semi-lateral decubitus, which aids gravity-assisted reduction; for chronic diastasis with interposed scar tissue, supine with a slight pelvic tilt is more convenient. The approach is Pfannenstiel; once the anterior symphysis is exposed, fixation may use a double (superior and anterior) reconstruction plate or a more rigid LCP plate with screws extending into the inferior pubic ramus. The classical technique in the absence of dedicated instrumentation uses a transpubic cerclage wire or screw circling the two pubic tubercles. Regardless of method, adjunctive plaster spica immobilization for 6–8 weeks is recommended in patients without posterior pelvic injury; with associated posterior injury, additional posterior stabilization (sacroiliac screw or plate) is needed because isolated anterior fixation is biomechanically inadequate.

## Combined Approach to Inferior Pubic Ramus, Ischium, and Body of Ilium (Zatsepin)

The Zatsepin combined approach is a classical Soviet concept providing wide exposure of the inferior pubic ramus, the body of the ischium, and the body of the ilium in a single operative session. It was developed for resection of tumours (benign or low-grade malignant) involving more than one anatomical zone of the innominate bone, where neither standard anterior nor standard posterior approaches alone provides adequate field. Conceptually Zatsepin combines an anterior perineal approach to the pubic bone and inferior ramus with a modified posterior Kocher-Langenbeck exposure of the body of the ilium and upper ischium.

The procedure is performed in two stages. Stage 1: the patient is supine in lithotomy position with hips and knees flexed and abducted. The skin incision starts lateral to the base of the scrotum along the proximal edge of the superior pubic ramus, continues along the inferior ramus, and reaches the ischial tuberosity along the gluteal crease. The adductor insertions are divided; the spermatic cord is retracted medially; the corpus cavernosum is bluntly mobilized at its base. Obturator externus and internus are elevated along with the obturator membrane. The inferior pubic ramus is divided with a Gigli saw at the two endpoints of the planned resection.

Stage 2: the patient is turned prone. A vertical incision is made 4 cm posterior to the greater trochanter. The tendinous part of gluteus maximus is divided, while in its upper pole the muscle fibres are split longitudinally by blunt technique — the critical step that permits high reflection of gluteus maximus without compromising its blood supply from the inferior gluteal artery and its innervation by the inferior gluteal nerve. The first incision is extended from the ischial tuberosity along the gluteal crease until it meets the midpoint of the second. To avoid necrosis of the inferior triangular flap, Zatsepin recommends that the outer part of the horizontal incision be made not exactly along the gluteal crease but horizontally, meeting the vertical limb at a right angle. The combined exposure provides access to the body of the ilium and the upper ischium from a posterior angle in which the sciatic nerve can be looped with a vessel loop and protected throughout the dissection.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The Zatsepin combined approach (1960s, Soviet school) is a two-stage procedure — anterior perineal plus posterior gluteal — for tumours involving the inferior pubic ramus, ischium, and body of the ilium together, in zones that no single approach covers. Technical key point: the posterior stage splits the tendinous and muscular parts of gluteus maximus by blunt dissection at the upper pole, preserving inferior gluteal artery and nerve — without this, the muscle is devitalised and persistent abductor weakness results.</p>

## Modified Posterior Approach to the Ischium and Posterior Acetabular Wall (Berglezov)

For tumours of the ischium involving the posterior wall of the acetabulum, V. G. Berglezov (Soviet orthopaedist) modified the posterior stage of the Zatsepin approach to provide safe exposure of the retroacetabular bone without sacrificing gluteus maximus as a whole. The patient is prone with the pelvis elevated. The skin incision begins 2–3 cm lateral to the posterior superior iliac spine, runs in an arc downward between the anterior border of gluteus maximus and the posterior border of tensor fasciae latae, crosses the gluteal crease, and continues as an S-shape for 7–8 cm down the posterior thigh.

The tendinous part of gluteus maximus inserting on the iliotibial tract and the gluteal tuberosity is divided. The entire muscle is reflected superiorly and medially, with selective blunt division of upper-pole fibres as needed to preserve the neurovascular pedicle. Tensor fasciae latae is retracted laterally and anteriorly to reveal the sciatic nerve and the short external rotators (gemelli, piriformis, quadratus femoris, obturator externus and internus). The sciatic nerve is taken on a vessel loop for monitoring throughout the procedure.

Berglezov's principle: if the extent and type of tumour permits strictly subperiosteal bony dissection, the risk of injury to the inferior gluteal artery and the pudendal vessels is virtually eliminated. The approach reveals the posterior acetabular wall from outside, the body and upper ramus of the ischium, and may be extended distally for access to the sciatic nerve in its distal course.

## Posterior Approach to the Sacroiliac Joint

The posterior SI joint approach is used for arthrodesis in tuberculous sacroiliitis, for arthrodesis in chronic fracture-dislocation with persistent pain beyond 1.5 years, for biopsy and resection of posterior pelvic tumours, and for open reduction and fixation of posterior pelvic injuries when percutaneous SI screw fixation is unsuitable (anatomical dysplasia, osteoporosis, extensive swelling).

The skin incision runs along the posterior third of the iliac crest and curves down and back 5–7 cm below the PSIS. The subcutaneous tissue and thoracolumbar fascia are divided. The fibres of gluteus maximus are split bluntly or its origin is reflected from the iliac crest. The aponeurosis of erector spinae is divided and retracted laterally and inferiorly, exposing the posterior surface of the ilium down to the SI joint. A rectangular bone block is cut with an osteotome from the ilium immediately over the joint, directed laterally; the inferior border of the block is roughly parallel to the greater sciatic notch. The joint cleft itself is opened by removing articular cartilage from both surfaces (iliac and sacral) down to cancellous bone. For arthrodesis, the bone block is impacted into the prepared trough with its cancellous surface inward; bone chips from the iliac crest are packed around it. Immobilization is in a plaster body cast from shoulder girdle to knees for 3 months — long, but necessary because the classical technique provides no compression. The contemporary alternative is percutaneous or mini-open SI screw fixation under fluoroscopic/3D navigation, which provides comparable fusion rates with much shorter immobilization.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>In the posterior SI approach, the principal hazards are: the superior gluteal artery emerging just above piriformis through the foramen suprapiriforme — if injured, it retracts into the pelvis and creates uncontrollable haematoma; lateral cutaneous branches of S1–S3 dorsal rami, the loss of which produces local cutaneous dysaesthesia; and with overly medial dissection, the superior cluneal nerves. The bone block is cut lateral to the joint, never crossing the anterior cortex, which abuts the lumbosacral plexus and internal iliac artery.</p>

## Intrapelvic Obturator Neurectomy (Selig-Stoffel)

Intrapelvic obturator neurectomy (the Selig-Stoffel technique, described in the late 1910s and 1920s) is a classical neurectomy for releasing the hip adductors. Current indications: severe adductor contractures in cerebral palsy refractory to botulinum toxin and conservative therapy; historically the procedure was used as a partial denervation for painful coxarthrosis with adductor contracture — an indication superseded by hip arthroplasty.

The patient is supine; the bladder is emptied by catheter pre-operatively. A transverse suprapubic incision is made, starting 2–3 cm lateral to the rectus abdominis and ending in the midline. The abdominal wall is divided and the preperitoneal space is entered (the same principle as the modified Stoppa approach). The peritoneum is retracted cephalad; the nerve is identified on the lateral wall of the lesser pelvis, where it passes along obturator internus toward the obturator foramen. A 2 cm segment of the nerve is resected, with clips or ligatures on both ends to discourage regrowth.

Alternatively, extrapelvic Stoffel neurectomy is performed at thigh level: an incision along the medial thigh 2 cm below the inguinal crease, dissection between pectineus and adductor longus; the anterior and posterior branches of the obturator nerve are identified at their emergence from the obturator foramen beneath obturator externus and selectively resected. The anterior branch supplies adductor longus, brevis, and gracilis; the posterior branch supplies obturator externus and part of adductor magnus. Selective anterior-branch resection (Adams, 1947) preserves adductor magnus as an active hip extensor — critical in ambulators, while complete trunk resection is acceptable in non-ambulators where hygiene positioning is the only goal.

## Resection of the Pubis and Ischium

Indications: benign bony tumours (osteochondroma, osteoid osteoma, aneurysmal bone cyst) and low-grade malignant tumours of limited size — chondrosarcoma G1, low-grade osteosarcoma after neoadjuvant therapy. The patient is in lithotomy. The skin incision begins 0.5 cm below the midpoint of the inguinal ligament, runs medially and parallel to it, curves down over the inferior pubic ramus, and ends at the ischial tuberosity. The adductor insertions and obturator externus are reflected subperiosteally, exposing the lower border of the obturator foramen with its membrane, part of the body and outer margin of the inferior pubic ramus, the ischial ramus, and the tuberosity.

For wider exposure the distal margin of gluteus maximus is retracted or divided transversely 3–4 cm, revealing the body of the ischium. The insertions of semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris (long head), and quadratus femoris are released from the lateral surface of the tuberosity. To expose the ischial ramus from inside, the sacrotuberous ligament and its insertion on the medial side of the tuberosity are divided — careful subperiosteal release of obturator internus and externus is required here to preserve, where possible, the obturator artery, vein, and nerve. The bone is cut with osteotome or Gigli saw at the two endpoints: ischial cut rarely extends above the ischial spine (protecting the pudendal nerve and vessels in the lesser sciatic notch); the pubis is divided from the symphysis with a resection knife. Wound closure is layered with a drain; with confirmed malignancy the wound is left open pending histology.

## Resection of the Ischium

Isolated ischial resection is indicated for primary tumours (chondrosarcoma, chordoma, low-grade Ewing) of limited size. The patient is in lithotomy. A gently curved incision is made over the inferior pubic ramus and ischium, passing over the tuberosity and ending lateral to the sacrum. The transverse margin of gluteus maximus at the level of the tuberosity is divided transversely 3–4 cm. Bony release begins at the tuberosity subperiosteally by separating biceps femoris (long head), semitendinosus, and semimembranosus.

Release of the ischial ramus from its medial side is the risky step: the internal pudendal vessels and the pudendal nerve run here in the pudendal (Alcock's) canal along the medial surface of obturator internus. The hazard is mitigated by strictly subperiosteal release of ischiocavernosus and transversus perinei. Deep in the wound the fibres of obturator internus are identified, drawn upward and medially to expose the lower-inner edge of the obturator foramen. Subperiosteal release continues posteriorly and superiorly to the ischial spine or just above it. Cutting with Gigli saw or osteotome is performed first inferiorly and then superiorly; the bone is grasped with bone forceps and lifted out after release from the obturator membrane. Layered closure with drain as needed.

## Resection of the Iliac Wing

Indications: tumours of the iliac wing of limited size (benign osteochondroma, aneurysmal bone cyst, low-grade malignant). The patient is supine with the relevant pelvic side gently elevated. The skin incision runs over the iliac crest, starting at the PSIS and curving anteriorly toward the ASIS, with distal extension over tensor fasciae latae as needed. Iliacus is reflected from the inner surface and the glutei from the outer, both subperiosteally. Wing resection may be economical (sparing the iliac crest as an apophyseal structure with attached muscles) or extensive (extending to the greater sciatic notch).

If the wing must be resected as far as the greater sciatic notch, the pelvic ring is disrupted — a substantial biomechanical compromise requiring posterior reconstruction in two-level resections. In this scenario particular care is required for the superior gluteal artery, which emerges below the upper margin of the greater sciatic notch covered by piriformis: iatrogenic avulsion retracts the vessel into the pelvis, producing a rapidly expanding haematoma that is technically demanding to control and often requires percutaneous embolization. Meticulous haemostasis is followed by a 24–48 hour drain and layered closure.

## Partial Sacrectomy (for Chordoma)

Sacral chordoma is the most common indication for partial sacrectomy in orthopaedic practice. Other indications include giant cell tumour and low-grade chondrosarcoma in the lower sacral segments. The operation is performed by a mixed team — orthopaedic/spine surgeon, general surgeon, and (with rectal involvement) colorectal surgeon. The patient is prone with the pelvis elevated by a bolster, or in Kraske position (prone with the legs hanging off the table — facilitating rectal manipulation and providing better ergonomics).

The classical Boychev technique uses a transverse arcuate incision over the midline of the sacrum, strongly convex superiorly, spanning the distance between the two gluteus maximus muscles. This incision has the advantage of a large distal flap sweeping down toward the anus — protecting the wound from contamination by the anal opening, important for patients with potential bacterial contamination. A midline longitudinal incision over the sacrum and coccyx is an alternative, but Boychev preferred the transverse incision for its lower infection rate.

The insertions of gluteus maximus into the sacrum are divided. The coccyx is freed from soft tissue, disarticulated, and removed. The rectum is carefully separated from the presacral tumour mass — a step where preoperative MRI assessment of rectal fascia invasion is critical. The sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments are divided bilaterally, as is coccygeus. The S4 and S5 roots are identified and divided bilaterally, preserving S2 and S3 (the pudendal nerve and parasympathetic innervation of the rectum and genitourinary functions).

Blunt dissection along the anterior surface of the sacrum with the tumour separates loose connective tissue down to the lower pole of both SI joints. The sacrum is divided with an osteotome between the 2nd and 3rd segments; the iliolumbar ligament and lateral sacral artery are divided. The resected bone with the tumour is removed en bloc.

High sacrectomies (above S3, sacrificing S2 bilaterally) entail severe neurological loss — pelvic stability is impossible without posterior reconstruction, and bladder and rectal incontinence are almost inevitable. The classical rule: preservation of at least one S2 root is critical for partial sphincteric function; preservation of both S3 roots is required for normal continence. For more extensive sacrectomies, pelvic reconstruction is performed with pedicle screws in L4–L5 linked to bilateral iliac screws (lumbopelvic fixation), and for very extensive resections a vascularised fibular graft between L5 and the ilium is added.

## Coccygectomy

Indications: persistent coccydynia following fracture or dislocation, refractory to 6 months of conservative care; coccygeal chordoma (rare). The patient is prone with a pelvic bolster or in Kraske position. A 10 cm longitudinal incision over the coccyx or a horseshoe (Boychev) incision convex superiorly is used — again to protect the wound from anal contamination. The coccyx is released from soft tissue (gluteus maximus, the anococcygeal ligament, levator ani medially, coccygei laterally) subperiosteally with a chisel and removed with its periosteum. Meticulous haemostasis; subcutaneous and skin closure. A small drain may be used at the surgeon's discretion.

## Interilioabdominal Resection (Internal Hemipelvectomy by Kocher)

Interilioabdominal resection (internal, limb-sparing hemipelvectomy) is the en bloc removal of the pubis, ischium, and ilium with the acetabulum and the proximal end of the femur, with the femoral stump positioned against the residual ilium to form a "flail hip" or a forced arthrodesis. The operation was first performed by the Swiss surgeon Theodor Kocher in 1884 for a chondrosarcoma of the ilium and described by him in 1897; it was subsequently performed by Roux and entered the classical European tumour-orthopaedic armamentarium. Indications: primary malignant bony tumours around the hip with low malignancy grade (chondrosarcoma G1–G2, low-grade osteosarcoma after chemotherapy, Ewing sarcoma after induction), where surgical radicality is achievable and the highly mutilating external interilioabdominal amputation (hemipelvectomy) can be avoided.

Preparation includes psychological preparation, full cardiovascular and renal assessment, and at least 2000–2500 ml of matched fresh blood (modern equivalent: ROTEM-guided transfusion protocol, cell saver, and proactive fibrinogen replacement — bleeding in pelvic tumour surgery remains substantial despite modern vascular technique). The patient is supine with provision for prone repositioning during the procedure.

The skin incision begins over the middle of the iliac crest, reaches the ASIS, and continues along the inguinal crease to the symphysis; it may cross the midline. The inguinal ligament is divided from ASIS to pubis. External and internal oblique are divided from the inner side of the iliac crest, and gluteus medius is divided externally with the insertion of tensor fasciae latae. The insertion of one or both recti abdomini with their sheaths is divided and reflected superiorly together with the inguinal ligament, with the spermatic cord retracted medially. This gives wide access to the pelvic viscera and neurovascular bundle.

The most demanding and critical step is mobilisation of the neurovascular bundle. Meticulous separation, ligation, and division of all branches from common iliac artery and vein down to the upper third of the femoral vessels is required; longitudinal incision of vessel adventitia eases the dissection. A rubber loop around the common iliac artery provides rapid haemorrhage control. The femoral nerve is dissected and looped. The iliopsoas is divided above the joint. The periosteum of the ilium is divided transversely well clear of the tumour, down to the greater sciatic notch. The symphysis is exposed by dividing the adductor insertions to the pubis. The obturator artery, vein, and nerve are divided as they enter the obturator membrane. A Gigli saw is passed through the greater sciatic notch, brought up over the iliac crest just anterior to the SI joint, and the ilium is divided. The inferior pubic ramus and ischial ramus are divided likewise.

The hip joint with the tumour and the surrounding soft tissues is removed en bloc. The proximal femur is positioned against the residual ilium and fixed with wire for a neoarthrosis or, for fusion, with cancellous screws and additional cancellous grafts harvested from the iliac crest remnant. The inguinal ligament is sutured to the adductor tendons and fascia lata; the oblique abdominal muscles are sutured to the proximal end of gluteus medius; drains are placed for 24–72 hours; spica plaster immobilization is applied. Boychev variant: if the tumour involves more of the iliac body or wing with extension into the acetabular floor, the entire ilium with the acetabulum, part of the superior pubic ramus, the body of the ischium, and the femoral head are resected; the residual femoral neck is positioned against the resected ends of pubis and ischium for bony union; in this type of resection the entire weight-bearing axis is transferred to the symphysis and the contralateral hemipelvis.

The modern equivalent of interilioabdominal resection is the periacetabular (Enneking type II) resection with saddle prosthesis (Mark), pelvic megaprosthesis (LUMiC, Stanmore), or modular periacetabular endoprosthesis. In all cases functional outcome is markedly better than external hemipelvectomy (limb preserved, ambulation with crutch or cane), at the cost of a longer operation, higher infection risk (15–30%), and late mechanical reconstruction failures.

## Sacroiliac Arthrodesis

Indications for SI arthrodesis in current practice are tuberculous SI joint disease, chronic fracture-dislocation with persistent pain beyond 1.5 years, degenerative sacroiliitis refractory to conservative care, and severe inflammatory sacroiliitis. The classical Boychev technique is a posterior approach with a curved skin incision from the midpoint of the iliac crest, curving down over the PSIS and continuing 5–7 cm parallel to the origin of gluteus maximus. Subcutaneous tissue and thoracolumbar fascia are divided; gluteus maximus and medius are reflected subperiosteally from the outer ilium; the superficial layer of thoracolumbar fascia is divided; erector spinae is reflected medially from the sacrum.

A rectangular bone block is cut from the ilium immediately over the joint, opening it. Articular cartilage is removed from both surfaces (iliac and sacral) down to cancellous bone, in which a trough is fashioned. The bone block is impacted cancellous-surface-in into the trough; cancellous chips are packed around it. Layered closure; plaster body cast immobilization shoulder-to-knee for 3 months. The contemporary standard is percutaneous or mini-open fixation with 1–2 cannulated screws in sacroiliac trajectories (S1 and/or S2) under fluoroscopic/3D navigation, providing comparable fusion rates (90–95%) with markedly reduced immobilization (6 weeks crutched ambulation) and surgical morbidity.

## Interilioabdominal Amputation (Hemipelvectomy)

Interilioabdominal amputation (hemipelvectomia) is the removal of the entire lower limb together with the corresponding half of the pelvis. First described by Charles Hawkins in 1851 and popularised as a systematic procedure by Pringle (1909) and Gordon-Taylor (1935). The indications are confined to the most severe scenarios: high-grade malignant tumours of the innominate or proximal femur where the limb cannot be salvaged (Ewing sarcoma with lumbosacral plexus invasion, osteosarcoma with extensive soft-tissue component, recurrence after prior internal hemipelvectomy), massive infections with limb gangrene, and catastrophic injuries with limb non-viability.

The approach is a three-part incision. Abdominal part: from the iliac crest along the inguinal crease to the symphysis; the inguinal ligament is divided; the external iliac artery and vein are isolated for high ligation (transfixion and double security) at the level of the common iliac vessels; the obturator artery, vein, and nerve are divided at the obturator membrane. A moist pack is left in place and attention turns to the perineal part. Perineal part: with the limb maximally abducted, the incision is continued from the symphysis down and laterally along the inferior pubic ramus and ischial ramus to the tuberosity; from the medial side the ischiocavernosus and transversus perinei superficialis are released subperiosteally; the symphyseal ligaments and fibrocartilage are divided with an osteotome, or the two pubic rami are divided with a Gigli saw 4–5 cm from the symphysis. Posterior part: the incision is continued posteriorly along the iliac crest to the PSIS, then turns sharply toward the greater trochanter and continues distally along the gluteal crease to the perineum, where it meets the perineal incision. The posterior and inferior margins of gluteus maximus are exposed, the aponeurosis is divided along the incision — forming a large skin-subcutaneous-muscular flap.

The superior and inferior gluteal vessels are isolated, ligated well proximally, and divided. Iliopsoas is divided at the SI joint level. A Gigli saw is passed through the greater sciatic notch, brought up over the iliac crest just anterior to the SI joint, and the ilium is divided. The sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments are divided, freeing the pelvic bones. Levator ani is divided close to its origin on the medial pubic surface, completely isolating the limb and its half of the pelvis. Closure is with a posterior (gluteal) myocutaneous flap (most common) or with an anterior (quadriceps) flap when posterior tumour involves the gluteal zone; drains for 48–72 hours. Complications are substantial: operative mortality 5–10% in contemporary series (higher in elderly and anaemic patients), flap necrosis 10–20%, deep infection 15–25%, phantom pain over 80%, and significant psychosocial sequelae with high rates of post-operative depression.

## Notes on Periacetabular and Pelvic Osteotomies

The classical periacetabular osteotomies for residual developmental hip dysplasia (DDH) are executed from the pelvic side and are systematically reviewed in Topic Ortho-1. From the pelvic anatomy perspective, the key concepts are: innominate osteotomy after Salter (1961) — a single transverse cut from the greater sciatic notch to the iliopectineal eminence, rotating the distal fragment anterolaterally around the symphysis as a hinge, yielding 15–25° of lateral coverage; ideal in children 18 months to 6 years. Pemberton osteotomy (1965) — incomplete (sparing posterior cortex) pericapsular osteotomy hinging on triradiate cartilage, yielding 25–35° of lateral coverage by remodelling the acetabular cavity itself; applicable until triradiate closure (around 10 years). Steel osteotomy (1973) — triple (ilium + superior pubic ramus + inferior ischial ramus), permitting full acetabular rotation in adolescents and young adults with mature or near-mature skeleton. Dega osteotomy — incomplete inferiorly-based osteotomy hinging on the posterior cortex, conceptually analogous to Pemberton and favoured in the Polish school. Chiari osteotomy — medially-displacing osteotomy above the joint capsule, forming a bony roof over the head as a joint replacement — a salvage in skeletally mature adults where PAO is unsuitable. Ganz periacetabular osteotomy (1988) — quadruple osteotomy (superior pubic ramus, inferior ischial ramus, inferior ilial ramus, posterior columnar cut), allowing full acetabular rotation while preserving the posterior pelvic ring — the modern gold standard in skeletally mature adults with symptomatic dysplasia.

From the pelvic anatomy perspective, all these osteotomies traverse three interrelated zones: the anterosuperior zone containing the femoral nerve within iliopsoas (at risk during retraction); the middle zone containing the obturator nerve at its emergence from the obturator foramen; and the posteroinferior zone containing the sciatic nerve at its emergence below piriformis. The Ganz PAO, the only one performed through a true pelvic approach (anterior part of the Smith-Petersen with infrapelvic osteotomy), demands intra-operative fluoroscopy to confirm that the posterior columnar cuts remain in the safe zone (at least 1 cm from the greater sciatic notch to protect the superior gluteal artery and sciatic nerve).

## Notes on Iliac Crest Bone Graft Harvesting

The iliac crest is the classical donor site for autograft in orthopaedics. The principal technical points: the incision lies along the crest, at least 2 cm posterior to the ASIS — to avoid the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (passing under the inguinal ligament 1–2 cm medial to ASIS, injury → meralgia paresthetica) and the ilioinguinal nerve. With an anterior approach, a tricortical block (for reconstructive pelvic or spinal procedures) or cortico-cancellous chips may be harvested; with a posterior approach (for spinal fusion), the incision runs along the crest from the PSIS, protecting the superior cluneal nerves (crossing the crest approximately 7–8 cm lateral to midline). Resection depth is limited to about 2 cm below the crest surface, preserving the pelvic ring and avoiding breach into the lesser pelvis; the inner table harvest is typically more problematic owing to iliacus and the neurovascular structures. Harvest-site complications: chronic donor-site pain (5–25%, the most common), meralgia paresthetica, ASIS fracture, herniation through the crest defect (rare but reported), and haematoma.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

The pelvic surgical anatomy is dominated by the complex relationships among the bony pelvis, the iliac vessels and their branches (including the dangerous corona mortis anastomosis crossing the superior pubic ramus), the lumbosacral plexus (with the sciatic nerve exiting the greater sciatic notch typically inferior to the piriformis and the femoral nerve within the iliopsoas), the pelvic viscera (bladder, rectum), and the abdominal wall. The ilioinguinal approach (Letournel) uses three windows (lateral around the iliopsoas, middle between iliopsoas and iliac vessels, medial between iliac vessels and spermatic cord/rectus) to provide comprehensive anterior access to the iliac wing, pelvic brim, and anterior column of the acetabulum. The modified Stoppa (anterior intrapelvic) approach provides similar access through a transverse suprapubic incision and the preperitoneal space, with better visualization of the quadrilateral surface and reduced morbidity. The Kocher-Langenbeck (posterior) approach through gluteus maximus split provides access to the posterior wall, posterior column, and ischial region, with the sciatic nerve below the piriformis being the principal neurological concern. The extended iliofemoral approach provides the most comprehensive single-incision access but is largely abandoned because of high morbidity (especially heterotopic ossification). The Pfannenstiel approach provides direct access to the symphysis pubis for anterior pelvic ring stabilization. The principal neurovascular structures at risk in pelvic surgery include the iliac vessels (particularly the corona mortis and the left common iliac vein), the sciatic nerve (in posterior approaches), the femoral nerve (within the iliopsoas in anterior approaches), the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (beneath the inguinal ligament, with meralgia paresthetica a common complication), the obturator nerve (in the obturator foramen), the superior gluteal nerve (between the gluteus medius and minimus), and the pudendal nerve (around the ischial spine). The bladder and rectum are the visceral structures requiring protection.

From the classical Bulgarian and Soviet schools of pelvic surgery, the points worth remembering are: the topographic concept of greater and lesser pelvis separated by the linea terminalis with the internal muscles (iliopsoas, piriformis, obturator internus) passing through pelvic foramina; the two-stage Zatsepin combined approach (anterior perineal plus posterior gluteal) for extensive innominate-bone tumours, in which sparing dissection of gluteus maximus at the upper pole is critical to preserve the inferior gluteal vessels and nerve; the Berglezov modified posterior approach for ischial tumours involving the posterior acetabular wall, where subperiosteal release of gluteus maximus and looping the sciatic nerve are key; the posterior SI joint approach with a rectangular bone block over the joint; the Selig-Stoffel intrapelvic obturator neurectomy for severe adductor contracture, with the extrapelvic Stoffel alternative and the selective anterior-branch resection after Adams; classical symphyseal fixation for traumatic diastasis; resections of the pubis, ischium, and ilium through the classical perineal and iliac approaches; partial sacrectomy for chordoma with the transverse arcuate Boychev incision and the inviolable rule of S3 root preservation; coccygectomy with the Boychev horseshoe incision; interilioabdominal resection (internal hemipelvectomy) after Kocher (1884/1897) with the Roux and Boychev variants and reconstruction as flail hip or forced arthrodesis — conceptual predecessor of the modern saddle prosthesis and pelvic megaprosthesis; classical SI arthrodesis after Boychev with rectangular bone block, superseded in current practice by percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation; and interilioabdominal amputation (hemipelvectomy) as the last-line indication systematically presented in the Boychev textbook. This classical foundation remains relevant both for understanding the evolution of pelvic orthopaedic surgery and for daily resolution of revision and non-standard clinical scenarios.

The chapter that follows turns to the hip, where the principles of pelvic anatomy continue and the head/acetabular blood supply considerations introduce additional surgical concerns.
