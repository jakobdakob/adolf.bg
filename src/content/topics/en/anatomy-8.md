## Introduction

The hip joint, the deepest and most constrained ball-and-socket articulation in the body, presents the orthopedic surgeon with multiple operative approaches, each with characteristic advantages, disadvantages, and risks. The approaches range from the anterior (Smith-Petersen) with its claim to muscle-sparing access, through the anterolateral (Watson-Jones) and direct lateral (Hardinge) approaches that balance access with abductor preservation, to the workhorse posterior approach with its complete exposure but greater dislocation risk, and the trochanteric flip osteotomy (Ganz) with its access to the entire femoral head while preserving the vascular supply. The fundamental anatomical consideration governing approach selection is the blood supply to the femoral head through the medial femoral circumflex artery and its retinacular branches — disruption of these vessels produces avascular necrosis, a substantial concern in fracture management and increasingly in joint-preserving surgery. This chapter draws on Orthopaedic Surgical Approaches, Operative Hip Arthroscopy (Byrd), Netter's Concise Orthopaedic Anatomy, Gray's Anatomy, and the classical Bulgarian "Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology" by Boycho Boychev — eponymous source of the University Specialized Hospital for Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya — augmented with the classical procedures for hip joint pathology by Smith-Petersen, Watson-Jones, Hardinge, Kocher–Langenbeck, Gibson, Ganz, Hueter, Olier, Ombrédanne, Colonna, Marino-Zuco, Hass, Moore, Osborne, Langenbeck, Charnley, Müller, McKee–Farrar, Thompson, Moore-Austin, Ring, Wagner, Sivash, Gerchev, Pauwels, McMurray, Schanz, Lorenz, Kirmisson, Schanz–Bayer, Imhäuser, Sugioka, Salter, Pemberton, Chiari, Steel, Dega, Zahradníček, Voss, Brittain, Trumble, Hibbs, Albee, Ghormley, Lange, Spitzy, Chaklin, Vreden, Balchev, Holevich, Dzherov, Vladimirov, Tenev, Zhelev, Iliev, Ivanov, Kozhuharov, and Ilizarov — techniques that remain an integral part of the orthopaedic curriculum in Bulgaria and of daily practice at the USBALO "Prof. Boycho Boychev".

The hip joint comprises the femoral head (covered by approximately 70 percent articular cartilage in the typical adult) articulating with the acetabulum (the cup formed by the fused ilium, ischium, and pubis). The detailed anatomy was addressed in Topics Trauma-20 (acetabulum) and Orth-7 (DDH). Key surgical landmarks include: Greater trochanter: The principal lateral landmark, insertion of the gluteus medius (lateral facet), gluteus minimus (anterior facet), piriformis (superior facet), and other short rotators. Lesser trochanter: Medial; insertion of the iliopsoas. Femoral head and neck: With the typical neck-shaft angle of 130 degrees and anteversion of 10 to 15 degrees. Intertrochanteric line (anteriorly) and intertrochanteric crest (posteriorly): The hip capsule attaches along these lines. ASIS and AIIS: The anterior landmarks for anterior approaches. The hip capsule is a strong fibrous structure with thickenings forming the iliofemoral (Y-ligament of Bigelow), pubofemoral, and ischiofemoral ligaments. The iliofemoral ligament is the strongest ligament in the body and the principal anterior restraint.

## Topography — Anterior Femoral and Gluteal Regions

The classical Bulgarian and Russian orthopaedic schools describe the hip joint as lying in two topographical regions — anterior femoral and gluteal — with detailed external landmarks used for approach planning and interpretation of palpatory findings. The external landmarks are the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), the pubic tubercle, the apex of the greater trochanter, and the ischial tuberosity. On the anterior aspect of the thigh, the most superior point of the femoral head lies at the midpoint between ASIS and pubic tubercle (Lubotsky, 1953). The line connecting that midpoint with the base of the greater trochanter averages 11 cm and divides the head and neck into two near-equal parts — useful in planning percutaneous neck instrumentation.

The gluteal region is bounded superiorly by the iliac crest, medially by the midline to the apex of the coccyx, inferiorly by the gluteal fold, and laterally by the line between the greater trochanter and the ASIS. Skin is thick with abundant sebaceous glands; the subcutaneous tissue is divided into lobules by fibrous septa and forms the corpus adiposum lumbo-iliacum over gluteus maximus and the corpus adiposum gluteofemorale distal to the trochanter — the vascular network of these fat pads sustains the skin flap during hip disarticulation. Cutaneous innervation derives from nn. clunium superiores (posterior rami of L1–L3) superiorly, nn. clunium medii (S1–S3) medially, and nn. clunium inferiores (from n. cutaneus femoris posterior) inferolaterally.

The deep neurovascular structures of the gluteal region are distributed between two connective-tissue planes — the superficial intergluteal space (between gluteus maximus and gluteus medius plus the external rotators), through which the contents of the infrapiriform foramen pass (medial to lateral: pudendal nerve, internal pudendal vessels, sciatic nerve with posterior femoral cutaneous nerve), and the deep intergluteal space (between gluteus medius and gluteus minimus) containing the superior gluteal vessels and nerve emerging from the suprapiriform foramen. The superior gluteal nerve crosses transversely between gluteus medius and TFL, entering the latter 4–6 cm below the ASIS in the middle third of its muscle belly — an anatomical constant defining the safe proximal extent of any lateral abductor split. The sciatic nerve courses medial to the midpoint between ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter along the posterior surface of obturator internus, the gemelli, and quadratus femoris.

## Tendon Insertions, Bursae, and Spaces

Clinically the surgeon must recognise the silhouette of the greater trochanter with its three facets: lateral facet — gluteus medius; anterior facet — gluteus minimus; superior facet — piriformis tendon. The lesser trochanter receives iliopsoas. Distal to the trochanter along the intertrochanteric crest lies quadratus femoris, and deeper still obturator externus, whose tendon slings hammock-fashion along the posteroinferior aspect of the neck and is the critical anatomic guarantee for preserving the retinacular branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery during the Ganz flip.

A constellation of synovial bursae surrounds the joint and must be recognised as pain sources and as orientation landmarks: bursa trochanterica m. glutei maximi (between the gluteus maximus tendon and the lateral trochanter — the most common source of trochanteric syndrome), bursa trochanterica superficialis (subcutaneous over the trochanter apex), bursa trochanterica m. glutei medii (between the gluteus medius tendon and the superior facet), bursa m. piriformis (deep to the tendon before its insertion), bursa m. obturatorii interni medialis (between the muscle and lesser sciatic notch — almost regularly communicates with the joint cavity), bursa ischiadica m. glutei maximi (between gluteus maximus and ischial tuberosity), and the iliopectineal bursa (between iliopsoas and iliopectineal eminence — in approximately 15% of specimens communicates with the hip joint cavity, accounting for bidirectional spread of infection and effusion).

## Blood Supply to the Femoral Head

The blood supply to the femoral head is the central anatomical concern of hip surgery:

Medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA): A branch of the deep femoral artery. The principal supply to the femoral head (approximately 70 to 80 percent in most specimens). Courses posteriorly around the femoral neck and gives off retinacular branches that ascend within the capsular reflection along the posterior aspect of the femoral neck to enter the femoral head near the cartilage-bone junction. Surgical approaches that disrupt these retinacular vessels (particularly the posterior approach with disruption of the obturator internus and gemelli insertion) produce AVN risk. Lateral femoral circumflex artery (LFCA): Provides smaller anterior contribution. Artery of the ligamentum teres: A branch of the obturator artery (variable). Of importance in children but generally inadequate as the sole blood supply in adults. The Ganz trochanteric flip osteotomy preserves the deep branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery by maintaining the obturator externus insertion and the posterior capsular reflection, providing safe surgical hip dislocation without AVN.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The medial femoral circumflex artery (deep branch) supplies 70-80% of the femoral head via retinacular vessels ascending along the posterior femoral neck. The Ganz trochanteric flip protects this supply by keeping the obturator externus insertion intact — this is the anatomic basis for safe surgical hip dislocation without AVN.</p>
</aside>

## Muscular Anatomy

The principal hip muscles include: Anterior compartment: Iliopsoas (hip flexion), rectus femoris (hip flexion, knee extension), sartorius (hip flexion, knee flexion), pectineus. Lateral compartment: Gluteus medius and minimus (abduction, internal rotation in flexion), tensor fasciae latae. Posterior compartment: Gluteus maximus (extension, external rotation), piriformis (external rotation), short external rotators (gemelli, obturator internus and externus, quadratus femoris). Medial compartment: Adductor longus, brevis, magnus; gracilis. The gluteus medius and minimus are critical for normal gait (Trendelenburg gait develops with their dysfunction); their preservation is paramount in any approach.

## Classification of Operative Approaches

The classical Bulgarian school divides hip approaches into five groups by direction of entry and anatomical interval: anterior (between sartorius and TFL, or between iliopsoas and rectus femoris), lateral (between TFL and gluteus medius, with or without trochanteric osteotomy), posterior (through gluteus maximus, with or without abductor detachment), medial (between the anterior and adductor groups, or between the adductors), and combined — anterolateral or posterolateral. The choice depends on the pathology (joint-preserving, joint-replacing, fracture, tumour, infection, DDH-reparative), soft-tissue quality, the need for full circumferential capsular exposure versus selective quadrant exposure, and the surgeon's familiarity.

## Anterior Approach Along the Anterior Border of Sartorius

The anterior approach along the anterior border of sartorius (also called the "vertical anterior approach") uses the medial border of the muscle with a 15–17 cm incision from ASIS distally and medially. After incising the subcutaneous tissue and fascia, the interval between rectus femoris (retracted laterally) and iliopsoas (retracted medially) is developed, exposing the anterior capsule. The anatomical caveats that make this approach suboptimal are the femoral nerve passing in the groove between psoas and iliacus with motor branches to the anterior compartment, the femoral artery along the medial border of iliopsoas and medial to sartorius, and the origin of the medial femoral circumflex artery 7–8 cm distal to the inguinal ligament — branches of which cross the operative field. Because of these risks to the femoral neurovascular bundle, the approach is no longer recommended routinely and has been replaced by the Hueter posterior-border-of-sartorius approach or by the true Smith-Petersen interval between sartorius and TFL.

## Hueter Anterior Approach

The Hueter approach uses the posterior border of sartorius — the skin incision begins at the ASIS and descends along the lateral border of the muscle for about 15 cm. The interval between sartorius (medially) and TFL (laterally) is developed, and proximally the TFL is detached osteoplastically from the ASIS and from gluteus medius 1 cm below the iliac crest. With a periosteal elevator, gluteus medius and minimus are reflected off the lateral surface of the iliac wing and retracted posteriorly and inferiorly, exposing the superior and anterior aspects of the joint capsule broadly. Hueter is the conceptual predecessor of the modern direct anterior approach (DAA) — the difference being that Hueter includes detachment of the TFL and the abductors from the iliac wing, whereas DAA preserves the TFL as a muscular wall.

## Indications

The approach has historically been used for hip arthrodesis, open reduction of femoral head fractures, biopsy, tumor procedures, and pelvic osteotomies. In recent years, it has gained substantial popularity as the direct anterior approach (DAA) to total hip arthroplasty with reduced soft-tissue disruption and lower dislocation rates.

Patient supine, often on a specialized table (Hana table) with traction. Incision from approximately 2 cm distal to the ASIS, extending distally for 8 to 15 cm in the line between the sartorius and the tensor fasciae latae. ## Complications

Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury (meralgia paresthetica) is common (20 to 80 percent transient, 5 to 10 percent persistent). Wound complications at the proximal incision (with creases and skin folds). Femoral fracture during stem insertion (more common in DAA than in posterior approach because of the more difficult femoral exposure). Stem malposition because of more limited visualization of the proximal femur.

## Lateral Approaches — Olier, Ombrédanne, and Colonna

Before the modern muscle-sparing intervals became standard, orthopaedics relied on wide lateral approaches with trochanteric osteotomy for broad circumferential exposure. The Olier approach has a U-shape around the greater trochanter apex: gluteus maximus is retracted posteriorly, TFL anteriorly, and a blunt curved instrument is passed beneath gluteus medius and minimus, under which the trochanter is osteotomised with an osteotome. The external rotators remain attached to the femur or are divided as needed; the osteomuscular flap is reflected proximally to expose the capsule. On reconstruction the trochanter is fixed with a screw or wire. In children with an actively growing trochanter, the abductor tendons are divided parallel to the trochanteric apex and reinserted at closure. McFarland and Osborne modified the approach by transecting the trochanter intracapsularly with a Gigli saw after anterior and posterior arthrotomy — a technique that elevates the entire superior capsule together with the osteomuscular flap, including the short external rotators.

The Ombrédanne approach uses a curved incision between TFL and gluteus medius with a stepwise trochanteric osteotomy and proximal reflection of the osteomuscular flap. It affords wide access to the joint and proximal third of the femur and is particularly suited to DDH with a high-riding femoral head, proximal femoral tumour resection, and severe joint destruction requiring wide exposure. The Colonna approach uses a reverse-C incision with anterior convexity, beginning about 3 cm posterior to the ASIS and curving distally to 12 cm below the trochanter, followed by detachment of the abductor terminal tendons. The Colonna approach is historically tied to his described capsuloplasty for high dislocations (see DDH section).

## Watson-Jones Anterolateral Approach

The Watson-Jones approach uses the interval between the gluteus medius posteriorly and the tensor fasciae latae anteriorly, with reflection of the gluteus medius from the anterior aspect of the greater trochanter.

## Indications

Total hip arthroplasty, open reduction internal fixation of certain proximal femur fractures, hemiarthroplasty.

## Technique

Patient supine or lateral decubitus. Incision over the greater trochanter, extending proximally and distally for the desired length. The fascia lata is incised. The interval between gluteus medius and tensor fasciae latae is developed. The anterior portion of the gluteus medius is partially detached from the trochanter (with later repair). The hip capsule is opened anteriorly.


## Complications

Abductor dysfunction with partial detachment of the gluteus medius; meticulous repair is essential. Superior gluteal nerve injury with deeper proximal dissection.

## Direct Lateral Approach (Hardinge)

The Hardinge approach splits the gluteus medius in line with its fibers, with anterior reflection of the anterior portion (including the gluteus minimus) attached to a flap of the vastus lateralis. The approach provides good anterior exposure of the hip joint while preserving the posterior capsule and structures.

## Indications

Total hip arthroplasty, hemiarthroplasty, femoral neck fracture fixation.

## Technique

Patient supine or lateral decubitus. Longitudinal lateral incision over the trochanter. Fascia lata opened. Gluteus medius split in line with its fibers (only as far proximal as 5 cm from the trochanter to protect the superior gluteal nerve). The anterior portion of the gluteus medius (with the gluteus minimus) is reflected anteriorly as a single flap, often in continuity with the vastus lateralis. The hip capsule is opened anteriorly.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>On the Hardinge direct lateral approach, never split the gluteus medius more than 5 cm proximal to the greater trochanter — beyond that the superior gluteal nerve (between gluteus medius and minimus) is denervated and persistent abductor weakness with Trendelenburg gait follows.</p>
</aside>

## Complications

Superior gluteal nerve injury with extended proximal dissection (limiting the abductor split to 5 cm from the trochanter is critical). Abductor weakness even with appropriate technique; trochanteric pain and persistent limp are recognized concerns.

## Classical Posterior Approaches — Langenbeck, Osborne, Moore, Zhelev

The historical Langenbeck posterior approach passes directly through gluteus maximus without trochanteric osteotomy and without abductor detachment. The skin incision is made with the hip flexed about 40°, forming a straight line along the femoral axis from just distal to the PSIS toward the trochanter apex and ending 4–5 cm below the base; on hip extension it becomes curved. The descending branch of the inferior gluteal artery runs medial to the midpoint between ischial tuberosity and trochanter, dividing into transverse branches that enter gluteus maximus perpendicular to the muscle fibres — a fact dictating blunt splitting (rather than sharp transection) of the muscle to avoid significant bleeding.

The Osborne approach differs only in skin incision and the level of muscle splitting: the incision starts 4–5 cm distal and lateral to the PSIS, runs parallel to the muscle fibres toward the superoposterior corner of the trochanter, and then curves distally for about 5 cm. The Moore (Southern posterior) approach starts about 10 cm distal to the PSIS, runs parallel to the muscle fibres toward the posterior border of the trochanter, and descends 10–12 cm parallel to the femoral shaft. When fascia lata is incised the gluteus maximus aponeurosis splitting into tractus iliotibialis is divided, and the terminal tendon is partially detached from the gluteal tuberosity to ease retraction. Moore allows wider exposure of the posterior acetabular rim and more medial structures than Osborne. In both approaches the sciatic nerve must be identified and protected.

The Zhelev approach (Bulgarian school) combines a posterior skin incision shaped like an inverted question mark — the upper arm starts behind the ASIS, curves down and back at the midpoint between the trochanter and iliac crest, sweeps around the posterior trochanter, and continues along the lateral thigh. After releasing the gluteus maximus insertion and reflecting it medially and superiorly, the posterior border of gluteus medius, the external rotators, and the sciatic nerve are exposed; after rotator release and anterior retraction of gluteus medius, the joint together with the posterior column and sciatic nerve are fully exposed — particularly valuable for combined work on the posterior column and the hip (fracture-dislocations with posterior column involvement).

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>When splitting gluteus maximus in the posterior approaches (Langenbeck, Osborne, Moore), divide the fibres bluntly along their course rather than sharply: branches of the inferior gluteal artery cross the muscle perpendicularly from medial to lateral, and sharp transverse cuts produce bleeding that is difficult to control.</p>
</aside>

## Combined Approaches — Marino-Zuco, Chaklin–Vreden, Kocher–Gibson, McFarland–Osborne

The anterolateral Marino-Zuco approach starts at the ASIS heading toward the greater trochanter and continues 10–15 cm down the anterolateral thigh. The interval between TFL and sartorius is developed, the TFL origin is detached osteoplastically, and the middle third of the muscle is crossed carefully to avoid the superior gluteal nerve. The Chaklin–Vreden combined anterolateral approach with trochanteric osteotomy starts at the middle third of the iliac crest, runs along the crest to the ASIS, then descends to the trochanter and curves posteriorly across the subtrochanteric region. The interval between TFL and sartorius is developed, the TFL origin is divided, and the anterior portion of gluteus medius is reflected; the trochanter is osteotomised for wide exposure. Vreden described a variant omitting the proximal iliac-crest portion and the muscle detachment from the iliac wing — a compromise between exposure and soft-tissue preservation.

The posterolateral Kocher–Gibson approach starts 6–7 cm anterior to the PSIS, runs anteriorly and inferiorly over the anterior border of gluteus maximus to the anterior trochanteric border, and continues 15–17 cm along the femoral axis. The tractus iliotibialis is split longitudinally and then either the abductor terminal tendons are partially divided or osteotomically detached, or the external rotators are released depending on procedure needs. The McFarland–Osborne approach uses a lateral incision over the trochanter with division of the medius/minimus insertions and external rotator release after stay-suture marking, exposing the posterior capsule — particularly suited to revision arthroplasty in difficult cases with prior joint blocking and where trochanteric release is needed to balance an abductor contracture.

## Medial Approach (Ludloff)

The Ludloff medial approach (K. Ludloff, 1908) uses the interval between the anterior and adductor groups of the thigh with the hip maximally abducted and flexed. The patient is supine with the knees flexed; a longitudinal medial-thigh incision starts about 2 cm distal to the pubic tubercle and runs between gracilis and adductor longus. Deeply, the dissection passes between adductor longus and pectineus (variantly per Stevens, between adductor magnus and adductor brevis) to reach the medial and inferior capsule. Anatomical caveat: the anterior branches of the obturator vessels and nerve emerging from the obturator foramen along the superior border of adductor brevis must be identified and protected. The Ludloff approach remains the accepted method for closed reduction with open tenotomy of dysplasia of the hip in small children (see DDH section) and for removal of medial loose bodies and tumours of the medial joint wall.

The posterior approach is the workhorse for total hip arthroplasty in many centers and the standard for posterior pelvic and acetabular surgery (the Kocher-Langenbeck variant addressed in [Topic Anatomy-7](/en/anatomy/7/)).


## Indications

Total hip arthroplasty, hemiarthroplasty, open reduction of posterior hip dislocations, posterior wall and posterior column acetabular fracture fixation, sciatic nerve exploration.

## Technique

Patient in lateral decubitus with the affected side up. Curved incision from the PSIS region, over the greater trochanter, extending distally into the proximal thigh. Fascia lata and gluteus maximus aponeurosis opened. Gluteus maximus split in line with its fibers (biased to avoid the inferior gluteal nerve). The short external rotators (piriformis, gemelli, obturator internus, quadratus femoris) are identified at their trochanteric insertions; the sciatic nerve is identified inferior to the piriformis. The short external rotators are detached from the trochanter and reflected medially to expose the posterior capsule. The capsule is opened and the joint is accessed.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>On the posterior hip approach the sciatic nerve must be identified inferior to piriformis before detaching the short external rotators; injury runs 1-3% in elective THA and higher in posterior dislocation reduction. Meticulous capsule and short-rotator repair at closure is essential to bring posterior THA dislocation rates down from the historical 3-5%.</p>
</aside>

## Complications

Sciatic nerve injury (1 to 3 percent in elective procedures, higher in posterior dislocation reduction). Posterior hip dislocation after total hip arthroplasty (the principal concern; historical rates of 3 to 5 percent, reduced by careful soft-tissue repair and modern implant positioning). Heterotopic ossification. Abductor preservation (an advantage of this approach, the gluteus medius and minimus are not disturbed).

## Trochanteric Flip Osteotomy (Ganz)

The Ganz trochanteric flip osteotomy (Ganz, 2001) provides comprehensive access to the entire femoral head and acetabulum with preservation of the medial femoral circumflex artery and consequently of the femoral head blood supply.

## Technique

Patient in lateral decubitus. Posterior incision similar to the Kocher-Langenbeck. The greater trochanter is osteotomized (typically with a stepwise cut) and reflected anteriorly with the attached gluteus medius and minimus. The capsular reflection containing the medial femoral circumflex artery is preserved by keeping the obturator externus insertion intact. The hip capsule is opened (typically Z-shaped or H-shaped), and the femoral head is dislocated anteriorly out of the acetabulum.

## Indications

Femoral head fracture fixation, FAI surgery, femoral head osteochondritis dissecans treatment, Perthes residual deformity correction, tumor resection of the femoral head, comprehensive surgical hip dislocation procedures.

## Complications

Trochanteric nonunion (1 to 5 percent), heterotopic ossification, abductor weakness.

## Tenotomies and Tenocapsulotomies Around the Hip

The classical Bulgarian and Russian schools describe an extensive repertoire of soft-tissue procedures — bursectomies, tenotomies, myotomies, aponeurotomies, and lengthenings — which either stand alone or prepare for reconstruction and remain useful in modern practice, particularly in cerebral palsy, snapping hip, and preparation for arthroplasty in a rigid contracture.

Bursectomy and bursal drainage around the hip are indicated for infection (banal or specific) or chronic inflammation with calcifications resistant to conservative therapy. The intermuscular gluteal bursa is best reached through a posterior approach; the iliopectineal bursa (between iliopsoas and iliopectineal eminence) is opened after sartorius release with medial retraction of the femoral neurovascular bundle, exposing the iliopsoas tendon with hip external rotation and extension. Subcutaneous tenotomy of the superficial flexors (sartorius and TFL) is performed with a tenotome near the ASIS for an isolated flexion contracture; the limb is immobilised in extension.

Adductor tenotomy (longus, brevis, partially magnus, gracilis) addresses adductor contracture from spasm or fixed shortening (cerebral palsy, fixed adduction in coxarthrosis). The subcutaneous technique uses a small transverse incision near the adductor longus insertion on the pubis; the open technique uses a 10 cm longitudinal incision along the tendon with muscle release close to the bone. Anterior-branch obturator neurectomy (between adductor longus and brevis) is often added in spastic CP — the distal variant described by Stoffel.

Beck's iliopsoas tenotomy for flexor contracture (most commonly in CP) uses a Hueter-type anterior incision with a slight medial distal extension; the origins of TFL, sartorius, and rectus femoris are divided, the iliopsoas tendon is identified and divided with a curved instrument near the lesser trochanter. The released tendon is reflected proximally and sutured along the anterolateral capsule for partial functional preservation.

Durham's myotomy of the internal rotators (the anterior portions of gluteus medius and minimus and TFL, most commonly in CP) uses an oblique incision over the superoanterior trochanter; the fascia lata and the tendinous extension of TFL are divided transversely with the limb in external rotation, followed by release of the anterior portions of the gluteal insertions. For persistent contracture iliopsoas can also be released and the anterior capsule opened. If the ASIS prominence prevents closure, a portion of the iliac wing is resected (Campbell, 1980).

## Voss Hanging Hip Procedure

The Voss "hanging hip" decompressive arthroplasty is a multistep soft-tissue release around the hip for patients with advanced essential or secondary coxarthrosis in whom THA is contraindicated (severe medical comorbidity, local contraindications) and for whom arthrodesis is unsuitable (mobility required). Requirements: flexion preserved above 70°, moderately reduced weight-bearing surface, and relatively congruent surfaces. Concurrent contractures are corrected unless bony incongruity is the cause. The classical technique includes: adductor detachment (open or subcutaneous); abductor and external rotator detachment via a posterolateral incision from the trochanter apex 10 cm distally; division of the rectus femoris articular head; with adductor contracture, additional iliopsoas detachment. Gluteus maximus is never divided — isolated transection causes significant gait instability, and extensor contracture in coxarthrosis is virtually unseen. Brand's modification adds selective posterior capsulotomy only with preserved head-acetabulum congruity. Outcomes show rare bony union, but most joints become painless and stable with limited flexion-extension motion — the so-called fibrous ankylosis.

## Muscle Transpositions for Flaccid Paralysis

Muscle transpositions around the hip are mainly indicated for residual polio. Prerequisites are absence of joint contracture, optimal muscle placement on the shortest functional vector, and sufficient muscle strength.

For flexor paralysis, Sauter's obliquus externus abdominis transposition uses a tongue-shaped myoaponeurotic flap based at the anterior axillary line, separated from the internal oblique, and passed through a subcutaneous tunnel to be sutured to the iliopsoas tendon under moderate tension with the hip flexed about 50°. Immobilisation in a hip-spica at 50° flexion for 4 weeks.

For gluteus maximus paralysis (extensors): the Ober technique mobilises erector spinae paravertebrally and attaches double fascia-lata strips to the greater trochanter via subcutaneous tunnel; Lange's modification sutures double fascia-lata strips to the distal erector spinae and fixes them to the trochanter via a bone tunnel. Spira's technique converts the knee flexors (biceps and semitendinosus) from biarticular to monoarticular muscles by suturing them to gluteus maximus.

For abductor paralysis (gluteus medius and minimus) — the most unsolved problem because no single muscle can replace the gluteal abductors and many described techniques give poor functional results in practice. Lange's technique detaches vastus lateralis from the trochanter base and passes two fascia-lata strips through four bone canals in the middle third of the iliac crest along fan-shaped subcutaneous tunnels, partially restoring abductor function; immobilisation in maximal abduction for 6 weeks. Thomas–Thompson–Straub's technique transposes obliquus externus for abduction. Berthon's technique partially uses gluteus maximus combined with TFL. Holevich's technique transposes TFL on a neurovascular pedicle, preserving its blood supply and innervation through careful tissue dissection and relocation into an iliac-crest groove. Ober–Barr's classical erector spinae transposition uses two-thirds of the muscle released from its vertebral and iliac/sacral insertions with preserved supply and innervation — a fascial strip passes beneath fascia iliaca and is fixed to the trochanter. Chaklin modified Ober with a smaller posterior incision and partial TFL mobilisation together with a strip of tractus iliotibialis. Mustard's iliopsoas transposition for active abduction and Sharrard's iliopsoas transposition through the iliac crest to the posterolateral trochanter offer biomechanically sound abductor substitutes in CP and polio — particularly applicable to paralytic dislocation combined with varus femoral osteotomy.

For paralytic pelvic obliquity (oblique pelvic position from unilateral pelvitrochanteric weakness), the Holevich–Dzherov–Vladimirov lavsanodesis uses a lavsan tape passed through two iliac-crest canals and a hole in the lower trochanter, with crossed weaving through gluteus maximus — generates 14–16 kg compressive force at standard tightening and avoids prolonged immobilisation. Analogous lavsanodesis for hip extensor paralysis per Vladimirov–Holevich runs paravertebrally beneath erector spinae and through a trochanteric bone canal with gluteus maximus crossing. Pelvic-obliquity lavsanodesis uses lavsan strips between the rib and the contralateral iliac wing.

## Snapping Hip

Snapping of musculotendinous elements over the greater trochanter (external coxa saltans) or within the inguinal canal (internal coxa saltans from iliopsoas over the iliopectineal eminence) causes pain and functional disturbance. Surgical treatment of the external type uses a curved longitudinal Watson-Jones-type incision; fibrous thickenings along the posterior border of gluteus maximus or the anterior fascial border of TFL are excised in stepwise fashion, the thickened posterior portion of tractus iliotibialis 10 cm below the trochanter is divided transversely, and a fascial Z-flap or duplication is formed and oversewn. With a widened superior trochanter border, part of the bone is resected. Early activation without immobilisation.

## Proximal Femoral Osteotomies

Proximal femoral osteotomies are classified by correction type (varus, valgus, rotational, flexion, extension, lengthening/shortening, three-dimensional), level (intertrochanteric, subtrochanteric, neck-level), fixation method (no internal fixation with cast, blade plate, dynamic hip screw, Holevich–Vladimirov compression blade), and nosology. The modern standard is intertrochanteric osteotomy with stable internal fixation allowing early motion without external immobilisation.

Varus intertrochanteric osteotomy is indicated in adult congenital subluxation (prophylactic for slowing coxarthrosis), coxa valga following Perthes disease with incongruity, paralytic dislocation for stabilisation, and in childhood with combined derotation for residual antetorsion after DDH treatment. The classical Pauwels technique removes a medial-based wedge from the distal fragment with the lateral apex angled to match the desired correction, followed by surface adaptation — at the cost of shortening. Alternatively, omitting the wedge and translating-adducting the distal fragment so the medial portion of the proximal fragment seats in the central distal cut reduces shortening. Fixation with a 90° AO blade plate after seating-chisel guide insertion (with instruments for precise neck orientation) gives stability that obviates postoperative casting and allows assisted standing from day 3. The Holevich–Vladimirov compression blade (three sizes — wide, medium, narrow) uses an adapting L-shaped jig and a compression bolt through a cortical channel of the distal fragment — generating compression between fragments without a rigid plate.

## Valgus Intertrochanteric Osteotomy

Valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy is indicated in coxa vara congenita (Bauer type) or acquired (malunited fractures, neck pseudarthroses, post-AVN deformity). In coxarthrosis it reduces Trendelenburg-derived limp and geometrically lengthens the limb, requiring active flexion to at least 60° and unrestricted adduction. With an AO blade plate, a K-wire reference is placed in the trochanter-neck with a planned angle to the shaft determined by subtracting the desired valgus from 120° (e.g., 40° valgus → 80° reference angle). For congenital coxa vara in a 4–5-year-old, the Holevich reverse blade plate with a predetermined lateral obtuse angle (complementary to the desired valgus — 150° for 30° valgus) provides stable fixation without casting. The correction goal is near-horizontalisation of the growth plate, with the postoperative angle to horizontal not exceeding 30°.

## Derotation Osteotomy

Derotation intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric osteotomy is indicated for excessive antetorsion (often after DDH treatment) with abnormal centering at neutral position. Correction is determined by serial AP X-rays at progressively increasing internal rotation. The classical Kirmisson technique and its Putti modification places two thick pins in the proximal fragment in the sagittal plane — at the neck and just above the lesser trochanter — and two more in the distal fragment, rotated internally by the planned derotation. A thin osteotome cuts the bone along a pre-perforated axis; derotation is performed until the pins are parallel. Holevich described an analogous technique but abandoned it because of pin loosening in paediatric metaphyseal bone. The modern paediatric standard uses intramedullary fixation with a short Künscher nail or a blade plate with the Holevich–Vladimirov compression blade — without a cast. Cardinal rule: derotation must never exceed the pre-existing active internal rotation — at least 10–15° of active IR must remain, or fixed external-rotation contracture follows.

## Flexion and Extension Osteotomies

Flexion and extension osteotomies are indicated for sagittal-plane malalignment (flexion contracture in CP or arthrogryposis, or fixed hyperextension). Correction is sagittal; fixation uses an AO blade plate at 130° or 90°. In flexion osteotomy the blade is seated in the trochanter such that the plate makes with the shaft an angle open anteriorly and distally, equal to the desired correction. In extension osteotomy, the angle opens posteriorly and distally.

## McMurray Osteotomy for Femoral Neck Pseudarthrosis

The McMurray osteotomy (T. P. McMurray, 1939) combines medialisation and valgisation of the distal fragment for femoral neck pseudarthrosis without AVN. The oblique cut starts below the trochanter base and runs medially and proximally to the neck base; the distal fragment is medialised by half the shaft diameter and abducted. The valgus correction depends on pseudarthrosis-line obliquity to horizontal — the steeper the line, the greater the valgus needed to convert shear into compression (Pauwels biomechanical principle). Fixation with an AO blade plate enables early motion; in McMurray's original cast immobilisation, iliopsoas pulled the proximal fragment into flexion with a posteriorly open angle — a problem that now mandates internal fixation.

## Three-Dimensional Imhäuser Correction for Coxa Vara Adolescentium

Adolescent epiphysiolysis (slipped capital femoral epiphysis, SCFE) in stable form with slip >5/10 of neck diameter requires correction in three planes — flexion, extension (to compensate retroversion), and derotation. The Imhäuser correction (Holevich–Dzherov modification) uses an oblique osteotomy with tri-axial fixation. For lesser slips (<5/10), stabilisation can be performed with a fan of 9–10 K-wires that provide relative stability and accelerate physeal closure. Postoperative: no cast, the limb in a foam splint at moderate abduction and slight flexion; standing on day 10 without loading; loading after radiological union at 2 months.

## Sugioka Transtrochanteric Rotational Osteotomy

Sugioka's transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy (Y. Sugioka, 1972) is indicated for early-stage AVN of the femoral head with preserved articular surface outside the necrotic zone. The principle is rotation of the head (anteriorly or posteriorly) such that the preserved viable portion bears in the acetabular weight-bearing zone while the necrotic portion rotates into the non-weight-bearing zone. The approach is an extended posterior or transtrochanteric flip; the critical condition is preservation of the medial femoral circumflex artery — Sugioka preserves obturator externus and the posterior capsular reflection in line with Ganz's principle. Correction is fixed with screws through the trochanter and neck. Results vary widely in the literature — the Japanese school reports favourable long-term outcomes, while Western series are more cautious. Method of choice in a young patient with focal necrosis outside the weight-bearing zone, Steinberg I–II.

## Hip Arthrodeses

Hip arthrodesis is indicated for severe unilateral joint destruction with marked motion restriction, for contracture with severe pain and failed prior surgery, and for limb stabilisation after flaccid paralysis of hip musculature. Best results in young patients (<30 years) with good spine mobility and intact adjacent joints (knee, contralateral hip, lumbar spine). Above 60 years, spinal stiffening and general condition make arthrodesis a relative contraindication. The functional position is 25–30° flexion, 0° (or slight 5°) adduction, and 5–10° external rotation.

Classification: I. Intra-articular (no metal fixation; non-compressive metal fixation; compressive metal fixation); II. Extra-articular (iliofemoral; ischiofemoral); III. Mixed; IV. Atypical and correcting arthrodeses.

## Closed Intra-articular Arthrodesis (Vreden)

Vreden's closed intra-articular arthrodesis is reached via a lateral trochanteric incision; vastus lateralis is split longitudinally and a guide wire is driven through trochanter, neck, and head (as in percutaneous neck-fracture fixation); a cylindrical drill creates a channel passing through the acetabulum, into which a tibial bone graft is impacted. Hip-spica immobilisation for 3 months, with extension as needed to union. Holevich's analogous closed intra-articular arthrodesis uses a Smith-Petersen anterior approach with capsular opening, partial decortication of head and acetabulum, and compression-screw fixation; indicated for hemiankylosis or delayed union after prior arthrodesis with proper functional limb position.

## Open Intra-articular Arthrodesis with Nail (Watson-Jones)

Watson-Jones arthrodesis (1937) uses an anterior iliofemoral approach with head dislocation, removal of articular cartilage from head and acetabulum, and reduction; through a lateral trochanteric incision a guide for a Smith-Petersen nail is inserted so that the nail traverses trochanter, neck, head, and upper thick acetabulum 2–3 cm into the pelvic cavity. Anterior autografts may be added along the superior neck. Hip-spica for 3–4 months. Charnley's open arthrodesis (the "central dislocation arthrodesis") inserts the head into a specially fashioned acetabular floor with Holmgren compression (introduced 1895) using a screw with bilateral nuts; many patients develop a stable painless fibrous ankylosis.

Holevich's compressive arthrodesis (Bulgarian school) uses dedicated instrumentation — nails of varying length (15–22 cm), a channel-centering jig, an 8 mm drill, and a tightener — to place two nails: the first through trochanter, neck, and head into the ilium; the second more proximally and crossed with the first — generating mean compression of 14–16 kg, sufficient stability for standing on day 5–7 without a cast. Nails removed at 6–8 months. Müller's compressive arthrodesis (M. E. Compressive arthrodesis with an Ilizarov external fixator uses bilateral skeletal traction with rings (two distal femoral rings and two proximal — one with wires through the iliac wing above the acetabulum, the second through the iliac crest below the ASIS) with threaded rods for compression — particularly useful with significant bone loss or active infection contraindicating internal metal fixation.

## Extra-articular Iliotrochanteric and Ischiofemoral Arthrodeses

Extra-articular arthrodeses bridge ilium to greater trochanter or ischial tuberosity without opening the joint — particularly useful in tuberculous coxitis where infection contraindicates internal metal fixation and joint opening.

Albee's iliotrochanteric arthrodesis takes a bone graft about 8 cm long from the lateral iliac wing and slides it down into the trochanteric fossa. Manfrini and Schlomsky place a wide graft in a groove at the trochanter apex. Michelsman rotates the trochanter tip 180° and fixes with a screw. Hass's arthrodesis takes the anterior two-thirds of the trochanter with 5 cm of lateral femoral cortex, opens the capsule, and impacts the harvested piece into a "window" of os ilium without disturbing muscle or periosteal attachments. Hibbs uses the Hass technique but rotates the trochanter fragment 180° before impaction. Ghormley creates a wide deep groove from the trochanter apex along the superior neck, head, and acetabular rim, into which a massive graft of full iliac wing thickness is impacted. John Wilson splits the trochanter apex sagittally, separates the lateral lamina of the anterior third of the iliac wing, and rotates it down over the superior joint, wedging it into the split trochanter. Badgley's iliofemoral arthrodesis uses a wide excision of the anterior third of the iliac crest with full bone thickness, impacted into a deep groove encompassing head, neck, and trochanteric region — particularly biomechanically stable.

Boychev's closed tunnel extra-articular arthrodesis was developed specifically for tuberculous coxitis in the quiescent phase: dedicated instrumentation tunnels a channel through ilium, neck, and trochanter into which a graft is placed without opening the joint — minimising the risk of disease reactivation.

Ischiofemoral arthrodeses by Trumble (posterior approach with detachment of the gluteus maximus tendon together with a fragment of gluteal tuberosity, bone graft between ischial tuberosity and femur), Kirkaldy–Willis (anteroinferior posterior junction with graft and screws), and Brittain (combined extra-articular arthrodesis with high supporting femoral osteotomy) are biomechanically sound but demand caution near the sciatic nerve.

## Mixed and Atypical Arthrodeses

Chokanov's mixed arthrodesis combines intra-articular union with iliofemoral bridging in two variants — vertical-graft (triangular transplant) and horizontal-graft (rectangular iliac-crest transplant wedged into a prepared trochanteric/neck groove). Balchev's mixed arthrodesis uses head-neck osteotomy with surface preparation of head, neck, and an AP groove in the trochanteric fossa, into which vertical bone strips from the iliac wing are inserted with lateral-lamina coverage. Davis's mixed arthrodesis uses an anterior iliofemoral approach with harvest of a large bone graft from the anterior iliac wing, left attached to TFL and the anterior portions of gluteus medius and minimus — a biologically active pedicled graft.

Abbott–Fischer–Lucas atypical arthrodesis is a three-stage procedure for severe destruction after infection or femoral-neck nonunion: I — deformity correction with skeletal traction; II — arthrodesis in marked abduction via anterior iliofemoral approach with neck-stump resection; III — derotation intertrochanteric osteotomy in the recovery phase. Weber's atypical arthrodesis for head-neck assimilation (as in severe DDH) valgises the trochanter into a prepared acetabular socket fixed with a curved plate into the ilium, followed by intertrochanteric osteotomy for limb alignment. Arthrodesis with a Künscher nail (further developed in Bulgaria by Karchinov with a dedicated targeting jig) uses a central nail through ilium and femur — particularly applicable to head-neck assimilation. Vladimirov's atypical arthrodesis with a ridge autograft uses a fibular head to bridge a defect between acetabulum and proximal femur after extensive resection for tumour or infection — the fibular head is impacted into the superior acetabulum, the distal end into a prepared femoral-shaft groove.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>Arthrodesis functional position must be 25–30° flexion, 0–5° adduction, and 5–10° external rotation. Greater flexion (>40°) produces lumbar hyperlordosis and back overload; abduction creates a short-limb gait; rotation beyond 15° (internal or external) restricts walking with asymmetry. In the elderly with a stiff spine, arthrodesis gives unacceptable function — a relative contraindication above 60 years.</p>
</aside>

## Biological-Interposition Arthroplasty — Historical Foundations

The classical hip arthroplasty with biological interposition has a century-long history (Aalbee, 1885) and was a standard method in the first half of the 20th century before endoprostheses. Murphy and Lexer interposed a pedicled fascial-fat graft; Hackenbrock emphasised the decisive role of postoperative distraction. Balchev modified with a fascia-lata graft 15×8 cm from tractus iliotibialis, wrapped in a moist compress. After developing the interval between TFL and abductors and exposing the joint, a Gigli saw is passed beneath the trochanter and the trochanteric mass is osteotomised; the femoral head articular surface is covered with the fascial graft, sutured in place. The decisive prognostic factor is sustained ~1 cm distraction between acetabulum and fascia-covered head for 20–30 days — most efficiently with an Ilizarov ring fixed to a hip-spica. In modern practice the technique is essentially abandoned, replaced by arthroplasty.

## Smith-Petersen Vitallium Mould Arthroplasty

Vitallium mould arthroplasty (Smith-Petersen, 1939) used a metal Vitallium cup placed over the spherically reshaped femoral head as an interpositional layer between head and acetabulum — with the hope of cartilage regeneration beneath the cup. The technique was the standard method of arthroplasty for coxarthrosis from the 1940s to the late 1950s, before total replacement became established. Long-term outcomes included high rates of pain and motion limitation, but the procedure has historical significance as the conceptual ancestor of hip resurfacing.

## Hemiarthroplasty — Moore and Thompson

Hemiarthroplasty was introduced by F. A. Moore (1945) — subsequently modified by Thompson and others. Indications today are limited to: femoral neck fractures in patients above 75 with limited life expectancy; pseudarthroses or AVN with an intact acetabulum; failed osteosynthesis in elderly patients at high reoperation risk. The two principal models are: Moore-Austin (with a long fenestrated stem and collar) — for press-fit fixation; and Thompson (with a shorter collared stem) — usually cemented. The approach is most often Moore's posterior. Head size is measured in the acetabulum; orientation 10–15° anteversion (parallel to a 90°-flexed knee). In osteoporotic bone or an oversized canal, cementation is preferred; otherwise press-fit. External rotators repaired. Standing with crutches from a few days, full loading at 3–4 months.

The first total replacements are tied to Wiles (1936), McKee (1951), Sivash (1958), and Charnley (1960). Charnley introduced the fundamental low-friction concept: a small 22 mm metal head against a high-density polyethylene cup, both fixed with acrylic cement. McKee–Farrar used metal-on-metal articulation (metal head against metal cup with screw fixation in the three acetabular bones); abandoned in the late 1970s due to aseptic loosening and metal-ion concerns. Müller introduced the self-fixing acetabular cup and standardised orientation (45° inclination, 10–20° anteversion). Ring developed the metal acetabular capsule with slots for bony ingrowth (1970s) — a precursor of the cementless standard. Wagner developed the conical stem (Zweimüller-type) and later hip resurfacing. Sivash (Moscow) combined stem and acetabulum into a single Soviet-standard total hip implant — widely used throughout the Eastern Bloc.

In Bulgaria the Gerchev complex endoprosthesis has distinctive features: two interchangeable femoral stems (for press-fit and cemented fixation), a 32 mm head, three neck-length sizes; two acetabular variants (press-fit with a titanium spirally serrated outer capsule and cemented with a polyethylene outer capsule); a polyethylene articular cup replaceable in a minor revision (by Charnley's wear estimate of 0.1 mm/year, replacement after more than 20 years). Component interchangeability between variants allows hybrid fixation — cemented stem with press-fit cup and vice versa — matched to bone quality. The approach is most often anterolateral or posterolateral (per Holevich) with osteoplastic trochanteric osteotomy. Active suction 36–48 h; sitting day 6–7; standing with crutches day 14.

For arthroplasty in adult-DDH with high-riding head (residual dislocation), Holevich described a modified technique: the proximal femoral fragment is deperiosted distally to proximally, iliopsoas detached and tagged, head and neck removed; the hypoplastic, triangular, anteverted acetabular dome is reamed to the inner lamina with small reamers preserving anterior and posterior walls; with a shallow dome, the prepared femoral head is fixed with compression screws as augmentation. Femoral canal reamed to cortex; a rectangular stem (Sivash modification) inserted; if reduction is impossible, additional femoral shortening is performed. Stem anteversion is matched to acetabular orientation — usually less than the standard 10°.

For wide proximal-femoral resection per Andreev–Gerchev–Tsachev (tumour or traumatic defect up to 15 cm below trochanter apex) a dedicated femoral stem with a conical proximal segment and a set of collars of varying diameter replaces the defect; the distal stem is standard. The standard "Etropal DMP" acetabular component is press-fit or cemented. For larger defects the alternative is replacement with a massive osteochondral allograft of the proximal femur using a Künscher intramedullary nail and reinsertion of the gluteals, external rotators, and iliopsoas to the allograft trochanters.

## Cementless Resurfacing (Wagner) Without Head Removal

Wagner developed (1978) hip resurfacing without removal of the femoral head — the head is reshaped to a spherical surface with a conical reamer and a metal shell is placed over it, articulating against a polyethylene or metal acetabular cup. The approach is posterior or anterolateral with rectus femoris release or excision, hip flexion-abduction-external rotation for anterior dislocation, and acetabular reaming. The head shell is cemented centred on the acetabular cup. Indications: young patient with AVN without extensive neck involvement, early coxarthrosis in a young patient with good bone quality. Complication: residual neck fracture (not uncommon in the first postoperative year). The modern equivalent of Wagner is the Birmingham Hip Resurfacing concept (McMinn), currently of limited European use because of metal-ion concerns with metal-on-metal articulation.

## "Salvage" Reconstructive Methods in Adults

"Salvage" methods do not promise full functional restoration and are indicated where local or systemic contraindications preclude arthroplasty or arthrodesis: chronic infection after failed arthroplasty, contraindications to reconstruction by general condition, neck pseudarthrosis in the elderly with significant comorbidity. Colonna's procedure (resection arthroplasty with gluteal-muscle envelope coverage of the proximal femoral remnant) is performed through an interval between sartorius and TFL with abductor and external-rotator release from the trochanter and a T-shaped capsulotomy; the head is removed, the neck is divided at its base, and the cut surface is covered with an inverted flap of joint capsule. Immobilisation — a K-wire from trochanter to acetabular dome, an abduction splint for 1 month, standing with assistance at 2 months.

Girdlestone resection arthroplasty (G. R. Girdlestone, 1928) is definitive removal of head and neck without replacement, allowing the limb to heal with fibrous ankylosis in a shortened position. Indications: severe infection after arthroplasty (the "explantation" stage before reimplantation in two-stage revision), tuberculous coxitis with full destruction, severe failed reconstructions. The limb is stable but shortened 4–6 cm; the patient generally requires a heel raise and a cane. A high supportive Schanz osteotomy (see below) can complement Girdlestone for improved function.

## Operations for Developmental Dysplasia/Dislocation of the Hip in Children

The classical Bulgarian and Russian schools describe a systematic operative approach to childhood DDH based on age, dislocation severity, acetabular development, and secondary proximal-femoral deformity.

## Paediatric Derotation–Varus Intertrochanteric Osteotomy

Residual centering deficits in a child over 3 years after untreated dysplasia or closed-treated DDH are corrected by intertrochanteric osteotomy. The actual antetorsion and valgus angles are determined by AP and lateral X-rays, and the planned correction is checked on an "optimal-position" AP in abduction and IR. With a Holevich–Vladimirov compression blade or an AO blade plate, the patient is in lateral decubitus or supine on a fracture table; the correction is trivial and stable without a cast. Precise blade orientation in the neck is performed under fluoroscopy; the assistant rotates the distal fragment externally until the 90°-flexed leg is horizontal. Valgus and antetorsion corrections are verified intraoperatively.

For neck-shaft angle correction the Iliev external fixator (Bulgarian) is attached to the trochanteric mass and the subtrochanteric shaft; intertrochanteric osteotomy with an oscillating saw allows precise antetorsion and valgus correction via a turning mechanism. K-wires placed parallel to the desired angle; correction fixed with a blade plate.

## Derotation Osteotomy (Putti)

Putti's subtrochanteric derotation osteotomy for excessive antetorsion without valgus uses a lateral incision at the level of the greater trochanter; two thick pins are placed in the proximal fragment at the neck and just above the lesser trochanter in the sagittal plane; two more in the distal fragment, internally rotated by the planned derotation. A thin osteotome cuts the bone along a pre-perforated channel. Holevich described an analogous technique but abandoned it because of pin loosening in infant/young-child metaphyseal bone.

## Acetabuloplasty (Albee)

Albee's acetabuloplasty horizontalises the acetabular roof for persistent roof dysplasia (>3 years with low likelihood of spontaneous correction, untreated dysplasia with deficient coverage). The approach is anterior iliofemoral; the level and direction of the osteotomy are determined fluoroscopically; the osteotome enters close to and parallel to the roof so that the distal fragment including the roof can be readily levered downward. After bending over the head, the resulting defect is filled with homografts.

## Acetabuloplasty (Lance, Pemberton, Gill)

Lance's acetabuloplasty makes a thin osteotomy in the lateral cortex of the iliac wing just above the capsular insertion along a curved line parallel to the acetabular rim; after deepening, the distal fragment is lowered and a compact allograft or tibial autograft is wedged into the defect. Lance is the conceptual predecessor of Pemberton. Gill's analogous technique uses iliac-crest autografts.

Pemberton's pericapsular osteotomy (P. A. Pemberton, 1965) is the most radical acetabuloplasty, relying on the plasticity of the triradiate cartilage to rotate the entire roof anteriorly and laterally. Greatest effect — between 2 and 4 years when the Y-cartilage is highly elastic; not applicable after 12 (Y-cartilage closure). Technique: anterior iliofemoral approach; subperiosteal release of TFL and gluteus medius/minimus; exposure of the anterior iliac wing; subperiosteal entry behind the joint into the greater sciatic notch; osteoplastic release of the abdominal muscles and sartorius from the iliac crest; subperiosteal exposure of the inner iliac wing and entry into the greater sciatic notch. The osteotomy is pericapsular (around the joint capsule) down to the Y-cartilage, allowing rotation of the acetabular fragment anteriorly and laterally without breaking the pelvic ring. The defect is filled with a triangular iliac-crest graft. Hip-spica in abduction and slight IR for 2 months.

## Salter Innominate Osteotomy

The Salter innominate osteotomy (R. B. Salter, 1961) is the standard correction for malorientation (antetorsion and lateroversion) of the acetabulum after closed treatment of DDH. Prerequisites: no intra-articular obstacle to reduction, congruent head centred in a concentric acetabulum, sufficient abduction and flexion. Age range: 18 months — 6 years (concept applicable to age 8–10); also feasible in adults combined with other procedures.

Technique: anterior iliofemoral approach with TFL and abdominal-muscle release from the iliac crest; subperiosteal exposure of both sides of the iliac wing; identification of the greater sciatic notch; passage of a Gigli saw or osteotome across the full width of the ilium from the greater sciatic notch to the AIIS. The distal fragment including the acetabulum is rotated anteriorly, inferiorly, and laterally and fixed with a triangular iliac-crest graft wedged in the osteotomy gap and two K-wires through the iliac wing into the graft and distal fragment. Hip-spica at 20° abduction and moderate IR for 45 days.

## Dega and Triple Steel Osteotomies

The Dega osteotomy (W. Dega, 1969) uses an oblique cut beginning anteriorly 1 cm above the acetabular rim and ending in the acetabular notch; the distal fragment including the acetabulum is rotated inferiorly and laterally, fixed with interposed bone grafts. It differs from Salter by incomplete osteotomy (inner cortex preserved), allowing concentric rotation without K-wires.

The Steel triple osteotomy (H. H. Steel, 1973) is indicated for hip instability from acetabular dysplasia in an older child or young adult (>10 years) for whom unilateral Salter is insufficient because of pelvic-ring rigidity. It begins with ischial osteotomy (anterior incision below the inguinal fold with hamstring detachment from the ischial tuberosity), followed by pubic osteotomy (midline incision below the symphysis with division of the superior pubic ramus), and iliac osteotomy (anterior iliofemoral approach with iliac-crest division from the greater sciatic notch to the AIIS). The entire acetabular fragment (freed from the pelvic ring) is rotated for optimal coverage and fixed with screws or K-wires.

## Chiari Osteotomy

The Chiari osteotomy (K. Chiari, 1955) differs from the acetabuloplasties by medialising the femur, which favourably alters internal-to-external lever-arm ratio, improves biomechanics, and reduces joint reaction forces. Indications: dysplasia with uncovered head in a child over 6, prior to adult coxarthrosis, or for major deformity without other reconstructive option. Technique: anterior iliofemoral approach; subperiosteal exposure of both iliac wing surfaces; osteotomy precisely above the joint capsule (not above the level of the roof but at the roof level), starting from the greater sciatic notch; division of the lateral cortex with an osteotome and the medial cortex with a Gigli saw passed through the greater sciatic notch. The distal fragment is medialised — the superior acetabular lamina is embedded in the ilium remnant, forming a new "pseudoarticular" roof. Hip-spica in abduction and slight IR for 2.5 months. Technical pitfalls: too-posterior translation of the distal fragment (sagittal-plane biomechanical compromise with compensatory lordosis); too-low cut (risk of opening the joint); too-close to the sacroiliac joint (impeded translation).

## Shelf Acetabuloplasty — Manfrini, Ivanov, Dickson

With insufficient weight-bearing surface but no possibility of rotation (older children and adolescents), osteoplastic roof lengthening with a pedicled bone graft is used. Manfrini's method takes a graft from the lateral iliac cortex, hinges it over the capsule, and additional grafts wedge the space between the wing and the pedicled graft. Ivanov's method takes a 1.5×5–6 cm graft near the greater sciatic notch; the inner cortex is incompletely divided, the graft is hinged externally over the capsule by bending the outer cortex, and fixation is with a K-wire. Dickson's method was developed specifically for high dislocation — capsular mobilisation from the wing without joint opening and creation of a new roof over it in a more anterior position.

## Supportive (Palliative) Osteotomies in High DDH

Supportive osteotomies in neglected high DDH are palliative — improving statics and gait, eliminating pain from head-iliac wing impingement, and cosmetic improvement. Classical eponyms: Kirmisson (high valgus), Froehlich (acetabular-level), Schanz (ischial-tuberosity-level), and Bayer (bifurcation). Milch's method combines valgus with ischial support. The aim is an angle open medially and inferiorly so the distal fragment can abut the ischial tuberosity on loading — significant functional improvement in painless patients where arthrodesis and arthroplasty are contraindicated. The drawback is exacerbation of pre-existing knee valgus (previously masked by the hip-adduction contracture). With proper technique, patient satisfaction is high.

## Open Reduction with Capsuloplasty (Colonna and Zahradníček)

In high dislocations in children over 5–6 with major anatomic deformity and head-acetabulum mismatch, the capsule serves as the interposition between the new acetabulum and the reduced head. Colonna's open reduction with capsuloplasty: preparatory traction (10–12 days, 5–10 kg through the distal femur with counter-traction via a hip-spica on the healthy side or via an Ilizarov ring). After head lowering to the acetabular level, open reduction is performed with the capsule used as head covering — the capsule is "wrapped" around the head, preventing bone-to-unformed-acetabulum contact. Often combined with intertrochanteric osteotomy of the proximal metaphysis to correct antetorsion and valgus (Colonna–Zahradníček procedure, introduced in Bulgaria by Balchev).

Zahradníček's modified technique for high dislocations over 3–4 years with tight reduction: oblique osteotomy in the sagittal plane from below the trochanter to below the lesser trochanter with access along the posterior border of vastus lateralis; proximal-fragment rotation to 0–10° antetorsion to the frontal plane; femoral shortening (abravation) by resection of a diaphyseal segment with significant tension. Fixation with a Holevich compression blade or with transverse intertrochanteric osteotomy and metal fixation. Ignatov's variant — transverse osteotomy distal to the lesser trochanter with fragment overlap and rotation for derotation — reduces the risk of proximal-fragment ischemia.

## Hip Disarticulation

Hip disarticulation is indicated for proximal-third femoral malignancies (osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, metastatic disease with pathological fracture), severe limb crushes with non-viable tissue, and life-threatening infectious complications (gas gangrene, uncontrollable post-traumatic sepsis). Technique: racquet-shaped skin incision starting below the ASIS, descending vertically and curving across the medial thigh about 6–7 cm below the inguinal ligament; the posterior limb passes 8 cm below the ischial tuberosity and curves laterally 10 cm below the trochanter apex, then returns along the anterior thigh. Piriformis and rectus femoris are released; pectineus divided 1 cm from insertion; the limb is externally rotated and adducted to expose the lesser trochanter; iliopsoas divided close to its insertion and allowed to retract; adductors and ischiotibial muscles released near insertions; the obturator artery identified and ligated between obturator externus and the rotators; the femoral neurovascular bundle is ligated separately (artery, then vein, then nerve); the sciatic nerve is identified medial to the ischial tuberosity and sharply transected after local-anaesthetic infiltration to prevent neuroma. The gluteals are sutured to adductor remnants. Suction drains. Prosthetic fitting is feasible with smooth healing, no earlier than 2–3 months.

## Traumatic Hip Dislocations

Surgical intervention for traumatic hip dislocation is required for: irreducible dislocation (soft-tissue or bony block); unreduced posterior wall fracture after closed reduction; unreduced femoral head fracture from the dislocation; dislocation with acetabular floor fracture remaining displaced after reduction. In all cases closed reduction must be performed under general anaesthesia with muscle relaxants immediately after diagnosis (within 6 hours to minimise AVN risk) — Allis or Stimson technique. Post-reduction imaging: AP and Zhelev oblique view (45° oblique-beam projection for posterior-wall visualisation).

For irreducible dislocation: Moore posterior approach with blunt splitting of gluteus maximus fibres; sciatic nerve identification and protection; capsule opening; direct manipulation; skeletal traction 45–60 days. For unreduced posterior wall: posterior approach with reduction and two-screw fixation of the fragment (see also Topic Trauma-12 for Letournel classification). For unreduced head fracture (Pipkin): anterolateral approach between sartorius and TFL with rectus femoris release and protection of the medial femoral circumflex artery; visualisation with rotation and flexion; fragment fixation with countersunk screws, or excision of a small non-weight-bearing fragment. For central dislocation (luxatio coxae centralis): the Tenev technique uses a dedicated 12–13 cm screw with two threads via the trochanter into the femoral head, with external fixation in a cast for head lateralisation.

Adduction (varus, Pauwels III) femoral neck fractures always require operative treatment because of 100% nonunion risk with conservative management. Abduction (valgus, Pauwels I) fractures may be treated conservatively, but the risk of secondary displacement in a cast warrants osteosynthesis. In children, fixation with K-wires is preferred because of redislocation risk in a cast.

Classical osteosynthesis with the Smith-Petersen tri-flanged nail (1937) uses guide wires driven subtrochanterically toward the contralateral ASIS (parallel to the floor); no resistance — wire in cancellous bone; resistance — wire in cortex requiring correction. After radiographic verification, the nail is driven over the wire, followed by an impactor for fragment wedging. In comminuted, subcapital fractures the nail may pass through the acetabular floor for added stability.

The Scaglietti–Putti screw osteosynthesis uses one or two cancellous screws that, via a basal washer, pull the head and generate compression. This is the modern standard in young patients with medial neck fractures — three cannulated screws in a perfect inverted triangle (two inferior, one superior subcapital-cortical). Complication: iatrogenic fracture during third-screw placement in extreme cortical position with osteoporotic bone.

The dynamic hip screw (DHS) is the modern standard for stable intertrochanteric fractures and selected basocervical fractures. The Holevich compression blade-plate is also applicable in these fractures, especially combined with an additional cancellous screw for rotational stability.

For femoral neck pseudarthrosis: in a young patient without AVN — McMurray valgising intertrochanteric osteotomy with stable fixation, or Pauwels Y-osteotomy (double-wedge) to convert shear into compression; with segmental necrosis — Meyers muscle-pedicled bone graft from vastus lateralis (pedicled periosteum) or, historically, Bonnel-Cabanela; with marked necrosis and head destruction in the elderly — arthroplasty (hemiarthroplasty or THA). Open osteosynthesis with a Whitman graft or the Ettore–Putti–McMurray technique with cancellous bone graft from the trochanter remains historical, supplanted by internal fixation with DHS or screws and arthroplasty in the elderly.

## Pertrochanteric Fractures — Kozhuharov Blade Plate, DHS, Ender, Gamma Nail

Pertrochanteric fractures in the elderly require stable fixation for early mobilisation and prevention of thromboembolic and pulmonary complications. The classical Kozhuharov blade-plate (Bulgarian school) uses a neck blade and a diaphyseal plate with 4 screws; reduction on a fracture table; fixation through a lateral 15–20 cm incision above and below the trochanter. The dynamic hip screw — the gold standard for stable intertrochanterics — allows controlled head descent with loading and dynamic compression. Ender's technique (1969) uses three thin elastic intramedullary nails inserted retrograde through the medial femoral condyle to subcapital level — minimal surgical trauma but high rates of distal migration and collapse in osteoporotic bone. The gamma nail and modern cephalomedullary nails (PFNa, InterTan) are biomechanically superior in subtrochanteric and reverse-obliquity fractures.

In children — closed percutaneous fixation with three K-wires in a perfect triangle under fluoroscopy; if closed reduction fails — open reduction via Moore posterior approach. Capsule opened T-shaped for visualisation; fixation with thick K-wires. Postoperative: cast immobilisation (unlike the stable fixation in adults). Wires removed at 3 months.

For reverse-obliquity fractures failing standard fixation, the Dial osteotomy with oblique trochanteric cut and reorientation provides stable biomechanics.

## Closed Reduction of Neglected Hip Dislocations in Children

Treatment of high DDH in children over 2–3 years requires preparatory head descent by prolonged traction: indirect traction with mastisol-adhesive (2 kg) for 15–20 days — applicable to small children under 3–4; direct skeletal traction with a femoral pin (3–4 kg) with leg-end elevation for counter-traction — insufficient in small-body-mass children; direct traction with contralateral hip-spica fixed to the bed frame — more effective; gradual distraction with an Ilizarov ring on the distal femur connected to a contralateral hip-spica by infinite screws — 0.5 cm/day up to 1 cm initially over 10–15 days. Ilizarov's original technique fixes a half-ring to the iliac wing and a full ring to the femur — with infection risk along the future open-reduction incision.

Somerville's limbectomy for incomplete reduction in children up to 1.5–2 years: the classical concept is that an inverted limbus acetabuli obstructs closed reduction (confirmed by preoperative arthrography). Small longitudinal incision over the anterior iliac crest, capsular opening, resection of the inverted limbus. Immobilisation in abduction and IR.

Typical open reduction: Hueter anterior approach; attention to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve crossing obliquely along the anterior border of sartorius; T-shaped capsulotomy; identification of the obstructing elements (inverted limbus, sliding capsule, iliopsoas, ruptured or hypertrophic ligamentum teres). Head reduced into the acetabulum, hip-spica in abduction and IR — stable position. A transverse bar is fixed between the two thigh portions of the cast, the bed is raised at the feet, and a 3–4 kg weight is attached to the bar for partial decompression — by Putti's rule (head held in the lower medial quadrant of the intersection of the horizontal bi-ischial line and the vertical from the acetabular roof edge).

## Hip Arthroscopy

Hip arthroscopy has emerged as the platform for management of FAI, labral tears, and other intra-articular hip pathology. The principal portals include: Anterolateral portal: Approximately 1 to 2 cm anterior and 2 cm distal to the greater trochanter tip. The principal viewing portal. Anterior portal: Established under fluoroscopic guidance, with attention to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. Posterolateral portal: Posterior to the trochanter. Mid-anterior portal: An accessory portal for specific procedures.

The arthroscopic examination of the central compartment (within the joint) and the peripheral compartment (between the capsule and the femoral neck) requires hip distraction (typically with a traction table) for central compartment access.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

The hip offers multiple surgical approaches, each with characteristic advantages and risks centered on the muscular envelope, the neurovascular structures, and the blood supply to the femoral head through the medial femoral circumflex artery. The Smith-Petersen anterior approach uses the internervous intervals between sartorius/tensor (superficial) and rectus/gluteus medius (deep) and has gained popularity as the direct anterior approach to THA. The Watson-Jones anterolateral approach uses the interval between gluteus medius and tensor fasciae latae with anterior gluteus medius reflection. The Hardinge direct lateral approach splits the gluteus medius and reflects the anterior portion with the gluteus minimus and a flap of vastus lateralis. The posterior approach through gluteus maximus split and short external rotator detachment is the workhorse for THA, with sciatic nerve injury and posterior dislocation as the principal concerns. The Ganz trochanteric flip osteotomy provides comprehensive access to the femoral head while preserving the medial femoral circumflex artery. The principal neurovascular structures at risk include the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (anterior approaches, meralgia paresthetica), the femoral nerve and vessels (deep anterior dissection), the sciatic nerve (posterior approach), and the superior gluteal nerve (between gluteus medius and minimus, at risk with extended proximal lateral dissection). The choice of approach depends on the surgeon's training and preference, the planned procedure (THA, fracture fixation, joint-preserving surgery, tumor resection), and the patient's anatomy and comorbidities.

From the classical Bulgarian and Russian orthopaedic schools in hip surgery the points worth remembering are: the classical approaches (vertical anterior, Hueter, Smith-Petersen, Watson-Jones, Hardinge, Olier, Ombrédanne, Colonna, Marino-Zuco, Chaklin–Vreden, Kocher–Gibson, Langenbeck, Osborne, Moore, Zhelev, McFarland–Osborne, Ludloff medial) with their muscular intervals and anatomical considerations; the soft-tissue procedures (Beck, Durham, Stoffel tenotomies, Voss hanging-hip method, Sauter, Lange, Ober–Barr, Chaklin, Berthon, Mustard, Sharrard muscle transpositions, Holevich–Dzherov–Vladimirov lavsanodeses); the proximal femoral osteotomies (Pauwels varus with Holevich–Vladimirov compression blade, valgus with AO blade plate and Holevich reverse blade plate for congenital coxa vara, Kirmisson–Putti derotation, flexion and extension, three-dimensional Imhäuser–Holevich–Dzherov for SCFE, McMurray for neck pseudarthrosis, Sugioka rotational for AVN); the arthrodeses (closed Vreden and Holevich, open Watson-Jones with nail, compressive Charnley–Holmgren, Holevich, Müller, Ilizarov; extra-articular Albee, Hass, Hibbs, Ghormley, J. Wilson, Badgley and Boychev closed-tunnel; ischiofemoral Trumble, Kirkaldy–Willis, Brittain; mixed Chokanov, Balchev, Davis; atypical Abbott–Fischer–Lucas, Weber, with Künscher nail, and Vladimirov ridge autograft); the arthroplasties (biological interposition by Murphy–Lexer–Hackenbrock–Balchev, Smith-Petersen Vitallium mould, Moore-Austin and Thompson hemiarthroplasties, total replacements by Wiles, McKee–Farrar, Charnley, Müller, Wagner, Ring, Sivash, and the Bulgarian Gerchev complex endoprosthesis, with modifications for adult-DDH by Holevich and for wide resections by Andreev–Gerchev–Tsachev); the paediatric DDH procedures (derotation-varus intertrochanteric with the Iliev fixator, acetabuloplasties by Albee, Lance, Pemberton, Gill, Salter innominate, Dega, Steel, Chiari, shelf acetabuloplasty by Manfrini, Ivanov, Dickson, supportive osteotomies by Kirmisson, Froehlich, Schanz, Bayer, Milch, open reduction with capsuloplasty by Colonna and Zahradníček, Somerville limbectomy); the fracture surgery (Smith-Petersen nail, Scaglietti–Putti screws, DHS, Kozhuharov blade plate, Ender nails, gamma nail, Tenev technique for luxatio centralis); and the salvage methods (Colonna, Girdlestone, Schanz, Wagner resurfacing). This classical foundation is the conceptual skeleton of modern algorithms for fracture stabilisation, joint preservation, and joint replacement, and remains relevant both for understanding the evolution of hip surgery and for daily practice at the USBALO "Prof. Boycho Boychev".
