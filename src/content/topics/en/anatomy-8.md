## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The hip joint — the deepest and most constrained ball-and-socket articulation in the body — carries a rich variety of surgical approaches, each with its own anatomic interval, dangers, and technical cost. Approaches range from the true anterior (Smith-Petersen) with its claim to muscle-sparing access, through the anterolateral (Watson-Jones) and direct lateral (Hardinge), balancing exposure with abductor preservation, to the universal posterior (Moore / Kocher-Langenbeck) with wide exposure but higher dislocation risk, and the trochanteric flip osteotomy (Ganz) for safe surgical hip dislocation that preserves the medial femoral circumflex artery. The central anatomic consideration governing choice and technical safety of any approach is the blood supply to the femoral head through the medial femoral circumflex artery and its retinacular branches — disruption produces avascular necrosis, particularly threatening in fracture surgery and in joint-preserving procedures. This chapter covers the bony, ligamentous, and muscular anatomy of the joint; neural and vascular topography; the five-group classification of approaches from the classical Bulgarian school; operative technique (Smith-Petersen, Hueter, Watson-Jones, Hardinge, Kocher-Langenbeck, Moore, Ludloff, Ganz, proximal femoral osteotomies, arthrodesis, arthroplasty, DDH surgery in children); typical complications by approach; and the classical Bulgarian and Russian orthopaedic school that remains part of the orthopaedic curriculum in Bulgaria and of daily practice at the USBALO "Prof. Boycho Boychev".

### Bony and Articular Anatomy
<details id="bony-articular">
<summary>Bony and Articular Anatomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · InjuryMap, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hip_anatomy.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>

The hip joint comprises the femoral head (covered by approximately 70% articular cartilage in the typical adult) articulating with the acetabulum (formed by the fused ilium, ischium, and pubis with the Y-shaped triradiate cartilage closing at age 12–16). Detailed acetabular column anatomy is presented in Topic Trauma-20 and DDH in Topic Orth-7.

**Acetabulum.** Hemispheric cavity with an adult diameter of 50–55 mm; covered by articular cartilage along the lunate facet (facies lunata), leaving a central acetabular fossa free of cartilage (filled by the pulvinar acetabuli and crossed by the transverse acetabular ligament). The acetabulum is oriented at approximately 40° lateral opening (cup inclination) and 15–20° anteversion; these parameters define cup positioning in total hip arthroplasty — the Lewinnek (1978) target is 40 ± 10° inclination and 15 ± 10° anteversion to minimize dislocation risk.

**Femoral head.** Sphere with adult diameter 40–55 mm; covered by hyaline cartilage up to 4 mm thick in the weight-bearing zone. The fovea capitis on the medial pole receives the ligamentum capitis femoris (ligamentum teres), which transmits the acetabular artery (a branch of the obturator artery) — vascularly significant in childhood and in some reconstructive procedures.

**Femoral neck.** The neck-shaft angle (CCD) averages 130° in adults; coxa vara <120°, coxa valga >135°. Femoral neck anteversion relative to the frontal plane is 10–15° in adults; >25° in a child is pathological residual anteversion (typically after DDH treatment). The capsule covers the neck anteriorly along the entire intertrochanteric line and posteriorly only along the proximal two-thirds (the capsular reflection ends before the intertrochanteric crest) — the anatomic basis for dividing neck fractures into intracapsular (subcapital, transcervical, basicervical medial) and extracapsular (per- and subtrochanteric).

**Greater trochanter.** The principal lateral landmark with three facets: lateral facet (gluteus medius), anterior facet (gluteus minimus), superior facet (piriformis tendon); the remaining short rotators (gemelli, obturator internus) insert in the trochanteric fossa behind the apex. Lesser trochanter — medial posterior landmark, insertion of iliopsoas. The intertrochanteric line anteriorly and intertrochanteric crest posteriorly mark the capsular attachments and form the reference for intertrochanteric osteotomies and femoral component positioning.

**Anterior bony landmarks.** Anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS) define the incision line in anterior approaches. Pubic tubercle is the medial boundary of the inguinal canal; the midpoint of the ASIS — pubic tubercle distance overlies the femoral head (Lubotsky 1953); the line from this midpoint to the base of the greater trochanter averages 11 cm and divides the head and neck into two nearly equal parts.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Neck-shaft angle (CCD): 130 ± 7° in the adult; coxa vara <120°, coxa valga >135°. Anteversion 10–15° in the adult, >25° in a child is pathological.</li>
    <li>Lewinnek safe zone for total hip arthroplasty: 40 ± 10° inclination, 15 ± 10° anteversion of the acetabular component.</li>
    <li>Capsule covers the neck anteriorly along the full intertrochanteric line and posteriorly only the proximal two-thirds — basis for the intra- vs. extracapsular fracture division.</li>
    <li>The femoral head overlies the midpoint of the ASIS — pubic tubercle line (Lubotsky 1953); the ASIS to greater trochanter base distance averages 11 cm.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<summary>Capsule and Ligaments</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The joint capsule is a thick fibrous sleeve attached proximally to the acetabular rim and transverse acetabular ligament; distally — anteriorly along the intertrochanteric line, posteriorly at the junction of the proximal and middle thirds of the neck. Deep circumferential fibres form the zona orbicularis — a ring-thickening encircling the neck that controls femoral head protrusion under traction.

Capsular thickness condenses into three named reinforcements:

**Iliofemoral ligament (Y-ligament of Bigelow).** The strongest ligament in the body. Arises from AIIS and the lower acetabular rim; splits into superior (lateral) and inferior (medial) limbs that insert along the intertrochanteric line. The principal anterior restraint of the joint; limits extension, external rotation, and anterior femoral head translation. Technical obstacle in the classical Smith-Petersen approach — transected transversely for access to the joint cavity.

**Pubofemoral ligament.** Crosses from the superior pubic ramus and obturator crest to the inferior capsule; reinforces the inferomedial quadrant; limits abduction and external rotation in extension.

**Ischiofemoral ligament.** Posterior restraint; arises from the ischial portion of the acetabular rim, sweeps around the posteroinferior femoral neck with obliquely ascending fibres inserting on the upper greater trochanter. Limits internal rotation in flexion — the biomechanical basis for the antero-inferior course of posterior dislocation in flexion-adduction-internal rotation.

**Acetabular labrum.** Fibrocartilaginous ring along the acetabular rim, increasing acetabular coverage by about 22% and providing a hydrostatic suction seal that stabilises the femoral head. Labral tears are the principal source of intraarticular pain in FAI and sports surgery; the posterior segment is wider peripherally and more vulnerable in posterior dislocations.

**Ligamentum capitis femoris (ligamentum teres).** Arises from the acetabular notch and transverse acetabular ligament, inserting at the fovea capitis; transmits the acetabular artery (branch of the obturator artery). Biomechanically important only in childhood; not a stabiliser in the adult but relevant in FAI and as a synovial diagnostic landmark.

### Blood Supply to the Femoral Head
<details id="blood-supply">
<summary>Blood Supply to the Femoral Head</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The blood supply to the femoral head is the central anatomic consideration in hip surgery and governs the safety of every operative approach.

**Medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA).** Branch of the deep femoral artery (90%) or directly of the femoral artery (10%). The principal supply to the femoral head — approximately 70–80% in most specimens (Trueta 1953, Sevitt-Thompson 1965). The artery follows a curved course around the posteroinferior femoral neck, passing between pectineus and iliopsoas, and ascends along the posterior neck deep to the obturator externus tendon. At the level of the capsular reflection it gives off 2–4 retinacular branches (Weitbrecht's retinacula) that ascend in the subsynovial layer and enter the femoral head close to the cartilage-bone junction in the subcapital zone.

**Lateral femoral circumflex artery (LFCA).** Smaller anterior contribution (15–25%) via anterior retinacular branches; in the adult usually insufficient to maintain head viability after MFCA disruption.

**Artery of the ligamentum teres.** Branch of the obturator artery passing through the ligamentum teres to the fovea capitis. Significant in the child (up to 25% of head supply to age 8 years); variable in the adult and usually insufficient as a sole source — basis for AVN development in subcapital fractures with disruption of the retinacular vessels.

**Ganz trochanteric flip — anatomic key.** The deep branch of the MFCA passes directly beneath the obturator externus tendon and above the quadratus femoris tendon along the posteroinferior femoral neck. Preserving the obturator externus insertion during the Ganz trochanteric flip osteotomy preserves the retinacular branches and permits safe surgical hip dislocation without AVN — basis for modern joint-preserving surgery in FAI, Perthes disease, and femoral head osteochondritis.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The MFCA (deep branch) provides 70–80% of femoral head blood supply; retinacular branches run subsynovially along the posterior neck beneath obturator externus.</li>
    <li>Disruption of the retinacular vessels in posterior approaches with detachment of obturator externus and/or the capsular reflection → AVN up to 30% in femoral neck fractures.</li>
    <li>Safe surgical hip dislocation (Ganz) requires preserving the obturator externus insertion — the only anatomic guarantee of MFCA continuity.</li>
    <li>For femoral neck fracture, surgical reduction and fixation within 6 hours of injury statistically reduces AVN incidence — the "6-hour rule" (Manninger, Garden).</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Ligamentum teres carries the acetabular artery — clinically significant to age 8 years; in the adult usually insufficient as sole supply.</li>
    <li>Capsulotomy does not disrupt MFCA because the vessel is subsynovial — disruption comes from dissecting the capsular reflection or detaching the short rotators.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Muscular Anatomy
<details id="muscular">
<summary>Muscular Anatomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Around the hip lie four muscle groups distributed in anatomic intervals that operative surgery uses for safe access.

**Anterior compartment.** Iliopsoas (flexion, key entry for the femoral triangle), rectus femoris (hip flexion, knee extension), sartorius (hip and knee flexion; inserts at pes anserinus), pectineus (flexion and adduction).

**Lateral compartment.** Gluteus medius and minimus (principal abductors; internal rotators in flexion — particularly their anterior portions); tensor fasciae latae (TFL; flexion, abduction, internal rotation; continues into the iliotibial tract). The gluteus medius insertion on the lateral facet and the gluteus minimus insertion on the anterior facet of the greater trochanter are the central reference for lateral approaches.

**Posterior compartment.** Gluteus maximus (extension and external rotation; continues into the iliotibial tract aponeurosis); piriformis (external rotation in extension, abduction in flexion; inserts on the superior facet of the trochanter); short external rotators — superior gemellus, obturator internus, inferior gemellus (the "tripod tendon") and quadratus femoris; obturator externus (slings hammock-fashion along the posteroinferior neck — the anatomic guardian of the deep branch of MFCA).

**Medial compartment.** Adductor longus, brevis, magnus (with a proximal adductor and a distal hamstring portion), gracilis. The adductors form the medial wall in the Ludloff approach and are released in adductor tenotomy for cerebral palsy.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Gluteus medius and minimus are critical for normal gait — Trendelenburg gait develops with their dysfunction; preservation is a priority in every lateral approach.</li>
    <li>Obturator externus is the anatomic guardian of the deep MFCA branch — never detached in posterior approaches or the Ganz flip.</li>
    <li>The iliopsoas tendon inserts on the lesser trochanter; its detachment is a standard step in the classical Smith-Petersen approach and in tenotomy for flexion contracture.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

**Femoral triangle.** Bounded superiorly by the inguinal ligament, laterally by sartorius, and medially by adductor longus; covered by fascia lata; the floor is formed by iliopsoas (lateral) and pectineus (medial). The contents pass from lateral to medial as NAVL: femoral nerve (in the groove between iliopsoas and iliacus), femoral artery, femoral vein, deep inguinal (Cloquet) lymph node — the latter the medial boundary marker.

**Femoral nerve (L2–L4).** Emerges from the lumbar plexus in the posterior part of the psoas major; passes beneath the inguinal ligament in the groove between psoas and iliacus. Gives motor branches to quadriceps, sartorius, and pectineus; sensory branches form the anterior femoral cutaneous nerves and the saphenous nerve. Principal neurological danger in anterior approaches — particularly the "vertical anterior" approach that enters the iliopsoas groove.

**Femoral artery.** The principal vascular danger in anterior approaches. About 7–8 cm distal to the inguinal ligament it gives off the deep femoral artery, the principal source of the medial (medial) and lateral (lateral) femoral circumflex arteries.

**Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (L2–L3).** Emerges near the ASIS, passing distally in the subcutaneous plane along the anterolateral thigh. Anatomic variability (Aszmann 1997): passes directly beneath ASIS in 27%, up to 1 cm medial in 21%, up to 1 cm lateral in 18%, and up to 3 cm lateral in 34% of specimens. The principal neurological danger in anterior approaches (Smith-Petersen, DAA); injury causes meralgia paresthetica — transient in 20–80% after DAA, permanent in 5–10%.

**Sciatic nerve (L4–S3).** The thickest nerve in the body; exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, typically below piriformis (Beaton-Anson type I in 85%; type II with a proximal portion through piriformis itself in 10%; remaining variants in 5%). Courses obliquely medial to lateral along the deep surface of the gemelli, obturator internus, and quadratus femoris, passing medial to the midpoint between the ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter. The principal neurological danger in posterior approaches; injury causes foot drop (especially the peroneal division) and sensory deficit along the lateral leg and foot.

**Superior gluteal nerve (L4–S1).** Exits the pelvis through the suprapiriform foramen, passes deep between gluteus medius and minimus, then forward into the tensor fasciae latae. Enters the TFL approximately 4–6 cm below ASIS in the middle third of the muscle — the anatomic constant defining the safe proximal extent of lateral abductor splits (the Hardinge rule of "no more than 5 cm proximal to the trochanter").

**Inferior gluteal nerve (L5–S2).** Exits through the infrapiriform foramen (below piriformis), innervates gluteus maximus; usually not critically endangered in posterior approaches because blunt fibre-splitting of gluteus maximus does not damage the nerve.

### Gluteal Region and Bursae
<details id="gluteal-region">
<summary>Gluteal Region and Bursae</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The gluteal region (regio glutaea) is bounded superiorly by the iliac crest, medially by the midline to the coccyx tip, inferiorly by the gluteal fold, and laterally by the line between the greater trochanter and ASIS. The skin is thick with abundant sebaceous glands; the subcutaneous tissue is divided into lobules by fibrous septa and forms the lumbo-iliac fat body over gluteus maximus and the gluteofemoral fat body distal to the trochanter — the vascular network of these fat pads sustains the skin flap during hip disarticulation.

**Cutaneous innervation.** The superior cluneal nerves (posterior rami of L1–L3) supply the superior quadrant; the middle cluneal nerves (S1–S3) medially; the inferior cluneal nerves (from the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve) inferolaterally.

**Gluteal spaces.** Deep neurovascular structures are distributed between two connective-tissue planes — the superficial intergluteal space (between gluteus maximus and gluteus medius plus the external rotators), through which the contents of the infrapiriform foramen pass (medial to lateral: pudendal nerve, internal pudendal vessels, sciatic nerve with posterior femoral cutaneous nerve), and the deep intergluteal space (between gluteus medius and minimus) containing the superior gluteal vessels and nerve emerging from the suprapiriform foramen. The superior gluteal nerve crosses transversely between gluteus medius and TFL and enters the latter 4–6 cm below the ASIS in the middle third of its muscle belly — an anatomic constant defining the safe proximal extent of any lateral abductor split.

**Bursae around the hip.** A constellation of synovial bursae must be recognised by the surgeon as pain sources and orientation landmarks: gluteus maximus trochanteric bursa (between the gluteus maximus tendon and the lateral trochanter — the most common source of trochanteric syndrome), superficial trochanteric bursa (subcutaneous over the trochanter apex), gluteus medius trochanteric bursa (between the gluteus medius tendon and the superior facet), piriformis bursa (deep to the tendon before its insertion), medial obturator internus bursa (between the muscle and the lesser sciatic notch — almost regularly communicates with the joint cavity), ischial bursa of gluteus maximus (between gluteus maximus and ischial tuberosity), and the iliopectineal bursa (between iliopsoas and iliopectineal eminence — in approximately 15% of specimens communicates with the hip joint cavity, accounting for bidirectional spread of infection and effusion).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The iliopectineal bursa communicates with the joint cavity in about 15% of specimens — septic coxitis may first present as a psoas abscess in the femoral triangle, and inguinal-crease fluctuation requires aspiration and exclusion of joint communication before incision.</li>
    <li>The medial obturator internus bursa almost regularly communicates with the joint cavity — anatomic basis for spread of infection to the posteroinferior gluteal region.</li>
    <li>The sciatic nerve runs medial to the midpoint between the ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter along the posterior surface of obturator internus, the gemelli, and quadratus femoris.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Pathophysiology and Mechanism
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="pathomech">
<details>
<summary>Pathophysiology and Mechanism</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Choice of operative approach to the hip is governed by the interplay of pathoanatomy, biomechanics of femoral head blood supply, and neurovascular vulnerability across the various anatomic intervals.

**Avascular necrosis.** Disruption of MFCA retinacular branches in subcapital neck fractures produces AVN in 10–30% (higher with displaced fractures, Garden III–IV). The classically safe window for fixation is within 6 hours of injury (the "6-hour rule"); later procedures do not show a linear time dependence but most authors recommend immediate reduction and fixation when clinically feasible. AVN as a pathogenetic mechanism is the central consideration in choosing hemiarthroplasty over osteosynthesis in older patients.

**Dislocation after total hip arthroplasty.** Historical rates of 3–5% with standard posterior approaches drop below 1% with meticulous capsule and short-rotator repair (Pellicci 1998), use of 36 mm heads (rather than 28 mm), and proper cup positioning within the Lewinnek safe zone (40 ± 10° inclination, 15 ± 10° anteversion). Dislocation is more common after posterior approaches (about 3% with repair, about 5% without) and rarer with direct anterior (about 1%) and Hardinge lateral (about 1%).

**Wear and aseptic loosening.** The Charnley low-friction concept (1962) — a small 22 mm metal head against a high-density polyethylene cup, fixed with acrylic cement — defined the biomechanical standards for arthroplasty. Polyethylene wear is approximately 0.1 mm/year (Charnley 1972) and governs long-term prosthesis survival through periprosthetic osteolysis. Cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE, introduced 1998) reduces wear to 0.01 mm/year; ceramic-on-ceramic and ceramic-on-XLPE eliminate metal ion release and largely eliminate wear.

**Hip joint forces.** The Pauwels biomechanical principle (1935): in single-leg stance, the pelvis is balanced by the abductors around the femoral head centre of rotation (CoR). The joint reaction force in single-leg stance is 2.5–3 × body weight, and during running up to 6 × BW. The biomechanical abductor lever arm is determined by the lateral distance from CoR to the abductor line of action (offset); medialising the CoR via a Chiari osteotomy or in arthroplasty reduces the joint force and improves biomechanics.

**FAI (femoroacetabular impingement).** Cam deformity (loss of head sphericity — post-Perthes, post-SCFE residual, idiopathic) and pincer deformity (acetabular over-coverage — protrusio, retroversion) cause repetitive labral and anterosuperior chondral trauma with progression to osteoarthritis in adulthood. Joint-preserving surgery (offset correction, acetabular rim trimming, labral reconstruction) — performable arthroscopically or via open Ganz flip — is standard in the young patient with symptomatic FAI.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>AVN with subcapital fracture: 10–30% overall; reduced with reduction and fixation within 6 hours of injury.</li>
    <li>Lewinnek safe zone for cup: 40 ± 10° inclination, 15 ± 10° anteversion — basis for minimising dislocation.</li>
    <li>Joint reaction force in single-leg stance: 2.5–3 × body weight; during running up to 6 × BW — the abductor lever arm is critical.</li>
    <li>Cross-linked polyethylene reduces wear from 0.1 mm/year (UHMWPE) to 0.01 mm/year (XLPE) — a fundamental improvement in long-term survival.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="clinical">
<details>
<summary>Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Preoperative assessment addresses the local anatomic status (for approach selection), functional reserve (to define the operative scope), and general status (for anaesthetic and thromboembolic risk).

**History.** Pain (location — groin, trochanteric, inguinal, referred to the knee; provocation — weight-bearing, sitting, stairs; character — mechanical with brief relief or nocturnal), limp (Trendelenburg from abductor weakness, antalgic from pain), functional deficit (difficulty putting on socks, cutting toenails). Previous surgical procedures, osteoporosis, rheumatic and metabolic disease, comorbidities with special emphasis on cardiovascular and pulmonary status.

**Physical examination.** Gait — antalgic limp on the affected side, shortened stance phase, positive Trendelenburg sign on single-leg stance (abductor weakness or fixed adduction). Limb length — true (ASIS to medial malleolus) versus apparent (umbilicus to medial malleolus). Range of motion: flexion 0–120° (normal), internal rotation in flexion 0–30° (lost in FAI and AVN), external rotation 0–45°, abduction 0–45°, adduction 0–30°, extension 0–10°. Thomas test (fixed flexion contracture), FABER/Patrick (sacroiliac and anterior impingement), FADIR (posterior impingement), log-roll test for intraarticular pathology.

**Imaging.** Standard AP pelvis radiograph centred 2 cm above the symphysis (to assess joint line and pelvic obliquity), and lateral femoral view — frog-leg lateral for FAI and AVN, cross-table lateral in the elderly with suspected fracture (without painful manipulation). Specialised views: false-profile (Lequesne) for vertical centring and anteroposterior coverage; inlet/outlet for pelvic fractures; Judet 45° obliques (obturator and iliac oblique) for acetabular fractures. CT with reconstructions for acetabular fractures and suspected occult neck fracture; MRI for suspected early AVN (Ficat I, before radiographic correlate), stress fracture, and trochanteric pain syndrome.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Pain in the inguinal fold — pathognomonic for intraarticular hip pathology; pain over the lateral trochanter — trochanteric bursitis or abductor pathology.</li>
    <li>Loss of internal rotation in flexion — an early and specific sign of FAI, AVN, and hip osteoarthritis.</li>
    <li>MRI is sensitive for occult femoral neck fractures and early AVN (Ficat I) before radiographic signs appear — mandatory in the elderly with typical history and normal radiograph.</li>
    <li>Lequesne false-profile assesses anteroposterior coverage of the head — standard in dysplasia analysis and pincer-type FAI.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Classification of Approaches
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classification">
<details>
<summary>Classification of Approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian school (after Boychev) divides surgical approaches to the hip into five main groups by direction of penetration and anatomic intervals:

**Anterior approaches.** Between sartorius and tensor fasciae latae (true anterior — Smith-Petersen, direct anterior DAA) or between iliopsoas and rectus femoris ("vertical" anterior). Internervous plane between femoral nerve (medial) and superior gluteal nerve (lateral). Indications: arthrodesis, ORIF of the femoral head, biopsy, pelvic osteotomies, total arthroplasty (DAA), Salter and Pemberton in the child.

**Lateral approaches.** Between tensor fasciae latae and gluteus medius (Watson-Jones) or through gluteus medius (Hardinge), with or without greater trochanter osteotomy (Olier, Ombrédanne, McFarland-Osborne). Indications: total arthroplasty, hemiarthroplasty, ORIF of femoral neck and pertrochanteric fractures.

**Posterior approaches.** Through gluteus maximus with detachment of the short external rotators (Moore, Langenbeck, Osborne, Zhelev, Kocher-Langenbeck). Indications: total arthroplasty, hemiarthroplasty, ORIF of posterior hip dislocation, fixation of posterior wall and column acetabular fractures.

**Medial approaches.** Between the anterior and medial muscle groups (Ludloff) or between muscles of the medial group (Stevens variant). Indications: open reduction of DDH in infants, adductor tenotomy, medial loose bodies.

**Combined approaches.** Anterolateral (Marino-Zuco, Chaklin-Vreden) or posterolateral (Kocher-Gibson, McFarland-Osborne); trochanteric flip osteotomy (Ganz) for full circumferential exposure. Indications: tumour resection of proximal femur, joint-preserving surgery for FAI and AVN requiring full head circumference, arthrodesis with full access.

**By purpose.** Decompression / stabilisation / arthroplasty / arthrodesis / joint preservation / resection / biopsy / drainage.

**By invasiveness.** Open / minimally invasive (MIS-DAA, MIS-PA, tubular, arthroscopy).

The choice is determined by the type of pathology (joint preservation, joint replacement, fracture, tumour, infection, DDH reconstruction), the soft-tissue quality, the need for full capsular exposure or selective exposure of a single quadrant, and not least the surgeon's experience. The Hoppenfeld principle that "the penalty of the exposure must not exceed the benefit of the procedure" remains directive.

</div>

## Treatment
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Nonoperative
<details id="nonoperative">
<summary>Nonoperative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Nonoperative management of hip pathology comprises skeletal traction, cast immobilisation, analgesic and anti-inflammatory therapy, and physiotherapy.

**Skeletal traction.** For fracture-dislocation of the head or acetabulum, in preparation for closed reduction of high DDH in a child, and in infection with joint destruction. The classical traction line is supracondylar through the femur with a weight of 1/7 of body weight (3–8 kg in an adult). Indirect traction (mastisol-adhesive) — suitable in infants up to 3–4 years; direct traction with a femoral pin — in older children and adults. Additional counterextension with a spica cast on the well limb fixed to the bed frame — more effective when preparing to reduce a high dislocated head.

**Gradual distraction with an Ilizarov ring.** In preparation for open reduction of high DDH in a child over 5–6 years — the distal femur is connected to a spica cast on the well limb via endless screws, advanced 0.5 cm daily up to 1 cm initially, for 10–15 days. Ilizarov's original technique fixes a half-ring in the iliac crest and a full ring on the femur — with infection risk in the zone of the planned open reduction.

**Cast immobilisation.** Spica cast in abduction and slight internal rotation after closed reduction of DDH in the child (Putti's rule — the head held in the lower medial quadrant of the intersection of a horizontal Hilgenreiner line and a vertical Perkins line from the acetabular margin). The cast is maintained 6 months to 1 year with changes every 2–3 months for position correction.

**Analgesic and anti-inflammatory therapy.** NSAIDs for hip osteoarthritis with mild to moderate discomfort; intraarticular steroid injection (rarely, given the risk of accelerating cartilage degeneration via iatrogenic damage) or hyaluronic acid (limited evidence in hip osteoarthritis compared to the knee).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Traction weight: classically 1/7 of body weight; 3–8 kg in adults. Heavier loads without radiographic monitoring are useless and potentially harmful.</li>
    <li>Putti's rule for cast immobilisation after closed DDH reduction: head held in the lower medial quadrant at the intersection of horizontal Hilgenreiner and vertical Perkins lines from the acetabular margin.</li>
    <li>Intraarticular injection in the hip is performed under fluoroscopic or ultrasound guidance — blind injection is unreliable and risky.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

The Smith-Petersen anterior approach (M. N. Smith-Petersen, 1917) uses a two-level internervous plane: superficially between sartorius (femoral nerve) medially and tensor fasciae latae (superior gluteal nerve) laterally; deeply between rectus femoris (femoral nerve) medially and gluteus medius (superior gluteal nerve) laterally.

**Indications.** Hip arthrodesis, open reduction of femoral head fractures (Pipkin type), biopsy, tumour procedures, pelvic osteotomies (Salter, Pemberton, Chiari), open reduction of DDH in the child. In the past two decades the approach has gained substantial popularity as the direct anterior approach (DAA) for total hip arthroplasty with reduced soft-tissue trauma and lower dislocation rates.

**Positioning.** Supine; for DAA total arthroplasty — often on a specialised Hana table with traction for femoral preparation. The skin incision begins about 2 cm distal and lateral to ASIS and extends distally 8–15 cm along the line toward the lateral patella.

**Technique.** Subcutaneous dissection close to ASIS is performed bluntly because of the variable course of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve; the nerve is identified and protected. Fascia lata over tensor fasciae latae is incised longitudinally, the muscle is retracted laterally, sartorius medially. Deep, the interval between rectus femoris (with its two heads — recurrent from AIIS and reflected from the acetabular rim) medially and gluteus medius laterally is developed. The lateral femoral circumflex artery branches cross the field in the deep layer — standard ligation following the "veins before arteries" rule. The capsule is opened T- or Z-shaped for joint access.

**Dangers.** Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (variable course relative to ASIS, injury causes meralgia paresthetica); femoral nerve in the deep layer (protected by sartorius); lateral femoral circumflex artery (ligation mandatory); rectus femoris (careful retraction, especially in the minimally invasive variant); medial femoral circumflex artery in the deep medial layer with iliopsoas detachment.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Smith-Petersen is a true two-level internervous plane: superficially sartorius (femoral n.) / TFL (superior gluteal n.); deeply rectus femoris (femoral n.) / gluteus medius (superior gluteal n.).</li>
    <li>The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve crosses near ASIS — meralgia paresthetica is the price of inattention; perform blunt subcutaneous dissection near ASIS, not with a knife.</li>
    <li>Standard ligation of the lateral femoral circumflex artery in the depth of the approach — under the "veins before arteries" rule.</li>
    <li>DAA for total arthroplasty is a technical realisation of Smith-Petersen with TFL preservation (no detachment); lower dislocation rate than the posterior approach but a steeper learning curve.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Extended medial extension of the incision medial to sartorius → danger to the femoral nerve in the iliopsoas groove.</li>
    <li>Excessive lateral retraction of TFL → denervation from traction on the superior gluteal nerve (enters 4–6 cm below ASIS).</li>
    <li>Periprosthetic fracture of the proximal femur during cementless stem insertion in DAA (more common than via posterior approach) — beware of the anterior femoral bow and use careful capsular release.</li>
    <li>Meralgia paresthetica — transient in 20–80%, permanent in 5–10% after DAA; mandatory preoperative patient counselling.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Hueter approach and the "vertical" anterior</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Hueter approach (1880) uses the posterior edge of sartorius — the skin incision starts from ASIS and descends distally along the muscle's lateral edge for about 15 cm. The interval between sartorius (medial) and TFL (lateral) is opened, and proximally TFL is detached osteoplastically from ASIS and from gluteus medius 1 cm below the iliac crest. Gluteus medius and minimus are elevated subperiosteally from the lateral surface of the iliac wing and retracted posteriorly and inferiorly, exposing the superior and anterior capsule broadly. Hueter is the conceptual predecessor of the modern direct anterior approach (DAA) — the difference is that Hueter detaches TFL and the abductors from the iliac wing, whereas DAA preserves TFL as a muscle wall.

The "vertical anterior approach" uses the medial edge of sartorius with an incision from ASIS distally and medially about 15–17 cm. The interval is opened between rectus femoris (retracted laterally) and iliopsoas (retracted medially), exposing the anterior capsule. Because of the femoral nerve in the groove between psoas and iliacus, the femoral artery along the medial edge of iliopsoas, and the origin of the medial femoral circumflex artery 7–8 cm distal to the inguinal ligament, the approach is no longer recommended for routine use and has been supplanted by Hueter/Smith-Petersen.

<details>
<summary>Anterolateral approach (Watson-Jones)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Watson-Jones approach (R. Watson-Jones, 1936) uses the interval between gluteus medius (posterior) and tensor fasciae latae (anterior), with reflection of gluteus medius from the anterior aspect of the greater trochanter.

**Indications.** Total hip arthroplasty, ORIF of selected proximal femoral fractures, hemiarthroplasty; the Bauer modification (Röttinger 2004) serves as the basis for the MIS anterolateral technique.

**Positioning and technique.** Supine or lateral. The incision is over the greater trochanter with proximal and distal extension as required. Fascia lata is incised. The interval between gluteus medius and TFL is developed. The anterior portion of gluteus medius is partially detached from the trochanter (with reattachment at closure). The capsule is opened anteriorly.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Abductor dysfunction from partial detachment of gluteus medius — precise repair at closure is mandatory.</li>
    <li>Extended proximal dissection → injury to the superior gluteal nerve; limit proximal dissection to 5 cm from the greater trochanter.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Direct lateral approach (Hardinge)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Hardinge approach (K. Hardinge, 1982) splits gluteus medius along its fibres, with anterior reflection of the anterior portion (together with gluteus minimus) as a flap continuous with vastus lateralis. The approach provides good anterior exposure of the joint while preserving the posterior capsule and posterior structures — a lower dislocation rate than the posterior approach.

**Indications.** Total hip arthroplasty, hemiarthroplasty, femoral neck fracture fixation, infection management after arthroplasty.

**Technique.** Supine or lateral. A longitudinal lateral incision over the trochanter, 7–10 cm proximal and distal. Fascia lata is opened along the incision. Gluteus medius is split along its fibres (only up to 5 cm proximal to the trochanter to protect the superior gluteal nerve). The anterior portion of gluteus medius (with gluteus minimus) is reflected anteriorly as a single flap, often in continuity with vastus lateralis. The capsule is opened anteriorly.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>In the Hardinge direct lateral approach never split gluteus medius more than 5 cm proximal to the greater trochanter — beyond that the superior gluteal nerve (between medius and minimus) is denervated, with permanent abductor weakness and Trendelenburg gait.</li>
    <li>Even with correct technique — transient abductor weakness in 4–20%; patients must be counselled that gait improvement may take 6–12 months postoperatively.</li>
    <li>Trochanteric pain and persistent limp are recognised problems — Hardinge remains a precise approach but with a higher cost in abductor comfort than MIS approaches.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Posterior approaches — Moore, Kocher-Langenbeck, Osborne, Langenbeck, Zhelev</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray434.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>

The posterior approach is the workhorse for total hip arthroplasty in many centres and the standard for posterior pelvic and acetabular surgery (Kocher-Langenbeck, addressed in detail in Topic Anatomy-7).

**Indications.** Total hip arthroplasty, hemiarthroplasty, ORIF of posterior hip dislocation, fixation of posterior wall and column acetabular fractures, sciatic nerve exploration.

**Positioning and technique.** Lateral with the affected side up, well fixed in a lateral holder. A curved incision from PSIS, over the greater trochanter, with distal extension along the proximal thigh. Fascia lata and the gluteus maximus aponeurosis are opened. Gluteus maximus is split bluntly along its fibres — branches of the inferior gluteal artery cross the muscle transversely from medial to lateral, and transverse division produces substantial bleeding that is difficult to control. The insertions of the short external rotators (piriformis, gemelli, obturator internus, quadratus femoris) on the greater trochanter are identified; the sciatic nerve is identified beneath piriformis. The short external rotators are detached from the trochanter and reflected medially, exposing the posterior capsule. The capsule is opened and the joint is entered.

**Classical variants.** The Langenbeck approach is a direct posterior approach through gluteus maximus without trochanteric osteotomy and without abductor release — the skin incision with the hip flexed at about 40° is a straight line from PSIS toward the trochanter tip, ending 4–5 cm below the base. The Osborne approach differs in the skin incision and the level of gluteus maximus division: the incision begins 4–5 cm distal and lateral to PSIS, runs parallel to the muscle fibres toward the posterosuperior corner of the trochanter, then turns distally about 5 cm. The Moore approach (southern posterior) begins about 10 cm distal to PSIS, runs parallel to the fibres toward the posterior trochanter, then distally 10–12 cm along the femur; on dividing fascia lata the gluteus maximus aponeurosis continuing into the iliotibial tract is split, and the terminal tendon is partially detached from the gluteal tuberosity to facilitate retraction. The Moore approach provides wider exposure of the posterior acetabular rim and of the more medial structures than Osborne.

The Zhelev approach <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> combines a posterior skin incision in the shape of an inverted question mark — the upper part begins behind ASIS, curves arc-like inferiorly and posteriorly along the midpoint of the trochanter–iliac crest line, sweeps widely around the posterior trochanteric border, and continues along the lateral femur. After dividing the gluteus maximus insertion and elevating it medially and superiorly, the posterior border of gluteus medius, the external rotators, and the sciatic nerve are exposed; after dividing the rotators and retracting gluteus medius anteriorly, broad access to the joint, the posterior acetabular column, and the sciatic nerve is obtained — particularly valuable when simultaneous work on the posterior column and the hip is needed (fracture-dislocations involving the posterior column).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>When dividing gluteus maximus in posterior approaches, fibres are bluntly separated along their course rather than cut transversely — inferior gluteal artery branches cross the muscle transversely from medial to lateral, and transverse division produces substantial bleeding.</li>
    <li>The sciatic nerve must be identified beneath piriformis before detaching the short external rotators; injury occurs in 1–3% of elective total arthroplasties and more frequently after reduction of posterior dislocations.</li>
    <li>Meticulous capsule and short-rotator repair at closure is mandatory to reduce posterior dislocation rates from the historical 3–5% to below 1% (Pellicci 1998).</li>
    <li>Preservation of the obturator externus insertion during dissection of the capsular reflection preserves the deep MFCA branch — critical in joint-preserving surgery and fracture management.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Sciatic nerve "high" division (Beaton-Anson type II, 10%) — the common peroneal division passes through piriformis and is vulnerable during piriformis tenotomy.</li>
    <li>Posterior dislocation of the hip prosthesis — the principal concern after the posterior approach; historically 3–5%, reduced to below 1% with modern capsular reconstruction and 36 mm heads.</li>
    <li>Heterotopic ossification — more frequent after posterior than other approaches; prophylaxis with NSAIDs (indomethacin) or single-dose radiation (7 Gy) in high-risk patients.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<details>
<summary>Medial approach (Ludloff)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Ludloff medial approach (K. Ludloff, 1908) uses the interval between the anterior and medial muscle groups of the thigh with the hip maximally abducted and flexed. The patient is supine with the tibial tubercles elevated; the skin incision is longitudinal along the medial thigh, beginning about 2 cm distal to the pubic tubercle and running between gracilis and adductor longus. In the deep layer, the interval between adductor longus and pectineus (or the Stevens variant — between adductor magnus and brevis) is opened, reaching the medial and inferior capsule.

The anatomic challenges require attention to the anterior branches of the obturator vessels and nerve emerging from the obturator foramen along the superior border of adductor brevis. The Ludloff approach remains the accepted route for closed reduction with open tenotomy in DDH of small infants (typically under 6 months) and for removal of medial loose bodies and tumours of the medial joint wall.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The anterior branch of the obturator nerve — between adductor longus and adductor brevis; injury causes medial-thigh hypoesthesia and adductor weakness.</li>
    <li>The MFCA passes between pectineus and iliopsoas — care during deep dissection.</li>
    <li>The approach is suitable in small infants (under 6 months); in older children it is replaced by Hueter/Smith-Petersen for better acetabular exposure.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</details>

<details>
<summary>Trochanteric flip osteotomy (Ganz)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Ganz trochanteric flip osteotomy (R. Ganz et al., 2001) provides broad access to the entire femoral head and acetabulum while preserving the medial femoral circumflex artery and the blood supply to the head — a revolutionary solution that re-opened joint-preserving hip surgery.

**Indications.** Fixation of femoral head fractures (Pipkin), femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) surgery, treatment of head osteochondritis dissecans, correction of residual Perthes deformity, resection of femoral head tumours, surgical hip dislocation procedures.

**Technique.** Lateral position. The posterior incision is similar to the Kocher-Langenbeck approach. The greater trochanter is osteotomised (typically with a stepwise cut 1.5 cm thick, longitudinal osteotomy with an oscillating saw), preserving the gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, and vastus lateralis insertions on the trochanteric fragment. The fragment is reflected anteriorly as a single osteomuscular block. The capsular reflection containing the deep MFCA branch is preserved by maintaining the obturator externus insertion — the critical anatomic key. The capsule is opened Z- or H-shaped and the head is dislocated anteriorly from the acetabulum. After the procedure the trochanter is reattached with 2–3 6.5 mm cancellous screws in its position.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Preservation of the obturator externus insertion is the only anatomic guarantee of deep MFCA continuity and AVN avoidance — never detached in the Ganz flip.</li>
    <li>The trochanteric osteotomy must be perpendicular to the lateral surface and stepwise for stable reattachment — an unstable osteotomy leads to nonunion in 1–5%.</li>
    <li>Detaching obturator externus to gain wider exposure is a catastrophic technical error — it produces head AVN.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Extensile lateral approaches — Olier, Ombrédanne, Colonna, McFarland-Osborne</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Before the modern muscle-sparing intervals were established, orthopaedics used broad lateral approaches with greater trochanter osteotomy for wide circumferential exposure.

**Olier approach.** U-shape around the trochanter apex: gluteus maximus is retracted posteriorly, TFL anteriorly, and a blunt curved instrument is passed beneath gluteus medius and minimus under which the trochanter is osteotomised with a chisel. The external rotators remain attached to the femur or are divided as needed; the osteomuscular flap is reflected proximally and the capsule is exposed. At closure the trochanter is fixed with a screw or wire. In children with an actively growing trochanter the abductor terminal tendons are divided at the apex and reinserted at closure.

**McFarland-Osborne.** Modification — the trochanter is divided intracapsularly with a Gigli saw after anteroposterior arthrotomy — the technique lifts the entire superior capsule together with the osteomuscular flap including the short rotators.

**Ombrédanne approach.** A curved incision between TFL and gluteus medius with a stepwise trochanteric osteotomy and reflection of the trivial osteomuscular flap superiorly. Provides broad access to the joint and the proximal third of the femur — particularly suitable in DDH with a high dislocated head, in tumour resection of the proximal femur, and in severe joint destruction.

**Colonna approach.** A reverse-C incision with anterior convexity, beginning about 3 cm behind ASIS and descending arc-like to 12 cm below the trochanter, followed by detachment of the abductor terminal tendons. The approach is historically linked with the capsuloplasty Colonna described for high dislocations.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Combined approaches — Marino-Zuco, Chaklin-Vreden, Kocher-Gibson</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Marino-Zuco anterolateral approach begins at ASIS, directed toward the greater trochanter, and continues 10–15 cm along the anterolateral thigh. The interval between TFL and sartorius is opened, TFL's proximal insertion is detached osteoplastically, and the middle third of the muscle is traversed cautiously to avoid the superior gluteal nerve.

The Chaklin-Vreden approach <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (combined anterolateral with greater trochanter osteotomy) begins at the middle third of the iliac crest, runs along it to ASIS, then descends to the trochanter, then turns horizontally posteriorly to cross the subtrochanteric region. The interval between TFL and sartorius is opened, the proximal TFL is divided, and the anterior portion of gluteus medius is detached; the trochanter is osteotomised for wide exposure. Vreden describes a variant without the proximal iliac-crest portion of the incision and without elevating muscles from the iliac bone — a compromise between exposure and sparing.

The Kocher-Gibson posterolateral approach begins 6–7 cm anterior to PSIS, continues anteriorly and inferiorly over the anterior border of gluteus maximus toward the anterior trochanter, then distally along the femur 15–17 cm. The iliotibial tract is split longitudinally and the abductor terminal tendons are then divided partially or osteotomically, or the external rotators are detached, depending on the purpose of the approach.

</div>
</details>

<div class="ts-body">

The classification of proximal femoral osteotomies follows the type of correction (varising, valgising, derotational, flexion, extension, lengthening/shortening, three-dimensional), the level (intertrochanteric, subtrochanteric, neck), the fixation method (no internal fixation and cast, blade plate, dynamic hip screw, Holevich compression blade), and the pathology. The modern standard is intertrochanteric osteotomy with stable osteosynthesis, allowing early mobilisation without cast.

**Pauwels varising intertrochanteric osteotomy.** Indicated for congenital subluxation in adults (prophylactic delay of osteoarthritis), coxa valga after Perthes with incongruity, paralytic dislocation for stabilisation, and in children combined with derotation for residual anteversion after DDH treatment. Classical Pauwels removes a wedge with medial base from the distal fragment with an angle equal to the required correction — at the cost of shortening. Alternatively, without wedge removal, the distal fragment is medialised and adducted. Fixation with a 90° AO blade plate provides stability that obviates postoperative casting and allows assisted weight-bearing from day 3. The Holevich-Vladimirov compression blade <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (3 sizes) uses an adjustable L-guide and a compression pin.

**Valgising intertrochanteric osteotomy.** For coxa vara congenita (Bauer) or acquired (malunited fractures, femoral neck nonunion, post-AVN deformity). In osteoarthritis it reduces limp from a positive Trendelenburg sign. In congenital coxa vara at age 4–5 years, the Holevich reverse blade plate <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> with a predetermined lateral obtuse angle (complementary to the desired valgisation — 150° for 30° valgisation) provides stable fixation without cast.

**Kirmisson and Putti derotational osteotomy.** For excessive anteversion (often after DDH treatment) with malalignment in neutral. The classical technique places two stout pins in the proximal fragment in the sagittal plane and two more in the distal fragment, rotated internally by the required amount of derotation. The modern standard in children uses intramedullary fixation with a short Küntscher nail or a blade plate with the Holevich-Vladimirov compression blade — no cast required.

**McMurray osteotomy.** For femoral neck nonunion without AVN — combines medialisation and valgisation of the distal fragment. The oblique cut begins below the greater trochanter base and is directed medially and proximally to the neck base; the distal fragment is medialised and abducted. The amount of valgisation depends on the nonunion line's inclination — Pauwels biomechanical principle (conversion of shear into compression).

**Three-dimensional Imhäuser correction (Holevich-Dzherov modification).** For SCFE with displacement above 5/10 — correction in three planes (flexion/extension, derotation). For smaller displacements (under 5/10) stabilisation may be achieved with 9–10 Kirschner wires that provide relative stability and accelerate physeal closure.

**Sugioka rotational osteotomy.** Transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy in early-stage AVN — anterior or posterior rotation of the head such that the preserved viable portion is brought into the weight-bearing zone. Critical condition: MFCA preservation (by analogy with the Ganz principle).

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>In derotational osteotomy for residual anteversion at least 10–15° of active internal rotation must remain — exceeding this leads to fixed external-rotation contracture.</li>
    <li>McMurray valgisation converts the shear forces of a Pauwels III fracture into compression — the biomechanical basis for nonunion consolidation.</li>
    <li>The Holevich-Vladimirov compression blade is a stable substitute for the classical AO blade plate with a smaller implant mass — particularly useful in the paediatric setting.</li>
  </ul>

<details>
<summary>Hip arthrodesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Arthrodesis is indicated in severe unilateral disease with destruction of joint surfaces and substantial loss of motion, in contractures with severe pain unresponsive to other procedures, and to stabilise the limb after flaccid paralysis. Best results in young patients (under 30) with good spinal mobility and healthy adjacent joints. Over 60 — relative contraindication because of spinal stiffness. Functional position: 25–30° flexion, 0° (or slight 5°) adduction, 5–10° external rotation.

**Classification.** Intra-articular (no metal fixation, non-compressive, compressive); extra-articular (ilio-femoral, ischio-femoral); mixed; atypical and corrective.

**Closed intra-articular Vreden arthrodesis <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Lateral incision over the greater trochanter; vastus lateralis split longitudinally; a guide is passed through the trochanter, neck, and head (as in percutaneous neck fracture fixation); a cylindrical reamer creates a channel passing through the acetabulum into which a tibial bone graft is driven.

**Open intra-articular Watson-Jones arthrodesis (with pin).** Anterior iliofemoral approach with head dislocation, removal of articular cartilage from head and acetabulum, and reduction; a Smith-Petersen pin is introduced through a lateral incision over the trochanter so that it passes through the trochanter, neck, head, and the thicker upper acetabulum, 2–3 cm into the pelvis. The Charnley open arthrodesis (so-called centrally dislocated arthrodesis) embeds the head into a specially shaped opening in the acetabular floor with Holmgren compression.

**Holevich compression arthrodesis <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Special instrumentation — pins of various lengths (15–22 cm), centring instrument, 8 mm reamer, and tensioner — to place two pins: one through trochanter, neck, and head into the ilium; the second more proximal and crossed with the first. Average 14–16 kg compression and stability are achieved, allowing standing on day 5–7 without cast. Pins are removed at 6–8 months.

**Müller compression arthrodesis.** Lateral approach with abductor detachment and passage between TFL and gluteus medius, preparation of the neck and head, and placement of a special compression plate — standing with crutches from day 5 and full weight-bearing after month 3.

**Ilizarov external fixator compression arthrodesis <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Bilateral bone traction with rings — two distal rings on the femur and two proximal (one with wires through the iliac bone above the acetabulum, the second through the iliac crest below ASIS) — with rods for compression — particularly useful with substantial bone loss or active infection.

**Extra-articular ilio-trochanteric arthrodesis.** Albee (8 cm bone graft from the iliac wing's outer wall driven into the trochanteric fossa); Hass (the anterior 2/3 of the trochanter with 5 cm of femoral cortex driven into an iliac "window"); Hibbs (analogous to Hass but with 180° rotation of the trochanter); Ghormley (massive bone block from the full thickness of the iliac wing into a slot from the trochanter apex along the superior neck, head, and acetabular rim); Wilson (sagittal split of the trochanter apex, the outer lamina of the anterior third of the iliac wing turned inferiorly); Badgley (wide cut from the anterior third of the iliac crest with the full bone thickness inserted into a deep slot). The closed tunnel extra-articular Boychev arthrodesis <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> was developed specifically for tuberculous coxitis in remission: with special instrumentation a channel is made through the ilium, neck, and trochanter into which a graft is placed without opening the joint.

**Ischio-femoral arthrodesis.** Trumble (posterior approach detaching the gluteus maximus terminal tendon and a bone graft between ischial tuberosity and femur); Kirkaldy-Willis (anteroinferior posterior synthesis); Brittain (extra-articular arthrodesis combined with a high supporting osteotomy).

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Functional position for arthrodesis: 25–30° flexion, 0–5° adduction, 5–10° external rotation. More flexion (>40°) → lumbar hyperlordosis; abduction → "short" limb; rotation >15° → gait asymmetry.</li>
    <li>Over 60 years arthrodesis gives unacceptable function due to spinal stiffness — relative contraindication.</li>
    <li>The closed tunnel Boychev arthrodesis is the technique of choice for tuberculous coxitis in remission because it minimises the risk of reactivation.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Biological interposition arthroplasty — historical basis.** Aalbee 1885; Murphy and Lexer (pedicled fascia-fat graft); Hackenbrock (importance of postoperative distraction); Balchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (iliotibial-tract fascial graft 15×8 cm, wrapped in a moist compress; maintenance of 1 cm distraction between acetabulum and fascia-covered head for 20–30 days with an Ilizarov ring). The technique is largely abandoned in modern practice, replaced by arthroplasty.

**Vitallium cup (Smith-Petersen, 1939).** Metal cup placed over the spherically reshaped head as an interposition between head and acetabulum — historical significance as the predecessor of hip resurfacing.

**Moore and Thompson hemiarthroplasty.** Monopolar replacement (F. A. Moore, 1945). Moore-Austin (long stem with fenestrations and collar) — mechanical fixation; Thompson (shorter stem with collar) — typically cemented. Indications today: femoral neck fractures in patients over 75 with limited life expectancy; nonunion or AVN with intact acetabulum.

**Total hip arthroplasty — historical evolution.** Wiles (1936) — the first attempt at full replacement; McKee (1951) — metal-on-metal articulation; Sivash (1958) — combined stem and cup in a single standard prosthesis widely used in the Eastern Bloc; Charnley (1962) — the foundational low-friction arthroplasty concept: small 22 mm metal head against a high-density polyethylene cup, fixed with acrylic cement; Müller — self-fixing acetabular cup and orientation standards (45° inclination, 10–20° anteversion); Ring — slotted metal acetabular shell for bone integration; Wagner — conical stem (Zweimüller-type) and later hip resurfacing (1978).

**Bulgarian Gerchev complex prosthesis <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Two interchangeable femoral stems (for press-fit and cemented fixation), 32 mm head, three neck lengths; two acetabular variants (titanium spiral-toothed shell for press-fit, polyethylene shell for cemented); polyethylene articular liner exchangeable through a small procedure. The interchangeability allows hybrid fixation according to bone quality. The approach is most often anterolateral or posterolateral (after Holevich) with osteoplastic trochanteric osteotomy.

**Holevich modifications for THA in adult DDH <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** For a high dislocated head in the adult: the proximal femoral fragment is deperiosted distal-to-proximal; iliopsoas is detached and repaired; the acetabular socket (hypoplastic, triangular, and anteverted) is reamed to the inner lamella with small reamers; with a shallow socket a portion of the reshaped femoral head is fixed above it with compression screws as augmentation; a rectangular stem (Sivash-prosthesis modification) is inserted.

**Andreev-Gerchev-Tsachev wide proximal-femoral resection <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** For tumour or traumatic defect up to 15 cm below the trochanter — a special femoral stem with a conical proximal part and a set of collars of various diameters fills the defect. The standard "Etropal DMP" acetabular cup is fixed press-fit or cemented.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Charnley low-friction (1962): 22 mm metal head, UHMWPE cup, acrylic cement — basis for the DeLee-Charnley (cup, 3 zones) and Gruen (stem, 7 zones) radiographic systems.</li>
    <li>Cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) reduces wear from 0.1 to 0.01 mm/year — standard pairing with ceramic or metal heads.</li>
    <li>The Bulgarian Gerchev prosthesis with interchangeable stem and cup permits hybrid fixation (cemented femur, press-fit acetabulum) — particularly useful in the older patient with poor femoral bone and a sound acetabular socket.</li>
  </ul>

<details>
<summary>DDH surgery in the child</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical Bulgarian and Russian schools describe a systematic operative approach in paediatric DDH according to age, dislocation severity, acetabular development, and secondary proximal-femoral deformity.

**Derotational and varising intertrochanteric osteotomy in children.** Intertrochanteric osteotomy with the Holevich-Vladimirov compression blade <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> or AO blade plate is performed with the patient lateral or supine on a fracture table; correction is stable and a cast is not required. For correction of the neck-shaft angle, the Iliev apparatus <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> provides an external fixator anchored to the trochanteric mass and the subtrochanteric femur; an intertrochanteric osteotomy with an oscillating saw allows precise tuning of anteversion and valgus via a rotation mechanism.

**Albee acetabuloplasty.** Horizontalises the acetabular roof in persistent dysplasia (>3 years). The anterior iliofemoral approach is used; the chisel enters the bone near and parallel to the roof so that the distal fragment containing the roof can be easily bent down; the defect is filled with allograft.

**Lance, Pemberton, Gill acetabuloplasty.** Lance cuts a thin chisel through the outer cortex of the iliac wing just above the capsular attachment — the conceptual predecessor of Pemberton. The Pemberton pericapsular osteotomy (P. A. Pemberton, 1965) relies on Y-cartilage plasticity to rotate the entire roof anteriorly and laterally — greatest effect between 2 and 4 years; after age 12 (Y-cartilage closure) it is no longer applicable.

**Salter innominate osteotomy.** R. B. Salter (1961) — the standard procedure for correcting acetabular orientation after closed DDH treatment with persistent malalignment. Age range: 18 months to 6 years (applicable up to 8–10); also feasible in adults combined with other procedures. A Gigli saw or osteotome passes through the full iliac width from the greater sciatic notch toward AIIS; the distal fragment is rotated anteriorly, inferiorly, and laterally, fixed with a triangular iliac-crest graft secured with two Kirschner wires.

**Dega osteotomy.** W. Dega (1969) — an oblique cut starting anteriorly 1 cm above the acetabular rim and ending in the acetabular notch; the distal fragment with the acetabulum rotates inferolaterally, fixed with interposed bone graft; differs from Salter by the incomplete cut (inner cortex remains intact).

**Steel triple osteotomy.** H. H. Steel (1973) — for instability from dysplasia in an older child or young adult (>10 years). Begins with ischial osteotomy, followed by pubic and iliac osteotomies; the entire acetabular fragment (freed from the pelvic ring) is rotated for optimal coverage.

**Chiari osteotomy.** K. Chiari (1955) — medialises the femur, favourably changing the inner/outer lever ratio and improving biomechanics. Indications: dysplasia with uncovered head in a child over 6 years, before adult osteoarthritis. Osteotomy just above the joint capsule, starting from the greater sciatic notch; the outer cortex is cut with a chisel and the inner cortex with a Gigli saw passed through the greater sciatic notch. The distal fragment is medialised.

**Shelf acetabuloplasty — Manfrini, Ivanov, Dickson.** When the supporting surface is inadequate without the option of roof rotation — osteoplastic shelf extension with a pedicled bone graft. Manfrini — outer iliac cortex graft folded over the capsule. Ivanov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> — a 1.5×5–6 cm graft near the greater sciatic notch; the inner cortex is cut with a chisel without full detachment, the graft is rotated outward over the capsule with bending of the outer cortex and fixed with a Kirschner wire. Dickson — for high dislocation, mobilisation of the capsule from the iliac wing without opening and creation of a new shelf above it.

**Supporting (palliative) osteotomies for high dislocated hip.** Palliative procedures to improve static and gait. Classical names: Kirmisson (high valgising), Froehlich (acetabular level), Schanz (ischial-tuberosity level), and Bayer (bifurcation). Milch combines valgisation with support from the ischial tuberosity.

**Open reduction with capsuloplasty (Colonna and Zahradníček).** In high dislocations in children over 5–6 years with major anatomic deformity — the joint capsule is used as an interposition between the new acetabulum and the reduced head. Colonna — preliminary preparation with prolonged traction; after the head is lowered to acetabular level, open reduction using the capsule as a covering. The Colonna-Zahradníček combination (introduced in Bulgaria by Balchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>) combines with intertrochanteric osteotomy to correct anteversion and valgus. The Ignatov modification <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> — a transverse osteotomy distal to the lesser trochanter with superposition of the two fragments and rotation for derotation — reduces the risk of disturbing proximal-fragment blood supply.

**Somerville limbectomy.** In incomplete reduction in children up to 1.5–2 years — resection of the inverted limbus, confirmed preoperatively by arthrography.

<summary>Traumatic dislocations and fractures</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Traumatic hip dislocation.** Closed reduction under general anaesthesia with muscle relaxation immediately after diagnosis (within 6 hours to optimise AVN risk) — Allis or Stimson technique. After reduction — radiographs in two projections (AP and the Zhelev semi-profile <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> — half-profile with the beam angled 45° to visualise the posterior acetabular rim).

For irreducible dislocation: a Moore posterior approach with blunt gluteus maximus splitting; sciatic nerve identification and protection; identification of the torn capsule; reduction. For unreduced posterior wall fracture: fixation with two screws. For unreduced head fracture (Pipkin type): anterolateral approach with rectus femoris division; screw fixation of the fragment with countersunk heads, or excision for a small non-weight-bearing fragment. For central dislocation — the Tenev technique <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> uses a special 12–13 cm two-thread screw through the trochanter into the head, with external fixation in a cast to lateralise the head.

**Femoral neck fractures.** The adduction (varus, Pauwels III) type always requires surgery because conservative treatment carries a 100% nonunion risk. The classical Smith-Petersen trifin nail (1937); Scaglietti-Putti screws (one or two cancellous screws); three cannulated screws in a perfect triangle; dynamic hip screw (DHS) for stable intertrochanteric fractures; the Holevich compression blade <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> with blade plate. For nonunion without AVN — McMurray valgising intertrochanteric osteotomy or Pauwels Y-osteotomy (two-wedge); for segmental necrosis — Meyers vastus lateralis muscle-pedicled bone graft.

**Pertrochanteric fractures.** The Kozhuharov classical blade plate <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (Bulgarian school) — pin in the neck and plate down the femur with 4 screws; reduction on a fracture table. The dynamic hip screw (DHS) — the gold standard for stable pertrochanteric fractures. The Ender method (1969) — three thin flexible intramedullary nails retrograde; minimal operative trauma but a high rate of distal migration. The gamma nail and modern cephalomedullary nails (PFNa, InterTan) are biomechanically superior in subtrochanteric and reverse-oblique intertrochanteric fractures.

In children, femoral neck fractures are fixed with Kirschner wires because of the redislocation risk in cast; iatrogenic fracture during placement of the third screw at the cortical position in osteoporotic bone is a real risk.

    <li>DHS — standard for stable intertrochanteric fractures; cephalomedullary nails (PFNa, InterTan, gamma) — standard for reverse-oblique and subtrochanteric.</li>
    <li>In children, femoral neck fractures are fixed with Kirschner wires and a cast; wires are removed at 3 months.</li>
  </ul>

<details>
<summary>Soft-tissue procedures — tenotomies, myotomies, muscle transpositions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Hip tenotomies.** Subcutaneous tenotomy of the superficial flexors (sartorius and TFL) — for isolated flexion contracture. Adductor tenotomy — for adduction contracture (cerebral palsy, fixed adduction in osteoarthritis); combined with anterior-branch obturator neurectomy (Stoffel) in spastic contracture from CP. Beck iliopsoas tenotomy — anterior Hueter-type incision; the iliopsoas tendon is divided with a curved instrument near the lesser trochanter; the detached tendon is lifted proximally and sutured to the anterolateral capsule to partially preserve function. Durham myotomy of the internal rotators — oblique skin incision over the anterior trochanter, division of fascia lata and the tendinous extension of TFL with the limb externally rotated.

**Voss method — creating a "hanging" hip.** Decompressive arthroplasty — multistep soft-tissue release in advanced osteoarthritis with contraindications for arthroplasty and unsuitability for arthrodesis. Adductor, abductor, and external-rotator detachment; division of the rectus femoris reflected head; never division of gluteus maximus. The Brand modification adds a selective capsulotomy. Results show rare fusion but most joints become painless and stable with small flexion-extension range — so-called fibrous ankylosis.

**Muscle transpositions for flaccid paralysis.** For flexor paralysis — Sauter external oblique transposition. For gluteus maximus (extensor) paralysis — Ober (erector spinae with double fascia-lata band to the trochanter); Lange (modification); Spira (biceps femoris and semitendinosus to gluteus maximus). For abductor paralysis (gluteus medius and minimus) — E. Lange technique; Thomas-Thompson-Straub (external oblique for abduction); Berthon (partial gluteus maximus with TFL); Holevich <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (transposition of TFL on a neurovascular pedicle preserving vascularisation and innervation); Ober-Barr (two thirds of erector spinae freed from its attachments); Chaklin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (Ober modification with smaller posterior skin incision and partial mobilisation of TFL with an iliotibial-tract strip); Mustard (iliopsoas for active abduction); Sharrard (iliopsoas across the iliac crest to the posterolateral trochanter).

**Holevich-Dzherov-Vladimirov tape stabilisation <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** For paralytic pelvic obliquity — a Dacron tape passed through two channels in the iliac crest and an opening in the lower trochanter, with cross-weaving through gluteus maximus — achieves 14–16 kg of tension under standard tightening and does not require prolonged cast immobilisation. An analogous Vladimirov-Holevich tape <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> for hip extensor paralysis passes paravertebrally beneath erector spinae and through a trochanteric bone channel with cross-weaving through gluteus maximus.

**Snapping hip.** External coxa saltans — fibrous thickenings along the posterior border of gluteus maximus or the anterior border of TFL fascia are excised stepwise; the thickened posterior iliotibial tract is split transversely and a fascial Z-flap or duplication is formed. Internal coxa saltans — from iliopsoas over the iliopectineal eminence.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Hip arthroscopy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Hip arthroscopy has established itself as a platform for treating femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), labral tears, and other intra-articular hip pathology.

**Portals.** Anterolateral — approximately 1–2 cm anterior and 2 cm distal to the trochanter apex; the principal viewing portal. Anterior — placed under fluoroscopic control with attention to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. Posterolateral — posterior to the trochanter; beware of the proximity of the sciatic nerve. Mid-anterior — accessory portal for specific procedures.

**Compartments.** Arthroscopic examination of the central compartment (intra-articular) and peripheral compartment (between capsule and neck) requires hip distraction (usually with a fracture table) for access to the central compartment.

**Indications.** FAI (cam, pincer, combined), labral tears, loose bodies, synovitis, focal chondral pathology, septic arthritis for drainage and lavage, early Perthes for arthrolysis.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Excessive traction (>50 lb for more than 2 hours) → pudendal or peroneal neuropathy — the standard is <2 hours of traction and strategic traction "breaks" in prolonged procedures.</li>
    <li>Joint capsule perforation during peripheral-compartment work → fluid extravasation into the retroperitoneum (rare but potentially catastrophic); monitor abdominal pressure in long procedures.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Salvage reconstructions and disarticulation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Colonna resection arthroplasty.** Coverage of the proximal femoral end with a gluteal muscle envelope. An incision between sartorius and TFL with abductor and external-rotator detachment from the trochanter and a T-cut of the capsule; the head is excised, the neck is divided at its base, and the cut surface is covered with an inverted capsular flap.

**Girdlestone resection arthroplasty (1928).** Definitive removal of head and neck without replacement, leaving the limb to heal as a fibrous ankylosis in shortened position. Indications: severe infection after arthroplasty (before reimplantation in a two-stage revision), tuberculous coxitis with full destruction, severe failed reconstructions. The limb is stable but shortened 4–6 cm.

**Wagner cementless resurfacing (1978).** Hip resurfacing — the head is reshaped to spherical with a conical reamer and a metal cup is placed over it, articulating with a polyethylene or metal acetabular cup. Indications: young patient with AVN without extensive neck involvement, early hip osteoarthritis in the young patient with good bone quality. Complication: residual neck fracture. The modern equivalent is the Birmingham Hip Resurfacing (McMinn) — restricted use in Europe due to metal-ion concerns.

**Hip disarticulation.** For malignant tumours involving the proximal third (osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, metastatic with pathological fracture), in severe crush injuries involving the whole limb, and in life-threatening infectious complications. A racket-shaped skin incision; piriformis and rectus femoris are detached; iliopsoas is divided near its insertion; the adductors and ischio-tibial muscles are divided near their insertions; the femoral neurovascular bundle is individually ligated (artery, then vein, then nerve); the sciatic nerve is identified medial to the ischial tuberosity and divided with sharp scissors after local anaesthetic infiltration to avoid neuroma. Prosthesis fitting — possible after uneventful healing, no earlier than 2–3 months.

## Outcomes and Complications
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Complications of hip approaches vary by anatomic region and procedure type. This brief overview summarises the typical problems by approach.

**Anterior (Smith-Petersen / DAA):** lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury (meralgia paresthetica: 20–80% transient, 5–10% permanent); wound complications of the proximal incision (in skin folds); periprosthetic femoral fracture during stem insertion (more frequent in DAA than in the posterior approach because of more difficult femoral exposure); component malposition due to restricted femoral visualisation; rarely — femoral nerve injury during extended medial dissection.

**Anterolateral (Watson-Jones):** abductor dysfunction with partial gluteus medius detachment; superior gluteal nerve injury during extended proximal dissection.

**Direct lateral (Hardinge):** superior gluteal nerve injury (with extended proximal dissection >5 cm); transient abductor weakness in 4–20%; permanent abductor weakness and Trendelenburg gait with denervation; trochanteric pain and persistent limp.

**Posterior (Moore / Kocher-Langenbeck):** sciatic nerve injury (1–3% in elective procedures, higher after reduction of posterior dislocations); posterior dislocation of the hip prosthesis (historically 3–5%, reduced to below 1% with meticulous capsular reconstruction and 36 mm heads); heterotopic ossification (more frequent than after other approaches); femoral head AVN with obturator externus and capsular-reflection detachment.

**Ganz trochanteric flip:** greater trochanter nonunion (1–5%); heterotopic ossification; abductor weakness with improper trochanter reattachment.

**Medial (Ludloff):** obturator nerve injury (anterior branch); adductor weakness; restricted access to the acetabular roof.

**Arthroscopy:** pudendal or peroneal neuropathy with excessive traction; joint capsule perforation with fluid extravasation; iatrogenic chondral injury; persistent pain from inadequately treated FAI.

**General thromboembolic prophylaxis.** Hip surgery carries a high VTE risk (without prophylaxis — 40–60% DVT, 4–7% symptomatic PE). The standard is LMWH (enoxaparin 40 mg/24 h) or direct oral anticoagulants (rivaroxaban, apixaban) for 14–35 days postoperatively after total arthroplasty, combined with mechanical prophylaxis (compression stockings or intermittent pneumatic compression) intraoperatively and until mobilisation.

## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the orthopaedic school in Bulgaria; the eponymous USBALO "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curricular reference and contains detailed descriptions of the classical hip approaches in the Bulgarian tradition. The classical five-group taxonomy of approaches (anterior, lateral, posterior, medial, combined) with defined anatomic intervals is the basis of the operative standard. The closed-tunnel extra-articular Boychev arthrodesis was developed specifically for tuberculous coxitis in remission: with special instrumentation a channel is made through the ilium, neck, and trochanter into which a graft is placed without opening the joint — the technique minimises the risk of reactivating the specific process.

**Holevich <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** A multi-faceted school in proximal femoral surgery. The Holevich-Vladimirov compression blade (3 sizes — wide, medium, narrow) with adjustable L-guide and compression pin for varising intertrochanteric osteotomy — achieves compression without a rigid plate and without postoperative cast. The Holevich reverse blade plate for valgising osteotomy in congenital coxa vara at age 4–5 with a predetermined lateral obtuse angle (complementary to the desired valgisation — 150° for 30°). The Holevich compression arthrodesis with two crossed pins (15–22 cm) and tensioner — 14–16 kg compression, standing on day 5–7 without cast; pins removed at 6–8 months. The closed intra-articular Holevich arthrodesis through an anterior Smith-Petersen approach with partial decortication of head and acetabulum and a compression screw — for hemiankylosis or delayed union. Holevich modifications for THA in adult DDH with a high dislocated head: proximal-fragment deperiostation, acetabular reaming to the inner lamella, augmentation with reshaped femoral head, rectangular stem (Sivash modification). Pedicled TFL transposition for abductor paralysis. Three-dimensional Imhäuser correction for SCFE in the Holevich-Dzherov modification.

**Vladimirov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Holevich-Vladimirov compression blade; fibula-head autograft for filling a defect between acetabulum and proximal femur after extensive tumour or infection resection. The Vladimirov-Holevich tape stabilisation for hip extensor paralysis — paravertebrally beneath erector spinae and through a bone channel in the lower trochanter with cross-weaving through gluteus maximus.

**Tenev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Technique for central hip dislocation (luxatio coxae centralis): a special 12–13 cm two-thread screw passed through the trochanter into the head with external cast fixation to lateralise the head. Left-sided extraperitoneal lumbar approach for the thoracolumbar junction (see also Topic Anatomy-1) with medial retraction of quadratus lumborum.

**Zhelev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Posterior approach in the shape of an inverted question mark — combines a posterior skin incision beginning behind ASIS with an arc-like inferoposterior course at the midpoint of the trochanter–iliac crest line; broad exposure of the joint together with the posterior acetabular column and the sciatic nerve — particularly valuable for simultaneous work on the posterior column and the hip joint (fracture-dislocations involving the posterior column). The Zhelev semi-profile radiograph with the beam angled 45° visualises the posterior acetabular rim.

**Gerchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian complex hip prosthesis with two interchangeable femoral stems (for press-fit and cemented fixation), 32 mm head, three neck lengths; two acetabular variants (titanium spiral-toothed shell for press-fit and polyethylene shell for cemented); polyethylene articular liner exchangeable through a small procedure. Hybrid fixation — cemented femur with press-fit acetabulum and vice versa — according to bone quality. The approach is most often anterolateral or posterolateral (after Holevich) with osteoplastic trochanteric osteotomy.

**Andreev-Gerchev-Tsachev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Wide proximal-femoral resection for tumour or traumatic defect up to 15 cm below the trochanter — a special femoral stem with a conical proximal part and a set of collars of various diameters fills the defect; the peripheral part is a standard stem. The standard "Etropal DMP" acetabular cup is fixed press-fit or cemented.

**Iliev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** External fixator for paediatric intertrochanteric osteotomy — anchored to the trochanteric mass and subtrochanteric femur; intertrochanteric osteotomy with an oscillating saw allows precise tuning of anteversion and valgus via a rotation mechanism. Kirschner wires are placed parallel to the desired angle, the correction is fixed with a blade plate.

**Ivanov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Shelf acetabuloplasty with a 1.5×5–6 cm graft near the greater sciatic notch; the inner cortex is cut with a chisel without complete detachment, the graft is rotated outward over the capsule with bending of the outer cortex and fixed with a Kirschner wire — a biomechanically elegant solution without complete osteotomy.

**Ignatov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Colonna-Zahradníček modification in high paediatric DDH — a transverse osteotomy distal to the lesser trochanter with superposition of the two fragments and rotation for derotation — reduces the risk of disturbing proximal-fragment blood supply during the classical sagittal osteotomy.

**Kozhuharov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Blade plate for pertrochanteric fractures (Bulgarian school) — pin in the neck and plate down the femur with 4 screws; reduction on a fracture table; osteosynthesis through a lateral incision 15–20 cm above and below the trochanter.

**Balchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Modified biological interposition arthroplasty — iliotibial-tract fascial graft 15×8 cm, wrapped in a moist compress; a Gigli saw is passed beneath the trochanter and the trochanteric mass is osteotomised; the femoral head's articular surface is covered with the fascial graft fixed with sutures; maintenance of about 1 cm distraction for 20–30 days with an Ilizarov ring. The Balchev mixed arthrodesis — osteotomy of head and neck with preparation of the upper head surface, neck, and antero-posterior groove of the trochanteric fossa, into which vertical bone plates from the iliac wing with outer-lamina cover are inserted. Introduction in Bulgaria of the Colonna-Zahradníček combination.

**Chokanov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Mixed arthrodesis combining intra-articular fusion with iliofemoral bridging in two variants — vertical graft (triangular bone graft) and horizontal graft (rectangular iliac-crest graft wedged into a prepared trochanter-neck slot).

**Kaymakchiev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Modification of percutaneous vertebral biopsy (applicable in paravertebral abscesses around the hip) and instrumented spondylodesis for idiopathic adolescent scoliosis with V. Tanchev.

**Karchinov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Further development of the atypical Küntscher-nail arthrodesis with a special directing apparatus — central nail through the iliac and femoral bones — particularly applicable in head-neck assimilation (as in severe DDH).

**Dial <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Oblique trochanteric osteotomy in reverse-oblique fractures with failure of standard fixation — reorientation provides stable biomechanics.

From the classical Russian and Ukrainian schools, deeply embedded in the Bulgarian curriculum:

**R. R. Vreden <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Closed intra-articular Vreden arthrodesis — lateral incision over the greater trochanter, vastus lateralis split longitudinally, guide through trochanter, neck, and head; a cylindrical reamer creates a channel passing through the acetabulum into which a tibial bone graft is driven. Immobilisation in a spica cast for 3 months. The combined Chaklin-Vreden anterolateral approach with greater trochanter osteotomy.

**V. D. Chaklin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Ober modification with smaller posterior skin incision and partial mobilisation of TFL together with an iliotibial-tract strip for transposition in abductor paralysis.

**Ya. L. Tsivyan <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Wedge osteotomy with application also for the high dislocated hip in the adult.

**A. A. Korzh <sup class="natflag">UA</sup>.** Classical modifications of joint-preserving and reconstructive surgery in the Ukrainian orthopaedic school.

**Yumashev and Furman <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Cylindrical bone trephines with 90° rotation of the graft for greater mechanical stability in arthrodesis and anterior interbody fusion.

**Sivash <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** The first combined standard total hip prosthesis (1958) — stem and acetabulum in a single construct, widely used in the Eastern Bloc; the Sivash concept is the basis for modern modular systems and for the Holevich modification for adult DDH.

**Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** The ring external fixator and the distraction osteogenesis principle — applicable in hip surgery for compression arthrodesis, for preparation of high paediatric DDH via gradual distraction, and for Bogoraz-Iliev segmental hip distraction. Bilateral bone traction with rings (two distal rings on the femur and two proximal — one with wires through the iliac bone above the acetabulum, the second through the iliac crest below ASIS) with rods for compression — particularly useful with substantial bone loss or active infection where internal metal fixation is contraindicated.

## Classical Sources
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">
<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical lineage — hip surgery</strong>
  <p>Hip surgery in its modern sense is a product of the late nineteenth century, when antiseptic wound management on the Listerian frame transformed joint resection and arthrodesis from a life-threatening venture into a standard procedure. The line of evolution:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Hueter 1880</strong> — the anterior anatomic interval in <a href="https://archive.org/details/archivfrklinisc11unkngoog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Langenbeck-Archiv für klinische Chirurgie</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>Lorenz 1895</strong> — closed reduction of DDH (<a href="https://archive.org/details/b21290441" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">1895 monograph</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Hoffa 1905</strong> — operative hip surgery in <a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderortho00hoff" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">*Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie*</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>Ludloff 1908</strong> — the medial approach.</li>
    <li><strong>Smith-Petersen 1917</strong> — modern anterior approach → 1937 trifin nail → 1939 vitallium cup.</li>
    <li><strong>Hibbs 1911 / Albee 1885</strong> — foundation of classical arthrodesis.</li>
    <li><strong>Watson-Jones 1936 → Hardinge 1982 → Ganz 2001</strong> — evolution of lateral and trochanteric-flip approaches with MFCA preservation.</li>
    <li><strong>Charnley 1962 → Müller 1968 → Wagner 1978</strong> — evolution of total hip arthroplasty from low-friction to modern modular systems.</li>
    <li><strong>Salter 1961 → Pemberton 1965 → Dega 1969 → Steel 1973 → Chiari 1955</strong> — evolution of pelvic osteotomies in the child and young adult.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Classical descriptions by approach and technique: Hueter 1880 (anterior interval); Lorenz 1895 (closed reduction); Hoffa 1905 (operative hip surgery); Ludloff 1908 (medial approach); Hibbs 1911 and Albee 1885 (arthrodesis); Smith-Petersen 1917 (anterior approach); Salter 1961 (innominate osteotomy); Pemberton 1965 (pericapsular acetabuloplasty); Dega 1969 (incomplete pelvic osteotomy); Steel 1973 (triple osteotomy); Chiari 1955 (medialising pelvic osteotomy); Imhäuser 1957 (three-dimensional SCFE correction); Sugioka 1972 (transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy); Watson-Jones 1936 (anterolateral approach); Hardinge 1982 (direct lateral); Moore 1957 (southern posterior approach); Kocher-Langenbeck (posterior acetabular); Ganz 2001 (trochanteric flip); McMurray 1939 (valgising for nonunion); Pauwels 1935 (biomechanics and valgising osteotomy); Charnley 1962 (low-friction); Sivash 1958 (combined prosthesis); Wagner 1978 (resurfacing).

Additional classical sources: **Bier / Braun / Kümmell** (1920), *Chirurgische Operationslehre*, vol. 3 — pelvis and lower-limb surgery ([Facsimile](https://archive.org/details/chirurgischeoperationslehre1920v1)). **Langenbeck-Archiv für klinische Chirurgie** (from 1860) — first descriptions of many eponyms ([Series in Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/archivfrklinisc11unkngoog)). **Boycho Boychev**, *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (three-volume, Sofia) — the classical textbook of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school with detailed treatment of hip approaches, arthrodesis, arthroplasty, DDH surgery, and the five-group taxonomy of approaches.
