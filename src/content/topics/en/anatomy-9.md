## Overview

<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The thigh and knee region contains the longest bone in the body (femur), the largest joint (knee), and a complex muscular envelope that drives the principal movements of upright gait. The surgical approaches in this region are built around three anatomical constants: the three fascial compartments of the thigh with their nerves (femoral, obturator, sciatic), two fixed neurovascular "choke points" (the adductor canal proximally and the popliteal fossa distally), and the complex intracapsular architecture of the knee with menisci, cruciate and collateral ligaments. The chapter follows the classical Bulgarian school of Boycho Boychev — the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya and his three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* remain a curricular reference — supplemented by international standards from Hoppenfeld–deBoer–Buckley, Insall & Scott (Surgery of the Knee), Hollinshead, and modern references for ligament and arthroscopic surgery (Strobel, Chhabra–Elliott–Miller). The classical ACL and patellar procedures (Hey-Groves 1917, Macey 1939, Jones 1963, Lemaire 1967, MacIntosh 1972, Landa 1947), the line of total knee arthroplasty (Walldius 1951, Charnley 1958, Insall-Burstein 1974), and arthroscopy following Watanabe (1959), Jackson and Gillquist (1971) trace over a century of surgical evolution that remains part of orthopaedic training in Bulgaria.

### Thigh Compartments

<details id="thigh-compartments">
<summary>Thigh Compartments</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Marshall Strother, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Thigh_cross_section.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The thigh has three fascial compartments separated by three intermuscular septa (medial, lateral, posterior) attached to the linea aspera and bounded externally by the fascia lata.

**Anterior compartment.** Contains the quadriceps femoris (rectus femoris from the anterior inferior iliac spine; vastus lateralis, medialis and intermedius — arising from the femur), sartorius (from the anterior superior iliac spine to the pes anserinus), iliopsoas (proximally) and pectineus. Innervated by the femoral nerve (L2–L4). The motor branches enter the proximal third of the vasti and divide obliquely along the muscle fibres — longitudinal splitting of the body of vastus medialis or intermedius denervates the peripheral portions and causes fibrosis with postoperative extension or flexion contracture. This anatomical consideration is central to choosing the femoral shaft approach.

**Medial compartment.** Adductor longus, brevis and magnus; gracilis; obturator externus. Innervated by the obturator nerve (L2–L4), with a contribution to the adductor magnus from the tibial portion of the sciatic nerve (hybrid innervation). Function — adduction, flexion and medial rotation of the hip. The adductor magnus is a "border" muscle between the medial and posterior compartments; its posterior (hamstring) part is innervated by the sciatic nerve.

**Posterior compartment.** Hamstrings: biceps femoris (long head from the ischial tuberosity + short head from the linea aspera), semitendinosus and semimembranosus. Innervated by the sciatic nerve — the tibial portion for all but the short head of biceps femoris (from the peroneal portion). Function — knee flexion, hip extension, medial/lateral rotation of the flexed knee.

**Classical Bulgarian topography.** The regio femoris is bounded proximally by the inguinal fold, the gluteal fold and the base of the greater trochanter; distally by ~4 cm above the base of the patella anteriorly and 11–12 cm above the transverse popliteal fold posteriorly — corresponding to the upper margin of the suprapatellar bursa and the inferior aperture of the adductor canal. The anteromedial surface transmits 5–6 branches of the femoral nerve (anterior femoral cutaneous branches); the lateral surface is supplied by the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (L2–L3), which emerges under the inguinal ligament and pierces the sartorius fascia 1–2 cm medial to the anterior superior iliac spine. The great saphenous vein pierces the fascia cribrosa at the saphenous hiatus 3–4 cm distal to the inguinal ligament and 3.5–4.5 cm lateral to the pubic tubercle before joining the femoral vein — constant anatomical relations critical for high ligation and for vein-graft harvest. In the subcutaneous fat just proximal to this junction the superficial epigastric and superficial circumflex iliac arteries branch from the femoral artery (separately or as a common trunk); plastic surgery uses them as axial pedicles for SIEA and DIEP flaps.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Motor branches of the femoral nerve to the vasti enter proximally and divide obliquely — longitudinal splitting of vastus intermedius denervates its lateral portion and produces extension contracture.</li>
    <li>Adductor magnus has hybrid innervation (obturator nerve to the adductor part, tibial portion of the sciatic to the posterior "hamstring" part) — explains the anatomical bridge between the two compartments.</li>
    <li>The short head of biceps femoris is the only posterior-compartment muscle innervated by the peroneal portion of the sciatic — relevant in isolated peroneal injuries.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Femoral Triangle and Adductor Canal

<details id="femoral-triangle">
<summary>Femoral Triangle and Adductor Canal</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Femoral triangle (Scarpa).** Apex pointing distally; superior border — inguinal ligament; lateral — sartorius; medial — adductor longus; floor — iliopsoas laterally and pectineus medially. Contents from lateral to medial follow the NAVEL mnemonic: femoral nerve (outside the femoral sheath, deep to the iliac fascia), femoral artery, femoral vein, empty space (the femoral canal with Cloquet's lymph node), and lymphatic.

**Adductor (Hunter, sub-sartorial) canal.** A tunnel in the middle third of the thigh between vastus medialis (anterolaterally), adductor longus and magnus (medioposteriorly) and the vastoadductor membrane (medial "roof"). Sartorius lies superficially over the canal. Contents — femoral artery, femoral vein (posterior to the artery), saphenous nerve (anterior sensory branch) and the nerve to vastus medialis (motor; exits proximally). The artery leaves the canal through the adductor hiatus (an opening in the adductor magnus tendon at the level of the adductor tubercle) and becomes the popliteal artery — a fixed point explaining its vulnerability in supracondylar fractures and acute knee dislocation.

The femoral artery projects along a line from the midpoint of the inguinal ligament to the adductor tubercle (Gunter–Kronlein line, also called the Pirogoff–Kronlein line). Proximal ligation in the femoral triangle leaves the limb perfused by the deep femoral artery and obturator collaterals; mid-thigh and canal ligation rely on collateral flow from the adductors and the descending genicular artery (off the femoral before the adductor hiatus).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>NAVEL in the femoral triangle: Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space (with Cloquet's node), Lymphatic — lateral to medial.</li>
    <li>The adductor hiatus is a fixed point — it explains why the popliteal artery is vulnerable in supracondylar fractures with posterior displacement and in knee dislocation.</li>
    <li>The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve emerges from the posterior border of sartorius at the level of the patella and crosses the operative field between patella and tibial tuberosity — an obstacle to any parapatellar incision.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Popliteal Fossa

<details id="popliteal-fossa">
<summary>Popliteal Fossa</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray434.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>
  </figcaption>

The popliteal fossa is a rhomboid intermuscular hollow behind the knee. Borders: superolaterally — biceps femoris; superomedially — semitendinosus and semimembranosus; inferiorly — medial and lateral heads of gastrocnemius; floor — popliteal surface of the femur (proximally), posterior capsule with oblique popliteal ligament (continuation of semimembranosus) and arcuate popliteal ligament (at joint line level), and popliteus with the capsule of the proximal tibiofibular joint (distally).

**Neurovascular bundle.** The sciatic nerve usually divides 10–12 cm proximal to the joint line (variant — at sacral level), giving off the tibial nerve (medial and deep) and the common peroneal nerve (lateral, along the medial border of the biceps tendon to the fibular head — the most superficial nerve in the body and the anatomical reason for its traumatic vulnerability). The tibial nerve lies in the central fat between the two heads of gastrocnemius; it gives rise to the medial sural cutaneous nerve. The popliteal artery lies deepest on the joint capsule; over it lies the popliteal vein; superficially the tibial nerve (mnemonic from deep to superficial: AVN). From the popliteal artery arise the superior medial and lateral genicular arteries (obliquely from the epicondyles to the upper patellar poles), the inferior medial and lateral genicular arteries (horizontally at the joint line), the middle genicular artery (straight through the intercondylar notch into the joint, supplying the cruciate ligaments) and branches to both heads of gastrocnemius.

**Bifurcation of the popliteal artery.** The popliteal artery branches into the anterior tibial artery and tibioperoneal trunk 2–3 cm distal to the lower border of popliteus, at the soleal tendinous arch — a fixed point explaining vulnerability in distal tibial procedures and in knee dislocation.

<aside class="callout callout-anatomic-warning">
  <strong>Critical</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The popliteal artery sits "between two fixed points" — the adductor hiatus proximally and the soleal arch distally; this explains why knee dislocation carries a 25–40% risk of vascular injury even after spontaneous reduction and mandates ABI/CTA for any such injury.</li>
    <li>The common peroneal nerve around the fibular head is the most superficial nerve in the body — vulnerable to direct trauma, to retraction of the biceps tendon, to lateral approaches, to cast immobilisation and to dislocation.</li>
    <li>The popliteal artery passes 5–7 mm posterior to the posterior horn of the medial meniscus — a critical vulnerability in classical open meniscectomy and arthroscopic posterior-horn resection.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Bony and Articular Anatomy of the Knee

<details id="knee-bones">
<summary>Bony and Articular Anatomy of the Knee</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-9/knee.svg" data-figure-alt="Knee joint anatomy" aria-label="Enlarge">

    <span class="md-figure-caption">Knee joint: tibiofemoral and patellofemoral articulations; static stabilizers (ACL, PCL, MCL, LCL, menisci) and dynamic ones (quadriceps, hamstrings, popliteus). The layered structure forms the basis of every approach and of systematic arthroscopic evaluation.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · InjuryMap, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Knee_anatomy.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The knee is a complex joint combining three distinct articulations within a common synovial cavity:

**Tibiofemoral joint.** A bicondylar (modified hinge) joint between the distal femur (medial and lateral condyles, separated by the intercondylar notch posteriorly and the trochlear groove anteriorly) and the proximal tibia (medial and lateral plateaus). The medial condyle is larger and extends more distally — explaining the physiological 5–7° valgus tibiofemoral angle. The medial plateau is mildly concave, the lateral convex; this asymmetric congruence is compensated by the menisci and explains the physiological "screw-home" mechanism (terminal external rotation of the tibia in full extension).

**Patellofemoral joint.** Between the posterior articular surface of the patella and the trochlear groove of the distal femur (with a higher lateral wall that resists lateral subluxation). The patella is the largest sesamoid bone in the body, ~5 cm in diameter; its posterior surface is divided by a central ridge into medial and lateral facets, plus a Wiberg "odd" facet medially. Patellar cartilage is 4–5 mm thick — the thickest hyaline cartilage in the body, reflecting the high contact forces (3–7× body weight in deep squatting). Contact zones: 0–20° flexion — lower pole; 20–60° — middle; 60–90° — upper pole; >90° flexion — odd facet articulating with the lateral condyle.

**Proximal tibiofibular joint.** A planar synovial joint between the lateral tibial condyle and the fibular head; though outside the "main" knee capsule, its dislocation is an important differential for lateral knee pain.

**Distal femoral bony landmarks.** Medial and lateral epicondyles — attachment of the collateral ligaments (MCL medially, LCL laterally); adductor tubercle above the medial epicondyle — attachment of adductor magnus and a constant landmark for osteotomy; trochlear groove with higher lateral wall (sulcus angle 138 ± 6°). The anatomical axis of the femur is 6° valgus to the mechanical axis (centre of femoral head to centre of knee) — this determines the 95–97° distal femoral resection in total knee arthroplasty.

**Proximal tibial bony landmarks.** Tibial tuberosity — attachment of the patellar tendon; Gerdy's tubercle on the lateral surface — attachment of the iliotibial tract; intercondylar eminence with anterior (for ACL tibial insertion) and posterior (for PCL) parts; retrograde from the tuberosity, on the anteromedial surface, lies the pes anserinus insertion (sartorius superficial, gracilis middle, semitendinosus posterior).

### Menisci

<details id="menisci">
<summary>Menisci</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/anatomy-9/knee-menisci.png" data-figure-alt="Menisci of the right tibia — superior view" aria-label="Enlarge">

  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Superior view of the tibial plateau: C-shaped medial meniscus (longer, narrower, tightly bound to the deep MCL — therefore more vulnerable in rotation of the fixed foot) and almost circular lateral meniscus with greater mobility, the meniscofemoral ligaments of Humphrey (anterior) and Wrisberg (posterior).</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray349.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The menisci are two fibrocartilaginous wedge-shaped structures (medial and lateral) that distribute load, improve congruence of the articular surfaces, protect the cartilage and contribute to stability. Cross-section — triangular with apex into the central joint cavity and base attached to the capsule.

**Medial meniscus.** C-shaped; the anterior horn attaches to the anterior intercondylar area (before the ACL insertion); the posterior horn — to the posterior intercondylar area (before the PCL); the body is firmly fused with the deep MCL (meniscofemoral and meniscotibial fibres of the coronary ligament) — making the medial meniscus relatively immobile and vulnerable to rotation of a fixed foot (the typical "bucket-handle" mechanism).

**Lateral meniscus.** Nearly circular; much more mobile (3–4× the medial); the popliteus tendon passes through the popliteomeniscal hiatus — the only point where the meniscus is not attached to the capsule. The anterior and posterior horns lie much closer together than the medial, leaving central space for the ACL insertion. The meniscofemoral ligaments of Humphrey (anterior, from the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus to the medial condyle, in front of the PCL) and Wrisberg (posterior, behind the PCL) anchor the lateral meniscus to the medial femoral condyle — a functional relation with the PCL that explains "pseudolaxity" in isolated lateral meniscal avulsion.

**Vascular supply and zones.** The outer 30% (peripheral "red zone") is supplied by perimeniscal arcades from the medial and lateral genicular arteries; the inner 70% (white zone) is avascular. Arnoczky's zonal concept (1982): red–red (outer 25%) — good repair potential, primary indication for meniscorrhaphy; red–white (middle) — borderline; white–white (inner) — unrepairable, indication for partial meniscectomy. Tear classification: vertical-longitudinal ("bucket handle"), horizontal (delamination), radial, flap, complex and root tears. The sagittal "bow-tie sign" (loss of normal continuity of the two horns) on MRI is pathognomonic for a bucket-handle tear.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Arnoczky's 1982 zonal concept — meniscorrhaphy is feasible only in the red zone (outer 30%); partial meniscectomy is indicated for tears in the white zone.</li>
    <li>The popliteus tendon passes through the popliteomeniscal hiatus of the lateral meniscus — the only capsule-free point, explaining the greater mobility of the lateral meniscus.</li>
    <li>Root tears detach the meniscus from its tibial insertion and are functionally equivalent to total meniscectomy — indication for transosseous root repair.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Cruciate and Collateral Ligaments

<details id="cruciate-collateral">
<summary>Cruciate and Collateral Ligaments</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

    <span class="md-figure-caption">Classical anterior view of the right knee with the capsule opened: anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) in the intercondylar notch, medial and lateral menisci, tibial collateral ligament (MCL) and fibular collateral ligament (LCL).</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray348.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL).** Length 32 ± 4 mm, diameter 11 mm; originates from the posteromedial aspect of the lateral femoral condyle (the residual tibial ridge surface) and inserts onto the anteromedial tibial plateau in front of and medial to the intercondylar eminence. Two functional bundles: anteromedial (AM, longer, tight in flexion — primary for anteroposterior stability) and posterolateral (PL, shorter, tight in extension — primary for rotational stability). Blood supply by the middle genicular artery (directly off the popliteal). Primary function: limits anterior translation of the tibia relative to the femur (Lachman test); secondary — rotational stability (pivot shift).

**Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL).** Length 38 mm, diameter 13 mm — thicker than the ACL. Originates from the posteromedial tibial plateau (5–10 mm below the joint line) and inserts onto the anteromedial surface of the medial femoral condyle in the intercondylar notch. Two bundles: anterolateral (AL, larger, tight in flexion) and posteromedial (PM, smaller, tight in extension). Primary function: limits posterior tibial translation (posterior drawer, posterior sag); secondary — rotational stability.

**Medial collateral ligament (MCL).** Trilaminar after Warren–Marshall–Hughston: superficial layer (tibial collateral ligament — the principal structure, 11 cm long, from the medial epicondyle to 4–6 cm below the joint line on the medial tibia, deep to the pes anserinus); middle layer (capsular ligament with deep and superficial meniscofemoral and meniscotibial fibres); and deep layer (capsule). The posterior oblique ligament (POL) — posterior thickening of the medial capsule with fibres directed obliquely from the adductor tubercle to the posteromedial tibial plateau — is a key structure for posteromedial rotational stability. Primary function: valgus stability (mainly superficial layer) and medial rotational stability (POL and posteromedial complex).

**Lateral collateral ligament (LCL).** The fibular collateral ligament — from the lateral epicondyle to the fibular head (7 cm long, cord-like). Unlike the MCL it is extracapsular and not attached to the lateral meniscus (popliteomeniscal hiatus). Primary function: varus stability in extension and flexion.

**Posterolateral corner (PLC).** A complex three-layer structure (Seebacher 1982; Chhabra–Elliott–Miller): superficial layer (iliotibial tract anteriorly and biceps tendon posteriorly); middle layer (quadriceps patellofibular ligament and lateral retinaculum); deep layer (LCL, popliteus with popliteofibular ligament, arcuate ligament, fabellofibular ligament). The PLC is a static and dynamic restraint to varus, external rotational and combined posterolateral translation. Isolated PLC tears produce a positive "external rotation recurvatum test" and a positive dial test (>10° additional external rotation at 30° relative to the contralateral knee, without an increase at 90° = isolated PLC).

**Posteromedial corner (PMC).** A posteromedial "triad": POL (posterior thickening of the medial capsule), oblique popliteal ligament (continuation of semimembranosus to the lateral epicondyle), and the five insertions of semimembranosus (direct tibial, "pars reflexa" to POL, oblique popliteal ligament, lateral tibial and to popliteus). The PMC is a key secondary restraint to anterior translation in an ACL-deficient knee and determines anteromedial rotational instability.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The MCL has three Warren–Marshall layers — superficial (the principal "tibial collateral" structure), middle (capsular) and deep (capsule); the POL is a specialised posterior thickening with a critical role in posteromedial rotational stability.</li>
    <li>PLC triad: LCL, popliteus and popliteofibular ligament — isolated PLC injury gives +10° external rotation at 30° (dial test) without difference at 90° (which would imply added PCL injury).</li>
    <li>ACL — two bundles (AM tight in flexion, PL in extension); double-bundle reconstruction theoretically restores rotational stability, but meta-analyses do not show a convincing clinical advantage over single-bundle reconstruction in standard cases.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Posterolateral and Posteromedial Corner

<details id="posterolateral-corner">
<summary>Posterolateral and Posteromedial Corner</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The three-layer concept of Seebacher–Inglis–Marshall–Warren (1982), refined by Hughston and by Chhabra and colleagues, divides the medial and lateral soft tissues into three layers for safe operative dissection:

**Medial side — three layers.**
- *Layer I* — sartorius fascia (superficial); transmits the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve.
- *Layer II* — superficial MCL; pes anserinus runs between layers I and II.
- *Layer III* — joint capsule with deep meniscofemoral and meniscotibial fibres, continuing posteriorly into the POL.

**Lateral side — three layers.**
- *Layer I* — iliotibial tract anteriorly and superficial layer of the biceps tendon posteriorly; the common peroneal nerve runs deep to the superficial biceps layer.
- *Layer II* — quadriceps retinaculum anteriorly and patellofemoral ligaments; parapatellar vessels.
- *Layer III* (subdivided into *superficial* and *deep*) — *superficial* contains LCL, fabellofibular ligament; *deep* — popliteus tendon and popliteofibular ligament, arcuate ligament, lateral meniscus and capsule.

Surgical exposure of the LCL–popliteus "triangle" for acute PLC reconstruction requires the plane between layers I and II and identification of the "peroneal nerve corridor" (1 cm proximal to the joint line along the medial border of the biceps tendon).

</div>
</details>

### Patellofemoral Anatomy

<details id="patellofemoral">
<summary>Patellofemoral Anatomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

The patella increases the mechanical lever of the quadriceps tendon by ~50%, amplifying extension efficiency. Wiberg (1941, types I–III) and Baumgartl (type IV) classification: type I — equal medial and lateral facets; type II — smaller medial, larger lateral; type III — pronounced lateral, minimal medial facet (Wiberg III and IV predispose to lateral patellar subluxation). Q-angle (Brattström 1964): the angle between the line from ASIS to the centre of patella and from patella centre to tibial tuberosity; norm 14° in men, 17° in women; >20° predisposes to lateral patellar instability.

Dynamic stabilisers: vastus medialis obliquus (VMO; fibres at ~50–55° to the femoral shaft axis; the only medial dynamic stabiliser) and vastus lateralis. Static stabilisers: trochlear groove (bony geometry with a higher lateral wall), medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL — described as a discrete structure by Warren and Marshall 1979; the primary static restraint to lateral patellar translation; inserts at Schöttle's point on the medial epicondyle), medial patellotibial and medial patellomeniscal ligaments.

Patellar height: patella alta (Insall–Salvati ratio > 1.2 — patellar tendon ÷ patellar diagonal) — predisposes to instability and patellar pain; patella baja (< 0.8) — often post-traumatic or following distal tuberosity transfer. TT–TG distance (tibial tuberosity to trochlear groove on axial CT or MRI) > 20 mm is an indication for distal medialisation of the tibial tuberosity (Elmslie–Trillat).

</div>

## Biomechanics

<section class="topic-section" id="biomechanics">
<details>
<summary>Biomechanics</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The knee allows flexion–extension (0–140°) with coupled rotation. "Screw-home" mechanism: terminal external rotation of the tibia 5–10° in the last 20° of extension — driven by the asymmetric femoral geometry (longer medial condyle), passively constrained by the ACL and actively reversed by popliteus at the initiation of flexion ("releasing" mechanism). Rotation in the flexed knee: 0° in full extension, up to 45° (internal and external combined) in 90° flexion.

**Tibiofemoral kinematics.** In flexion the lateral condyle translates posteriorly more than the medial (rolling-and-gliding sequentially constrained by the menisci and by the ACL). In the normal knee the centre of rotation shifts posteriorly with flexion (femoral roll-back), increasing the quadriceps lever and explaining the discomforting weakness in deep squatting after PCL rupture — loss of posterior roll-back with anterior tibial sliding.

**Femoro-tibial angle.** The anatomical femoral axis is 6° valgus to the mechanical axis; total femoro-tibial angle is 5–7° valgus — individually variable and determining medial or lateral compartment load. Mechanical alignment (HKA — hip–knee–ankle angle): varus HKA (>3°) increases medial loading with progression to medial gonarthrosis; valgus HKA — lateral compartment. Posterior tibial slope is normally 7°; increase >12° predisposes to anterior translation and ACL loading.

**Patellofemoral kinematics.** Contact forces in walking 0.5× body weight; stair climbing 3×; deep squatting 7–8×. The contact zone progressively moves from the lower pole (10–30° flexion), through the middle zone (45–60°), to the upper pole (90°), and for a poorly placed component at arthroplasty — to the lateral Wiberg "odd" facet in deep flexion. Q-angle vectors drive lateral patellar translation, opposed by MPFL (static) and VMO (dynamic).

**Meniscal biomechanics.** In full extension the menisci transmit ~50% of axial load; at 90° flexion ~85%. Total meniscectomy increases contact stress 2–3× — the anatomical basis of the classical Fairbank cascade (1948) of radiographic changes after total medial meniscectomy: joint-line narrowing, marginal osteophytes, cystic change and progression to gonarthrosis within 15–20 years.

<aside class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>Note</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>"Screw-home" — terminal external rotation of the tibia 5–10° in the last 20° extension; popliteus is the "key" releasing rotation at the initiation of flexion.</li>
    <li>Patellofemoral contact forces: 0.5× walking, 3× stair, 7–8× deep squat — explains the clinical predisposition of stair-and-squat patients to anterior knee pain.</li>
    <li>Menisci transmit 50–85% of axial load; total meniscectomy doubles contact stress (Fairbank cascade 1948).</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Classification of Approaches

<section class="topic-section" id="classification">
<details>
<summary>Classification of Approaches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Approaches in the thigh-and-knee region are classified along three intersecting axes.

**By location.** Approaches to the femoral shaft (anterior, anterolateral, lateral, posterolateral, posterior); approaches to the proximal femur (with the trochanter); approaches to the distal femur and supracondylar zone; approaches to the knee joint (medial parapatellar and its minimally invasive variants midvastus and subvastus; lateral parapatellar; classical wide-exposure approaches — Textor, Pütti, Cogswell–Adam, Kocher, Abbott–Carpenter); posterior approaches to the knee (Henderson with Burks–Schaffer modification; Trickey for tibial inlay); dedicated meniscectomy approaches (Erkes, Bruser, Platt, Charnley, Cohen, Smillie); arthroscopic portals.

**By goal.** Decompression (fasciotomy for compartment syndrome); fixation (osteosynthesis of diaphyseal and supracondylar fractures); reconstruction (meniscal repair, ACL and PCL reconstruction, MPFL reconstruction); correction (high tibial and distal femoral osteotomy); replacement (total and unicompartmental arthroplasty); arthrodesis; amputation; biopsy and tumour resection; diagnostic arthroscopy.

**By invasiveness.** Open "extensile" exposure (Textor, Pütti, Hoffa); standard open parapatellar (medial or lateral); muscle-sparing (midvastus, subvastus); minimally invasive (MIPO for distal femur and proximal tibia); arthroscopy-assisted (for tibial plateau fractures, ACL/PCL reconstruction); pure arthroscopic (standard for meniscal surgery, synovectomy, cartilage procedures).

The Hoppenfeld principle that "the penalty of the approach must not exceed the benefit of the procedure" remains directive — minimally invasive variants are not an end in themselves but a choice for appropriate pathology and surgical expertise.

</div>
</details>

## Treatment

<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

### Nonoperative

<details id="nonoperative">
<summary>Nonoperative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Nonoperative management in the thigh and knee covers: skeletal traction and plaster immobilisation of stable fractures; brace immobilisation of isolated ACL, PCL and collateral ligament tears in selected patients; staged rehabilitation for patellofemoral pain; nonoperative management of grade I and II gonarthrosis (physiotherapy, weight control, NSAIDs, intra-articular hyaluronic acid or corticosteroid injections). Skeletal traction through the tibial tuberosity (weight 8–10% of body weight) is the method of choice for temporary immobilisation of closed femoral shaft fractures in patients with contraindications to intramedullary nailing; traction through the femoral condyles is used for central acetabular dislocation and acetabular fractures. The classical Bulgarian recommendation for skeletal traction (Boychev): 3 mm pins through the tibial tuberosity, weight 1/8–1/10 of body weight, bed elevation 25–30°, daily assessment of length and rotation through palpation of the medial malleoli.

For acute isolated MCL tears (grade I–II), an appropriate brace for 4–6 weeks with progressive rehabilitation gives clinical recovery in 90% of cases without residual instability. For isolated PCL tears grade I–II — full extension brace for 4–6 weeks followed by progressive quadriceps rehabilitation (which actively counteracts posterior tibial translation). Isolated ACL tears in low-demand patients over 50 without associated meniscal lesions may be managed nonoperatively with focus on the quadriceps and hamstrings; in active and young patients early reconstruction is recommended.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Tibial-tuberosity skeletal traction — weight 1/8–1/10 of body weight, bed elevation 25–30°; not through the femoral condyles in patients under 14 years (physis).</li>
    <li>Isolated MCL grade I–II tears — excellent nonoperative recovery in 90% of cases after 4–6 weeks of bracing.</li>
    <li>In combined injuries (ACL + MCL + meniscus, "terrible triad") the isolated-MCL nonoperative tactic loses its advantage — early ACL reconstruction is standard.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

### Operative

<details id="operative">
<summary>Operative</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<details>
<summary>Femoral shaft approach (lateral and posterolateral)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Lateral approach.** Standard for compression or LCP plate osteosynthesis. Patient supine with a small bolster under the ipsilateral gluteal region (slight internal rotation of the thigh); skin incision along the anterolateral line of the thigh — from 3–4 cm distal to the greater trochanter to 5 cm above the lateral epicondyle; fascia lata incised along the incision. Internervous plane — between vastus lateralis (femoral nerve) and the iliotibial tract (no motor innervation in this zone). Dissection proceeds along the anterior border of vastus lateralis, with secondary dissection at its posterior border; the muscle is elevated anteriorly subperiosteally off the lateral intermuscular septum. Perforating branches of the profunda femoris (typically 3–5 along the length of the femur) are identified, ligated and divided — they are the most frequent source of retraction bleeding.

**Posterolateral approach.** Skin incision along the same line with more posterior centring; internervous plane between vastus lateralis (femoral) and the hamstrings (tibial portion of the sciatic). Vastus lateralis is reflected anteriorly off the lateral intermuscular septum, without splitting its body — an anatomically sparing approach that preserves motor innervation. The first perforating artery typically lies 5–10 cm distal to the greater trochanter vertex, runs transversely through the septum and must be pre-ligated. Biceps femoris (especially the short head) shields the posterior compartment; the sciatic nerve runs medial to biceps and is not exposed in proper execution, but it must be protected in the distal third (where it has divided into tibial and common peroneal).

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Perforating branches of the profunda femoris cross the lateral intermuscular septum transversely — retraction without pre-ligation produces retrograde "deep field" bleeding that may force enlargement of the approach.</li>
    <li>Splitting vastus intermedius denervates its lateral fibres (motor branches of the femoral nerve are proximal and oblique) — postoperative extension contracture.</li>
    <li>In the distal third the sciatic nerve and its terminal branches lie nearby — local identification of the common peroneal nerve in the posterolateral corner is mandatory.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Classical Küntscher intramedullary nailing</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Küntscher intramedullary nail (Germany, 1939–1940), introduced in Bulgaria in the late 1940s and standardised by the Boychev school, remains the historical reference for interlocking intramedullary nails. Classical indications: transverse or short-oblique fractures of the femoral shaft in the middle third, a large third fragment in comminuted fractures, two-level fractures, and torsional fractures in which stable internal fixation permits early rehabilitation. Operation is recommended 2–3 days after the injury with prior tibial-tuberosity traction to overcome longitudinal displacement.

**Classical (open) technique.** Patient on the unaffected side, secured with thoracic restraints and a belt across the foot and knee of the unaffected limb. The fracture zone is exposed through a posterolateral or mid-lateral approach; the distal fragment is freed and curetted of clot; a guide wire is driven into the cancellous bone of the femoral condyles (in skeletally immature patients, without crossing the distal physis). After radiographic check of the distal fragment length, the proximal fragment is delivered into the wound, the guide wire is advanced into its medullary canal, and is brought out through the tip of the greater trochanter via a small gluteal incision. Over the guide wire the nail is driven, oriented so that the extraction slot faces laterally; the fracture is reduced, aligned and the nail advanced into the distal fragment. For short-oblique fractures the fragments are pre-clamped with bone holders; when stable interfragmentary compression is not possible, a screw or cerclage is added.

Postoperatively, for unstable patterns, the classical recommendation was a plaster boot with an anti-rotational "fin" — a concern eliminated by modern interlocking nails. For femoral pseudarthrosis the Küntscher technique is combined with **Judet decortication** — circular elevation of thin osteoperiosteal lamellae around the pseudarthrosis with a wide chisel, forming a biologically active "osteoperiosteal sleeve" around the ends of both main fragments, followed by canal reaming (12–16 mm), intramedullary nailing and cancellous autografting from the iliac crest or tibial condyles.

Modern intramedullary nailing (antegrade — piriformis or trochanteric entry; retrograde — intercondylar notch entry through a parapatellar arthrotomy) with interlocking screws is the standard for femoral shaft fractures and is covered in Topic Trauma-23.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Femoral lengthening and shortening</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Lengthening.** Classical femoral lengthening by V. N. Bogoraz (USSR, 1948) was performed by oblique or segmental osteotomy followed by prolonged direct traction to achieve the desired lengthening and plaster immobilisation to consolidation — a method superseded in current practice by compression-distraction frames. **The Ilizarov apparatus** (Kurgan, USSR, 1954–1971), applied to the femur with a classical mounting of two 3/4-rings through the proximal and two complete rings through the distal fragment, provides stable axial fixation and controlled distraction; daily distraction 1 mm in four 1/4-mm steps. After the target length is reached the frame is retained until a dense regenerate forms, followed when needed by a plaster cast to complete remodelling. **Wagner lengthening** (Germany, 1971) uses a monolateral distractor with secondary plate fixation and cancellous grafting of the distraction gap; effective up to 6–8 cm, it gives way to circular frames when axial or rotational correction is needed. Later internal (intramedullary) lengtheners — Albizzia (1990s) with mechanical distraction by rotational manipulations, ISKD and PRECICE with magnetic/motorised systems — inherit the principle of Bliskunov (USSR, 1983), the first clinically successful intramedullary distraction system.

Lengthening across the distal physis (chondrodiastasis) in children over 6–8 years uses a circular frame with crossed wires either side of the growth plate and controlled distraction of 0.5–1 mm daily — a technique largely supplanted by distraction epiphysiolysis with modern internal lengtheners.

**Shortening.** Indication — length discrepancy over 2–3 cm; maximum allowable shortening without significant functional loss is 5–6 cm. The classical technique requires a lateral approach through vastus lateralis, segmental resection of the diaphysis with a Gigli saw or oscillating burr, and Küntscher intramedullary nailing (with prior canal reaming to 12–14–16 mm). **The Z-shaped (step-cut) segmental osteotomy of Boychev–Imamaliev** provides larger fragment contact area and better rotational stability for subtrochanteric shortening.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Distal femoral osteotomy and supracondylar corrections</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Corrective supracondylar osteotomy of the femur (varising, valgising or flexion/extension rotational) is indicated for post-traumatic and post-paralytic deformity (genu valgum, varum, recurvatum, flexum) and for arthritis with lateral knee overload. The classical technique uses an L-shaped (90°-blade) plate with the transverse arm driven into the condyles perpendicular to the long axis of the femur and the longitudinal arm fixed to the shaft with bicortical screws; fluoroscopy confirms correct centring between both condyles. External compression osteosynthesis (with K-wires and a Greifensteiner clamp or an external fixator) is an alternative in osteoporotic bone or where plate fixation is technically difficult. For excessive recurvatum — supracondylar osteotomy with an anterior closing wedge and plate or external fixation. The distal femoral varising osteotomy is particularly useful in idiopathic or post-valgus genu valgum in young active patients with lateral compartment osteoarthritis, where a high tibial osteotomy would create patellar tilt.

For Salter–Harris type III–IV physeal fractures of the distal femur (Topic Trauma-24), open reduction and fixation with K-wires or cannulated screws perpendicular to the physis is mandatory; compression with two K-wires in a Greifensteiner clamp accelerates consolidation.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Classical approaches to the knee joint</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Textor U-shaped approach.** A U-shaped ("horseshoe") incision with a transverse limb along the proximal patellar border and two vertical limbs along its lateral edges, starting at the epicondyles; provides wide exposure of the entire joint with the patella turned down together with the patellar tendon. Oblique orientation of the vertical limbs and proximal extension preserve the proximal sources of patellar blood supply. Because the extensor apparatus is disrupted, the classical recommendation is osteotomy of the tibial tuberosity rather than transverse division of the patellar tendon when needed; if osteotomised, the transverse limb can be moved distally over the tuberosity. Historically the main exposure before the standardisation of the medial parapatellar approach; today it is used in revision and extensive intra-articular procedures.

**Pütti U-shaped approach.** A U-shape around the patella with the open arc directed downward and a vertical midline limb proximally (6–8 cm); proximal skin flaps are reflected; the medial and lateral limbs descend 2–3 cm from the patellar edges in front of the collaterals and reach below the joint line. The quadriceps tendon is split Z-shaped in the frontal plane, the anterior half is divided transversely proximally, and the flap with the patella is reflected downward — giving wide anterior exposure. An alternative to Textor with better preservation of patellar blood supply.

**Cogswell–Adam (medial parapatellar) approach.** Longitudinal medial parapatellar skin incision; the capsule is opened just medial to the patella; the entire joint and the suprapatellar bursa are accessible. Tibial tuberosity osteotomy is added when needed. Modified for the skin incision by Payer and Fischer; among the most used until the standardisation of the modern medial parapatellar approach.

**Kocher anterolateral approach.** Parapatellar incision along the edge of vastus lateralis, from 8 cm above the patella to 2 cm below the tibial tuberosity; proximally — sharp dissection between vastus lateralis and the terminal tendon; distally — incision of the retinaculum and capsule. Less convenient than the medial parapatellar (the patella is harder to displace medially than laterally because of the asymmetry of the femoral condyles) — release of the patellar tendon from the tuberosity is often required. Reserved for severe valgus gonarthrosis in TKA, lateral patellar instability, and lateral tibial plateau fractures.

**Abbott–Carpenter lateral approach.** From the distal sulcus femoris lateralis 6 cm proximal to the fibular head, distally along the anterior border of biceps femoris, then S-shaped anteriorly around the patellar tendon to the tibial tuberosity. Exposes the LCL, the entire lateral side and, when needed, the posterolateral zone; main indications — PLC revision, fibular head fixation, LCL reconstruction. Identification and protection of the common peroneal nerve along the medial border of the biceps tendon to the fibular head is critical — the nerve is most superficial at this point.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Standard medial parapatellar approach</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The medial parapatellar approach is the classical and most widely used approach for total knee arthroplasty, arthrotomy and many open procedures. Longitudinal anterior midline skin incision (from 5 cm above the patella to 2 cm distal to the tibial tuberosity). The deep arthrotomy begins at the medial edge of the quadriceps tendon, continues distally along the medial edge of the patella, and then down along the medial edge of the patellar tendon. The quadriceps tendon is incised approximately 1–2 cm medial to the patella to leave a cuff for closure. The patella is everted (or simply translated) laterally to access the joint.

The approach provides excellent visualisation of the entire joint but disrupts the insertion of vastus medialis on the patella, causing short-term quadriceps weakness and contributing to extensor-mechanism imbalance. The MPFL is partially disrupted and may contribute to lateral subluxation if not properly reconstructed.

**Midvastus.** Preserves a larger portion of vastus medialis by splitting the muscle along its fibres (no more than 4–5 cm proximal to the patella), rather than detaching its insertion. Exposure similar to the medial parapatellar with theoretical preservation of extensor-mechanism integrity.

**Subvastus.** Preserves the entire vastus medialis — lifts it off the medial femur (rather than splitting it) and reflects it laterally. Maximum preservation of the extensor mechanism but more limited exposure of the lateral compartment.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The medial parapatellar approach is standard in TKA — leave a 1–2 cm cuff of the quadriceps tendon medial to the patella for closure.</li>
    <li>Midvastus splits VMO along the fibres (extension no more than 4–5 cm proximal to the patella) — a compromise between exposure and extensor-mechanism preservation.</li>
    <li>Subvastus elevates the entire vastus medialis without dividing it — the most extensor-sparing approach but with the most limited exposure.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Posterior approach (Henderson, Burks–Schaffer modification)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The posterior approach to the knee is indicated for PCL reconstruction by tibial inlay, fixation of posterior tibial plateau fractures, posterior capsular procedures, revision of the popliteal vessels and tibial nerve, removal of a Baker's cyst and for tumours in the popliteal fossa. The classical Z ("lazy-S") incision: proximal limb along the semitendinosus tendon to the joint line, transverse limb across the popliteal fossa along a skin crease (~5 cm) and distal limb over the lateral head of gastrocnemius.

**Surgical anatomy and technique.** After flap elevation the popliteal fascia is opened around the small saphenous vein and the medial sural cutaneous nerve; in the groove between the two heads of gastrocnemius lie the neurovascular structures — tibial nerve superficially, popliteal vein, popliteal artery (deepest on the joint capsule). The bimuscular approach between the two heads of gastrocnemius (Henderson, modified by Burks–Schaffer) with medial retraction of the medial gastrocnemius head is the standard for PCL tibial inlay reconstruction. The classical approach to the common peroneal nerve (along the medial border of biceps to the fibular head) is separate — the nerve is easily found with the knee flexed and the biceps tendon retracted.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The popliteal artery lies 5–7 mm posterior to the posterior horn of the medial meniscus — at arthroscopic meniscectomy the posterior portals and working instruments must be in a strict anterior-to-posterior direction.</li>
    <li>The common peroneal nerve lies along the medial border of biceps femoris when the knee is flexed — mandatory identification at lateral approaches.</li>
    <li>The small saphenous vein and medial sural cutaneous nerve run beneath the popliteal fascia — the skin incision should cross them as obliquely as possible to minimise postoperative pain and neuroma.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Meniscectomy approaches and meniscectomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical approaches for open meniscectomy — before the arthroscopic era — were oblique, transverse, posteriorly convex and anteriorly convex. The patient lies supine with the knee flexed to 90° and the leg hanging over the edge of the table. **Oblique approaches** (Erkes — along a line from the femoral epicondyle to the tibial tuberosity; Mueller–Köller, Hofmann — variants with different obliquities) are placed between the anterior border of the collateral ligament and the patellar edge, not extending more than 1–2 cm below the joint line. **Transverse approaches** (Platt, Charnley, Cohen) — a 5 cm incision at the joint line between patella and collateral ligament — give good exposure of the middle and anterior third of the meniscus. **Posteriorly convex approaches** (Bruser) use a curved incision from the posterior border of the collateral ligament anteriorly to the tibial tuberosity — especially useful for isolated posterior-horn lesions. **Anteriorly convex approaches** (Smillie; Smillie–Fischer with U-shaped extension) start at the lateral edge of the patella, cross the joint line and end on the tibial condyle.

**Classical open medial meniscectomy after Smillie/Boychev.** Two approaches — parapatellar (for the anterior and middle segment) and retroligamentous posterior (for the posterior horn). The anterior segment is mobilised with a straight meniscotome, detached from the paracapsular zone and pulled forward with a meniscal grasper. The posterior horn is detached through the second retroligamentous incision with a curved meniscotome, which is pushed posteriorly against the posterior capsule, fixed, and the meniscus pulled anteriorly against the cutting edge — a technique that protects the structures of the popliteal fossa. In a "bucket handle" the central fragment is divided transversely from its anterior attachment, gripped with Smillie's meniscal grasper and pulled forward; the posterior attachment is divided blindly with a "backward" meniscotome. The lateral meniscectomy is performed in the "figure-of-four" (Cabot) position — hip flexion, knee flexion and external rotation of the leg — to inspect the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus. After the era of De Haven (1979), Jackson and Dandy (1976), and Gillquist (1971), partial arthroscopic meniscectomy and meniscal repair (inside-out, outside-in, all-inside) became standard; classical open meniscectomy is practically obsolete. Fairbank (1948) described the radiographic sequelae of total meniscectomy — the anatomical basis of the modern emphasis on meniscal preservation.

<details>
<summary>ACL reconstruction — classical line</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

ACL reconstruction has over a century of history; more than 200 techniques have been described in the literature, grouped into three categories: intra-articular (replacement along the anatomical path of the ACL), extra-articular (dynamic stabilisation through posterolateral tenodesis) and combined (central reconstruction + lateral augmentation).

**Chronology.** **Hey-Groves** (England, 1917) — first intra-articular reconstruction with the iliotibial tract through femoral and tibial tunnels. **Macey** (1939) — a free segment of the iliotibial tract. **Jones** (1963) and **Lindemann** (1950) — techniques using the central third of the patellar tendon with a bone block from the tibial tuberosity passed through tunnels — the conceptual ancestor of the modern bone–patellar tendon–bone (BPTB) autograft. **Lemaire** (France, 1967) — extra-articular tenodesis with the iliotibial tract for chronic anterolateral instability. **MacIntosh** (Canada, 1972, 1976) — combined intra- and extra-articular procedures with an iliotibial-tract strip through the intercondylar notch — the "over-the-top" MacIntosh technique. **Kennedy and Insall** (1979) — central quadriceps reconstruction (LARS predecessor).

**Contribution of Landa.** A particular role belongs to the Soviet orthopaedist Landa (Moscow, 1947), who described a technique in which one third of the middle part of the patellar tendon is taken together with part of the quadriceps tendon above the patella and a femoral aponeurosis — a strip passed through tibial and femoral bone tunnels. This operation was "rediscovered" in the West by Kenneth Jones (1963) and is generally referred to as the "Kenneth Jones operation", but the priority is Russian. The Bulgarian school continues with the modifications of Marshall (4 cm proximal tendinous extension, 180° flip and overlap), Shoylev (1982) — combination of a patellar tendon graft with pes anserinus transposition (Slocum–Larson) and lateral augmentation — results in 37 patients with chronic anterior global instability, and Lindemann–Ficat (with the semitendinosus tendon).

**Modern arthroscopic ACL reconstruction.** Standard — single-bundle with BPTB or four-strand hamstring (semitendinosus + gracilis) autograft. Femoral tunnel through the intercondylar notch with anatomic position on the "resident's ridge" of the lateral femoral condyle (10–11 o'clock for the right knee). Tibial tunnel through the tibial tuberosity with an external 60° angle and anatomic exit anterior and medial to the intercondylar eminence. Fixation: femoral — EndoButton (Mitek), TightRope or Bio-Tenodesis screw; tibial — interference screw or with staple backup. All-inside (no tibial incision), double-bundle (AM + PL for anatomic restoration of both bundles) and quadriceps tendon — modern variants.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>PCL reconstruction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

PCL reconstruction is needed for chronic instability with functional discomfort (instability when leaning on a flexed knee — "kneeling test"), diffuse posterior pain, recurrent unexplained hydrarthrosis and/or combined injuries. In chronic ruptures direct repair or suture is almost impossible.

**Classical palliative techniques.** **Augustine** (1956) — central reconstruction with the medial third of the patellar tendon passed through a tibial tunnel; extension immobilisation for 6 weeks. **Hughston** (1969) — dynamic reconstruction with the medial half of the medial gastrocnemius tendon (separated from the muscle without cutting the femoral insertion) passed through a medial femoral tunnel and fixed to the tibia — a dynamic effect in flexion; Hughston (Lyon 1979; New Orleans) reported satisfactory results in more than 30 patients. **Trickey** (1968) — reconstruction with a tibial inlay technique through a posterior approach using a split semitendinosus tendon. **Insall** — reconstruction with the medial meniscus (as a free meniscocapsular graft) for irreparable PCL — variable postoperative results.

**Modern standard.** Arthroscopic tibial tunnel or tibial inlay technique with BPTB, Achilles allograft or quadriceps tendon. Tibial inlay (through a posterior approach) eliminates the "killer turn" — the sharp graft bend through the tibial tunnel that is the principal mechanism of late transtibial failure.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Repair of acute ligamentous injuries (Hughston, Boychev)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Acute medial soft tissues.** In anteromedial, posteromedial or anterior global instability (traumatic disruption of MCL, ACL and/or medial meniscus) an extended medial parapatellar approach is used. After releasing pes anserinus to inspect the tibial MCL insertion, a parapatellar fascial incision is made up to the medial edge of vastus medialis. The medial meniscus, ACL, patellar articular surface, lateral meniscus and PCL are systematically inspected. Transchondral meniscal tears are excised (or, in current practice, repaired). Cruciate ligament tears are repaired with transosseous Vicryl or wire sutures by the classical method: in femoral ACL avulsion a bone tunnel is made in the lateral condyle and the ligament suture is passed through it; tibial eminence avulsion is fixed with a screw or transosseous suture.

For MCL lesions: femoral insertion lesions (re-insertion with sutures or a metal staple to the medial epicondyle); mid-substance lesions (non-absorbable suture); tibial insertion (transosseous re-insertion). For posteromedial capsule injury the oblique popliteal ligament is re-inserted to the adductor tubercle with bone tunnels and a staple (Hughston), and the tibial attachment is restored with transosseous tibial tunnels and Vicryl or Dexon sutures tied ventrally (Hughston). With interrupted mattress sutures the oblique popliteal ligament is sutured to the semimembranosus tendon and then to the reconstructed MCL.

**Acute lateral soft tissues.** In acute posterolateral instability (traumatic disruption of LCL, popliteus, popliteofibular ligament or biceps tendon) an anterolateral incision (12–14 cm) is used, starting 3–4 cm above the patella, along the fibres of the iliotibial tract, ending 3 cm below Gerdy's tubercle. The common peroneal nerve is identified and protected. Re-insertion of popliteus and LCL to the lateral epicondyle is performed "up and forward" with transosseous sutures, lightly tensioned, with the knee in 30° flexion and external tibial rotation — a position that applies the correct tensiogram. Behind the LCL the posterior capsule and arcuate ligament are re-inserted to the tibia through transosseous tunnels. After the repair a plaster boot at 70° flexion and external tibial rotation for 6 weeks.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Classical procedures for patellar instability</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Recurrent and habitual patellar dislocations have been treated with dozens of described techniques. The classical eponyms in the Bulgarian curriculum:

**Krogius** (Au. Krogius, Finland, 1904) — fashioning a 20 cm medial capsulofascial strip incorporating the distal vastus medialis fibres and transposing it through a lateral capsular incision for tightening; the medial gap is filled by a lateral parapatellar flap.

**Roux** (1888) and **Roux–Goldthwait** (Goldthwait, 1899) — the lateral half of the patellar tendon with a bone wedge from the tibial tuberosity is detached and transferred medially and caudally; the central third remains in situ. The conceptual ancestor of modern distal patellar realignment in children with open physes (when tibial tuberosity osteotomy is contraindicated).

**Brucknér** — the medial third of the patellar tendon with a bone wedge from the tibial tuberosity is detached and transferred medially over the anteromedial tibia; an alternative in adults.

**Boychev** — a tendomuscular flap from vastus medialis (with a VMO slip) is passed through a longitudinal slit in the lateral capsule and sutured to the patella and the medial capsule. The patellar tendon strip with part of the tibial tuberosity is fixed to the anteromedial tibia; the lateral capsule is left open (release).

**Insall** (1972) — proximal realignment with VMO medialisation onto the patella and lateral retinacular release; combined with Elmslie–Trillat in distal malalignment.

**Madigan** (1975) and **Stanisavljević** (1976) — for habitual dislocation in children with severe dysplasia, extensive "derotation" of the quadriceps mechanism and tibial tuberosity.

**Shoylev** (1983) — combined technique: lateral retinacular release after Ficat (resection of a 7–8 × 1.5–2 cm rectangle from the lateral retinaculum, fibrous capsule, alar ligament and distal vastus lateralis fibres), followed by Elmslie–Trillat medialisation and distalisation of the tibial tuberosity with compression screws and proximal VMO advancement. Shoylev reported 60 cases with very good clinical outcome.

**Elmslie–Trillat (Trillat, 1964).** For anteromedial rotational instability and lateral patellar instability — osteotomy and medialisation of the tibial tuberosity. Anterior medial incision from the proximal insertion of vastus medialis around the tibial tuberosity 4–5 cm distally; the patellar tendon is released medially and laterally, and a wide flat osteotome performs a frontal-plane osteotomy of the tuberosity. The tuberosity is rotated medially 8–11 mm and distalised 4–5 mm (to correct patella alta), then fixed with one or two AO compression screws. Medialisation requires a lateral retinacular release. The classical **Hauser** procedure (1938) — the same principle but with more aggressive medialisation — has been abandoned because of chronic patellofemoral overload and arthritis. **Maquet** (1963, 1976) added ventralisation ("anteromedialisation") with a bone graft under the tuberosity to offload the patellofemoral joint (elevation 1.5–2.5 cm) — later modified by **Fulkerson** (1983) into the anteromedialising osteotomy with an obliquely directed osteotomy line for simultaneous anteriorisation and medialisation, optimal for laterally and distally placed patellar cartilage lesions.

**MPFL reconstruction (modern standard).** In acute patellar dislocation with MPFL rupture — reconstruction with a gracilis or semitendinosus autograft and anatomic attachment at "Schöttle's point" on the medial epicondyle (1 mm in front of the extended posterior femoral cortical line, 2.5 mm distal to the perpendicular to the posterior cortical line at the level of the upper point of the medial condyle).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>High tibial osteotomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Right knee anteriorly with the capsule opened: ACL, PCL, medial meniscus, patellar tendon. The classical joint-preserving surgery — high tibial osteotomy by Coventry (1965), Maquet-Bardot, Puddu, Holevich HTO — is guided by this architecture: a lateral closing wedge (~1 mm per 1° of correction) offloads the medial compartment in varus gonarthrosis.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Henry Vandyke Carter, Gray's Anatomy (1918), <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gray347.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PD</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

High osteotomy of the proximal tibia was first described by **Jackson** (1958) and standardised by **Coventry** (Mayo, 1965) as a lateral closing-wedge osteotomy for varus gonarthrosis with medial unicompartmental arthritis.

**Coventry classical technique.** Lateral horseshoe incision below the fibular head; osteotomy of the fibular shaft to release (or fibular head resection in severe valgus correction); marking and stepwise removal of a wedge with a base of 1° per 1 mm of width; the medial cortex is perforated with small chisels without breaking (for hinge stability), and after valgisation the two fragments are fixed with staples in front of the fibula. Two parallel channels are drilled through the fibular head and two through the tibial fragments to accept a Charnley or Greifensteiner direct-traction clamp that creates compression. Plaster cylinder for 6 weeks.

**Modification by Holevich, Dzherov and Vladimirov (Bulgaria, 1970s).** Preserves the fibular head, osteotomises the fibula at the start of the diaphysis with resection of a 1–1.5 cm segment, and fixes the two fibular fragments with a long cancellous AO screw with a washer, using the fibular head as a lever to tension the LCL and the biceps tendon — a substantial advantage in cases with associated joint instability.

**Maquet–Bardot dome osteotomy.** Anterior longitudinal approach, periosteal stripping of the tibia on both sides of the patellar tendon, oscillating-saw osteotomy in a semicircular arc concave upward, correction into the desired valgus position and compression fixation with Steinmann pins and a compressor.

**Maquet–Puddu opening-wedge osteotomy.** Medial wedge osteotomy with stepwise opening and placement of a dedicated plate with a "foot" in the wedge ("Puddu plate"); classical grafts to fill the wedge — tricortical iliac-crest wedges or autografts. For corrections over 15–20° the fibula is osteotomised first.

**Distal femoral varising osteotomy.** Analogue for valgus gonarthrosis with lateral unicompartmental arthritis in young patients where HTO would create an oblique joint line. Options: medial opening wedge or lateral closing wedge with a locking plate.

</details>

<summary>Total and unicompartmental knee arthroplasty</summary>

Total knee arthroplasty evolved from **Walldius** (1951, hinged total replacement — the first clinically successful design), **Charnley** (1958, polycentric), **Insall–Burstein** (1974, condylar — the basis of the modern standard), the geometric prosthesis (Geomedic, 1973) and the Robert Brigham semitotal (1976). The classical medial parapatellar approach, distal femoral cuts (95° or 97° to the mechanical axis), proximal tibial cut (90° to the tibial mechanical axis), and meniscus and cruciate resection (in posterior-stabilised designs) are standard.

The **Bandl approach** (Walter Bandl, described in the Bulgarian school) uses an arc-shaped incision starting at the medial epicondyle, crossing the tibial tuberosity and ending at the lateral epicondyle — an alternative to the standard anterior midline incision when closure is difficult.

**Unicompartmental arthroplasty (UKA).** In younger patients (under 60) with unicompartmental gonarthrosis (medial or lateral) — Marmor (1972), Goodfellow and O'Connor (Oxford UKA, 1976). Requirements: intact ACL and PCL, varus/valgus deformity correctable under 10–15°, flexion contracture < 20°, range of motion at least 70°.

**Patellectomy.** For severe chondromalacia or chronic patellofemoral arthritis — as a stand-alone procedure or as part of arthroplasty when patellar pain dominates. Described by Compère (1937) and Boyd (1955); the patella is removed, the extensor expansion is duplicated and the lateral retinacula are sutured along the midline. Patelloplasty (resurfacing with the lateral half of the patella turned over with its facet upward) is a classical alternative.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Knee arthrodesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For resistant joint infection, severe Charcot destruction, failed TKA and post-paralytic instability, arthrodesis remains a "salvage" solution.

**Charnley arthrodesis** (1948) — compression arthrodesis with an external compression frame; tibia and femur are cut transversely at the joint line, the articular surfaces are cleaned to cancellous bone and apposed under compression (10° flexion, 10° external rotation). The Charnley external frame with two Steinmann pin groups and compression screws holds the position until bony consolidation (6–8 weeks).

**Lucas–Cottrell arthrodesis** (1955) — resection of the articular surfaces with intramedullary fixation using a long Küntscher nail passed from the greater trochanter through the femur, the joint (with canal reaming) and into the tibia; effective in young active patients.

**Ilizarov arthrodesis** — circular frame with compression and, when required, axial and length correction; particularly useful in infected non-union and in significant bone defects requiring bone transport.

Arthrodesis is contraindicated in children under 6–7 years because of growth. The fusion position is critical — 10° flexion and 10° external rotation of the leg relative to the thigh (for optimal gait).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Repair of the quadriceps tendon and patellar tendon</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Rupture of the quadriceps tendon (typically 1–2 cm proximal to the patella) and of the patellar tendon (typically distally at the patellar insertion) requires urgent operative repair. Classical technique: arc-shaped transverse incision above the upper edge of the patella (or longitudinal anterior for the patellar tendon), identification of the rupture, evacuation of the haematoma and approximation of the tendon edges with the knee in full extension. Suturing with interrupted non-absorbable sutures (Tycron, Ethibond) or chromic catgut.

For chronic ruptures, lengthening by **Codivilla** (triangular flap based proximally on the divided tendon, flipped down and sutured to the distal end) or **Scuderi** (V-Y plasty) is standard. A protective tension wire by **Bunnell** (from the tibial tuberosity or patella) is added for uncertain reconstructions; the wire is removed at 4 months.

For patellar tendon rupture the end-to-end suture is reinforced by a transosseous circumferential wire from the tibial tuberosity through the quadriceps tendon above the patella, fixed without tension. **McLaughlin** offers a modification with a transverse pin through the patella (or tibial tuberosity for patellar tendon rupture) with a wire through the proximal tendon and fixed to both pin ends. For chronic patellar-tendon rupture with patellar retraction the operation is done in two stages: first mobilisation of the patella with downward traction for 2–3 weeks, then reconstruction with a semitendinosus autograft or Achilles allograft.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Knee synovectomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Classical open total/subtotal knee synovectomy is indicated for rheumatoid arthritis without significant joint destruction (Preston: if no remission after 4–6 months — do not delay), pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS), chronic traumatic and essential synovitis, and historically for the synovial form of joint tuberculosis.

The approach is medial parapatellar (Payer) with proximal release of rectus femoris from vastus medialis; the bulging synovium is divided along the incision, the patella is retracted laterally and the anteromedial, anterolateral zones and the suprapatellar recess are exposed. Resection begins at the suprapatellar bursa and proceeds downward, around the medial and lateral recesses and includes Hoffa's fat pad (usually infiltrated by pannus). The two posterior meniscal horns block complete synovial clearance from the posterior recesses — added posteromedial and posterolateral incisions are made in ~30% of cases. The menisci are preserved unless torn; pannus infiltrating the articular cartilage is curetted. Haemostasis with the cautery; suction drainage for 48 h.

In haemophilic patients (Christensen 1965, Storti 1973) early postoperative mobilisation and factor VIII/IX replacement therapy are mandatory. Arthroscopic synovectomy is the technical alternative in current practice and is preferred in younger, suitable patients.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Knee denervation (Bateman) and sensory branches</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Knee joint denervation (Bateman, 1968) is indicated for severe osteoarthritis in patients who wish to preserve joint mobility (an alternative to arthrodesis or instead of arthroplasty). The three sensory branches to the knee are: a branch from the posterior tibial nerve (to the posterior capsule), a branch from the obturator nerve (to the anteromedial side, branching from the musculotendinous part of vastus medialis — may be absent), and a branch from the nerve to vastus intermedius (to the suprapatellar bursa, along the mid-anterior femoral surface).

Denervation is done in two positions — first prone (posterior approach to the popliteal fossa, identification of the tibial nerve and motor branches to the two gastrocnemius heads, isolation of the sensory branch to the posterior capsule, division and traction), then supine (medial parapatellar approach with identification of the branch to vastus intermedius and, when required, the obturator branch medially). With preserved medial branch innervation the procedure is considered incomplete — but significant pain relief in moderately severe arthritis is still achievable.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Above-knee amputation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The level for above-knee amputation is determined by modern prosthetic requirements — the optimal level is 10–12 cm above the knee, providing a sufficiently long stump for adequate prosthetic control in all directions. The distal 7–8 cm of the femur are unsuitable because of prosthetic-joint constraints, and short stumps under 5 cm — because of insufficient muscular control.

**Classical technique.** Two half-arc flaps — anterior:posterior ratio 2:1. Bone is cut at 8–10 cm above the desired final stump level. Myoplasty is performed — fixation of the quadriceps and adductors to the posterior muscle segment to close the femoral canal and provide a load-bearing surface. Drainage is brought out distally in the wound.

In severe contaminated trauma, war wounds and gas gangrene an open (guillotine) amputation by **Pirogov** is performed with a circular resection — modified with two half-arc skin flaps, anterior higher and posterior lower, without closure. The wound is covered with compresses soaked in Vishnevsky balsam and a late secondary closure (day 4–5 with good local condition) or late secondary suture after granulation is planned. Osteoplastic amputations by **Yu. A. Dzhanelidze** (1953) with an osteoplastic cover — historically advantageous; today rarely performed since modern prosthetics do not require a bone cap.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Classical operations for flexion contracture</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For spastic flexion contracture from hamstring hypertonus the classical technique is transposition of the flexors from the ischiopubicum to the femoral condyles (**Eggers**, 1952; modified by Keat and Smillie): the tendons of biceps femoris, semitendinosus and semimembranosus are detached from the tibial insertions, pulled proximally and sutured to the femoral condyles (medial for semitendinosus/semimembranosus, lateral for biceps), converting them from two-joint to one-joint hip extensors. With internal rotation of the leg the technique is modified — resection of the branches of semimembranosus and semitendinosus from the sciatic nerve (Hoel resection) is performed, preserving biceps femoris for external rotation.

For quadriceps paralysis with severe knee instability the classical transposition of the flexors to the patella (biceps femoris and semitendinosus tendons routed through two oblique subcutaneous tunnels on either side of the patella and fixed with periosteal flaps or subperiosteal tunnels) is standard (**Compère**, 1939). Conforti modifies with two parallel longitudinal tunnels along the entire patellar length.

For flexion contracture from gastrocnemius hypertonus (childhood spastic palsy) — release of its proximal part by **Silfverskiöld**: transverse popliteal incision parallel to the skin crease, opening of the transverse fascia, identification of the tibial nerve and motor branches to the two gastrocnemius heads, and division of 1–2 of them.

</details>

### Arthroscopy

<details id="arthroscopy">
<summary>Arthroscopy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Tim1965, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Typical_arthroscopic_surgery_incisions_-_knee.JPG" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>

**Historical development.** The first recorded attempt at arthroscopy — Nordentoft (1912) presented at the 41st German Congress of Surgeons in Berlin a paper "On the Endoscopy of Closed Cavities Using My Trocar Endoscope" describing arthroscopia genu. Takagi (Japan, 1918) and Bircher (Switzerland, 1921) were long considered the "fathers of arthroscopy". Masaki **Watanabe** (Japan, 1959) was the first to perform operative arthroscopy — removing a xanthomatous tumour from the suprapatellar recess on 9 March 1955; his Watanabe Arthroscope No. 21 (1962), with fibreoptic illumination and a working channel, was the first clinically distributable instrument. Robert **Jackson**, John Joyce, Lanny Johnson, Richard O'Connor and Heshmat Shahriaree (1970s) spread the technique in North America. **Gillquist** (Sweden, 1971) described the transseptal portal; **De Haven** (1979) and **Dandy** (1976) developed partial arthroscopic meniscectomy.

**Standard anterior portals.**
- *Anterolateral* (1 cm above the joint line, lateral to the patellar tendon) — standard camera portal.
- *Anteromedial* (1 cm above the joint line, medial to the patellar tendon) — working portal. Localised with a needle by the "outside-in" technique under direct arthroscopic visualisation for precise positioning relative to the medial meniscus and the anterior ACL footprint at reconstruction.

**Suprapatellar portals.**
- *Superolateral* (3 cm proximal to the upper patellar pole, lateral to the quadriceps tendon) — for drainage, inspection of the suprapatellar bursa and the patellofemoral joint at depth.
- *Superomedial* — drainage alternative; rarely used because of proximity to the VMO.

**Posterior portals.**
- *Posteromedial* (1 cm above the joint line, just behind the MCL, in the "soft spot" between the medial femoral condyle and the semimembranosus tendon) — for inspection of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus, the PCL tibial insertion and the posteromedial recess.
- *Posterolateral* (between the biceps femoris tendon and the iliotibial tract) — caution for the common peroneal nerve (~1 cm proximal to the joint line along the medial border of the biceps tendon).

**Transseptal portal (Gillquist 1971)** — through the intercondylar septum between the anterolateral and posteromedial portals — gives access to the posterolateral compartment without a separate posterior incision.

**Systematic arthroscopic examination.** Suprapatellar bursa → patellofemoral joint (patellar cartilage by the Outerbridge classification 0–IV) → medial and lateral gutters → medial compartment (meniscus, anteromedial, middle and posterior — with screw-home test, valgus stress for opening) → intercondylar notch (ACL, PCL, Hoffa fat pad, ligamentum mucosum) → lateral compartment (meniscus, popliteus tendon, popliteomeniscal fascicles) → when needed, posteromedial and posterolateral compartments through the posterior portals.

**Classical arthroscopic procedures.** Partial meniscectomy, meniscal repair (inside-out after Henning, outside-in, all-inside with devices such as FasT-Fix, Meniscal Cinch); cartilage procedures (microfracture after Steadman, OATS — osteochondral autograft transfer, ACI — autologous chondrocyte implantation, MACI — matrix-assisted); ACL reconstruction (single-bundle, double-bundle, BPTB, hamstring, quadriceps tendon); PCL reconstruction (single, double bundle, transtibial and tibial inlay); MPFL reconstruction for patellar instability; treatment of loose bodies and chondromatosis; arthroscopic synovectomy (PVNS, rheumatoid arthritis, haemophilic synoviopathy); assessment and treatment of posteromedial and posterolateral instability; lateral retinacular release for lateral patellar overpressure; assisted fixation of tibial plateau fractures.

**Anaesthesia and medium.** General or regional (spinal/epidural); the joint is distended with saline or Ringer's solution, rarely with gas (carbon dioxide). In acute trauma repeated saline irrigation lowers infection risk. Tourniquet pressure 275 mmHg in the upper limb and 450 mmHg in the lower limb (or 100 mmHg above systolic in hypertensive patients); not exceeding 1.5 h in the lower limb.

## Outcomes and Complications

<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Complications in the thigh and knee vary by approach and procedure.

**Lateral and posterolateral femoral approach.** Bleeding from perforating branches of the profunda femoris (prevented by pre-ligation); iatrogenic sciatic nerve injury in the distal third (particularly common peroneal stretching); extension or flexion contracture from splitting vastus intermedius; postoperative distal-fragment displacement in comminuted fractures with insufficient plate length.

**Posterior approach.** Tibial nerve injury during dissection in the central gastrocnemius groove (especially in revisions through scar tissue); popliteal artery bleeding (catastrophic and requires vascular surgery); neuroma of the medial sural cutaneous nerve and small saphenous vein at the skin incision — a typical cause of chronic lateral popliteal pain.

**Medial parapatellar approach and TKA.** Patellar instability when MPFL reconstruction is inadequate and the lateral retinacular balance is poor; arthrofibrosis (limited ROM < 90° at 6 months — indication for manipulation under anaesthesia); infection (1–2% primary, 5–7% revision); neurological injury of the common peroneal nerve in severe valgus correction (posterolateral stretch); thromboembolic disease — indication for thromboprophylaxis; component loosening and osteolysis (polyethylene wear); periprosthetic fracture (Vancouver/UCS classification).

**Arthroscopy.** Infection (0.1–0.5%, higher in revision or prolonged procedures); haemarthrosis; iatrogenic articular cartilage injury from instruments; neurological injury (saphenous nerve at medial meniscectomy — 5–10%, typically transient; common peroneal nerve at lateral meniscectomy); effusion and compartment syndrome from extravasation of irrigation fluid (especially in prolonged procedures and capsular anomalies); thromboembolic disease (lower risk than open surgery but not zero — particularly in patients with prior VTE); patellar fracture in patellar procedures; meniscal repair failure (retear in 20% of inside-out cases).

**ACL/PCL reconstruction.** Arthrofibrosis (cyclops lesion — focal intra-articular soft-tissue mass blocking full extension — 3–5%); graft failure (rerupture in 5–10% — higher in adolescents); tibial compartment syndrome at BPTB harvest (extremely rare); neurological injury at hamstring harvest (saphenous nerve and its sartorial branch); patellar fracture at BPTB harvest (1–2%); stiffness from misplaced tunnels ("notchplasty" is often required for a low femoral tunnel).

**Arthrodesis.** Pseudarthrosis (5–15%, higher in infection and extensive bone loss); limb shortening (must be planned); instability with inappropriate fusion position (always 10° flexion + 10° external rotation).

</div>
</details>
</section>

## Local Contributions

<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the orthopaedic school in Bulgaria; the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. His three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curricular reference. Describes classical thigh and knee approaches, the technique of medial meniscectomy with combined parapatellar and retroligamentous posterior incisions, and a tendomuscular vastus-medialis flap through a lateral capsular slit for patellar instability.

**Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Boychev's modification of medial meniscectomy (combination of parapatellar + retroligamentous posterior approach with the classical Smillie meniscotome); patellar-instability technique (muscle flap from vastus medialis through a lateral capsular slit).

**Boychev–Imamaliev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Z-shaped (step-cut) segmental osteotomy for subtrochanteric femoral shortening — provides a larger contact area and better rotational stability than a transverse cut.

**Holevich, Dzherov, Vladimirov <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1970s).** Bulgarian modification of Coventry's high tibial osteotomy — preservation of the fibular head, osteotomy of the fibula at the start of the diaphysis with resection of a 1–1.5 cm segment, and fixation with a long cancellous AO screw and washer; simultaneous use of the fibular head as a lever for the LCL and biceps tendon in cases combined with joint instability.

**Shoylev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1982, 1983).** Modification of ACL reconstruction — combination of a patellar tendon graft with pes anserinus transposition (Slocum–Larson) and lateral augmentation (37 patients with chronic anterior global instability). Combined patellar stabilisation — Ficat lateral retinacular release + Elmslie–Trillat medialisation + proximal VMO advancement (60 cases with very good clinical outcome).

**Landa <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1947).** First descriptor of ACL reconstruction with a strip of the middle third of the patellar tendon together with part of the quadriceps tendon above the patella and a femoral aponeurosis, passed through tibial and femoral bone tunnels — an operation "rediscovered" in the West by Kenneth Jones (1963) and generally referred to as the "Kenneth Jones operation", but with Russian priority.

**V. N. Bogoraz <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1948).** Classical femoral lengthening through oblique or segmental osteotomy with prolonged direct traction and plaster immobilisation to consolidation — historical predecessor of Ilizarov and Wagner systems.

**G. A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (Kurgan, 1954–1971).** Circular compression-distraction frame with the principle of "distraction osteogenesis" — applicable in lengthening, axial correction and arthrodesis. Classical femoral mounting: two 3/4-rings through the proximal and two complete rings through the distal fragment.

**A. I. Bliskunov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (USSR, 1983).** First clinically successful intramedullary distraction system — the conceptual predecessor of modern magnetic (PRECICE) and motorised (ISKD) lengtheners.

**Yu. A. Dzhanelidze <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1953).** Osteoplastic amputations with a periosteal-bone cap — historically advantageous for stump stabilisation in the pre-modern-prosthetic era.

**Watanabe <sup class="natflag">JP</sup> (Japan, 1959).** First clinically successful operative arthroscopy — removal of a xanthomatous tumour from the suprapatellar bursa on 9 March 1955; Watanabe Arthroscope No. 21 (1962) — the first clinically distributable instrument.

**Gillquist <sup class="natflag">SE</sup> (Sweden, 1971).** Transseptal portal for access to the posterolateral compartment without a separate posterior incision; one of the pioneers of partial arthroscopic meniscectomy.

**Hey-Groves <sup class="natflag">GB</sup> (1917).** First intra-articular ACL reconstruction with the iliotibial tract.

**Macey <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1939).** Free segment of the iliotibial tract for ACL reconstruction.

**Lindemann <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> (1950).** Reconstruction with the medial third of the patellar tendon.

**Jones <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (Kenneth Jones, 1963).** The classical BPTB ACL reconstruction — Western rediscovery of Landa's principle (1947).

**Lemaire <sup class="natflag">FR</sup> (France, 1967).** Extra-articular tenodesis with the iliotibial tract — foundation of modern lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) techniques.

**MacIntosh <sup class="natflag">CA</sup> (Canada, 1972, 1976).** "Over-the-top" technique — combined intra- and extra-articular approach.

**Kennedy–Insall <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1979).** Central quadriceps reconstruction (predecessor of LARS).

**Hughston <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (Columbus, GA).** Classical taxonomy of knee instability, dynamic PCL reconstruction with medial gastrocnemius; standardisation of acute posteromedial soft-tissue repair.

**Coventry <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (Mayo, 1965).** Standardisation of the high tibial osteotomy as a lateral closing wedge for varus gonarthrosis.

**Maquet <sup class="natflag">BE</sup> (Belgium, 1963, 1976).** Dome tibial osteotomy and tibial-tuberosity anteromedialisation for offloading the patellofemoral joint.

**Trillat <sup class="natflag">FR</sup> (France, 1964).** Medialisation of the tibial tuberosity — foundation of modern distal patellar realignment.

**Insall–Burstein <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1974).** Condylar total knee arthroplasty — the basis of modern TKA.

**Charnley <sup class="natflag">GB</sup> (1958).** Polycentric TKA design and compression arthrodesis with an external fixator.

**Walldius <sup class="natflag">SE</sup> (Sweden, 1951).** Hinged total knee replacement — the first clinically successful design.

**Goodfellow and O'Connor <sup class="natflag">GB</sup> (Oxford, 1976).** Oxford UKA — the modern standard for unicompartmental arthroplasty.

**Marmor <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1972).** Unicompartmental arthroplasty — early reference.

**Küntscher <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> (Germany, 1939–1940).** The intramedullary nail — standardisation of intramedullary fixation of long bones.

**Judet <sup class="natflag">FR</sup>.** Decortication of pseudarthroses — biological activation by an "osteoperiosteal sleeve".

**Wagner <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> (Germany, 1971).** Monolateral distractor for femoral and tibial lengthening.

**Schöttle <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>.** Radiographic marker for anatomic MPFL attachment on the medial epicondyle (Schöttle's point).

**Smillie <sup class="natflag">GB</sup>.** Meniscotome for classical open medial and lateral meniscectomy — the standard instrument in the pre-arthroscopic era.

**Fairbank <sup class="natflag">GB</sup> (1948).** Radiographic sequelae of total meniscectomy — the anatomical basis of modern meniscal preservation.

**Hauser <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1938).** Medialisation of the tibial tuberosity — an abandoned technique superseded by Elmslie–Trillat and MPFL reconstruction.

**Krogius <sup class="natflag">FI</sup> (Finland, 1904).** Capsulofascial patellar stabilisation with a medial VMO strip.

**Roux–Goldthwait <sup class="natflag">CH/US</sup> (Roux 1888, Goldthwait 1899).** Lateral half of the patellar tendon transferred medially — basis of paediatric patellar stabilisation.

**Slocum–Larson <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1968).** Pes anserinus transposition for chronic anteromedial rotational instability.

**Elmslie <sup class="natflag">GB</sup> and Trillat <sup class="natflag">FR</sup> (1964).** The classical medialisation of the tibial tuberosity.

**Fulkerson <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1983).** Anteromedialising osteotomy — optimal for laterally-distally placed patellar cartilage lesions.

**Bandl <sup class="natflag">AT</sup>.** Arc-shaped approach in total knee arthroplasty — alternative when closure is difficult.

**Brattström <sup class="natflag">SE</sup> (1964).** Definition and measurement of the Q-angle.

**Wiberg <sup class="natflag">SE</sup> (1941).** Classification of patellar shapes (I–III, plus Baumgartl type IV).

**Warren–Marshall <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1979).** Description of the MPFL as a discrete anatomical entity; three-layer concept of the medial soft tissues.

**Seebacher–Inglis <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1982).** Three-layer concept of the lateral soft tissues.

**Arnoczky <sup class="natflag">US</sup> (1982).** Zonal concept of meniscal vascular supply (red–red, red–white, white–white).
