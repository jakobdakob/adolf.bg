## Introduction

The thigh and knee region contains the longest bone in the body (femur), the largest joint (knee), and a substantial muscular envelope that organizes the major movements of the lower extremity. The surgical approaches to this region are diverse: the thigh compartments require specific fasciotomy approaches; the distal femur and proximal tibia require approaches tailored to the location of pathology (lateral, medial, anterior); and the knee joint can be accessed through the standard medial parapatellar approach, the muscle-sparing variants (midvastus, subvastus), and the dedicated arthroscopic portals. The knowledge of these approaches, of the muscular compartments of the thigh, of the neurovascular structures (femoral nerve and vessels anteriorly, sciatic nerve posteriorly, popliteal vessels in the popliteal fossa), and of the arthroscopic portals and intra-articular anatomy is essential to the orthopedic surgeon. This chapter, drawing on Orthopaedic Surgical Approaches, Netter's Concise Orthopaedic Anatomy, Gray's Anatomy, and the classical Bulgarian Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology of Boycho Boychev — the namesake of the University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya — is supplemented with the classical procedures for the thigh, knee, and operations on the menisci and collateral ligaments after Küntscher, Wagner, Ilizarov, Coventry, Maquet, Bandy, Insall–Burstein, Charnley, Watanabe, Smillie, Hauser, Roux–Goldthwait, Elmslie–Trillat, Fulkerson, Hughston, Bunnell, Lemaire, MacIntosh, Lindemann, Hey-Groves, Kenneth–Jones, Landa, Shoylev, Boychev, Holevich, Dzherov, Vladimirov, Imamaliev, and the Soviet schools (Bogoraz, Ilizarov, Dzhanelidze) — techniques which, together with the principles of arthroscopy after Watanabe, remain part of the Bulgarian orthopedic curriculum.

The thigh has three principal muscular compartments separated by intermuscular septa attached to the linea aspera of the femur: Anterior compartment: Contains the quadriceps femoris (rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius), the sartorius, the iliacus and psoas (proximally), and the pectineus. Innervated principally by the femoral nerve (L2-L4). Functions principally as knee extension and hip flexion. Medial compartment: Contains the adductor longus, brevis, and magnus; the gracilis; the obturator externus. Innervated principally by the obturator nerve (L2-L4). Functions as hip adduction. Posterior compartment: Contains the hamstrings (biceps femoris with long and short heads, semitendinosus, semimembranosus). Innervated by the sciatic nerve (tibial portion for most; common peroneal portion for the short head of biceps). Functions as hip extension and knee flexion. The principal landmarks and neurovascular structures include: Femoral triangle: Bounded by the inguinal ligament superiorly, the sartorius laterally, and the adductor longus medially. Contains the femoral artery (laterally to medially: NAVEL, Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space, Lymphatic). Adductor canal (Hunter’s canal): A tunnel in the middle third of the thigh through which the femoral vessels pass deep to the sartorius. The artery becomes the popliteal artery as it emerges through the adductor hiatus into the popliteal fossa.

Popliteal fossa: The posterior knee region bounded by the biceps femoris (superolateral), the semimembranosus/semitendinosus (superomedial), and the two heads of gastrocnemius (inferiorly). Contains the popliteal artery, popliteal vein, and tibial/common peroneal nerves (the sciatic nerve divides at the apex of the fossa).

## Topography after the Classical Bulgarian School

The classical Bulgarian topographic school (Boychev) defines the regio femoris with the proximal border running along the inguinal fold, the gluteal fold, and the base of the greater trochanter, and the distal border lying 4 cm above the base of the patella anteriorly and 11–12 cm above the transverse popliteal fold posteriorly — corresponding to the upper margin of the suprapatellar bursa and the inferior aperture of the adductor canal. The region is divided into anterior (regio femoris anterior) and posterior (regio femoris posterior) parts. Along the anteromedial surface five to six branches of the femoral nerve (rr. cutanei femoris anteriores) emerge, while the lateral surface is innervated by the n. cutaneus femoris lateralis, which exits beneath the inguinal ligament and pierces the fascial sheath of the sartorius 1–2 cm medial to the anterior superior iliac spine. The great saphenous vein (v. saphena magna) runs along the anteromedial surface and pierces the fascia cribrosa at the saphenous hiatus 3–4 cm distal to the inguinal ligament and 3.5–4.5 cm lateral to the pubic tubercle before joining the femoral vein; knowing these consistent anatomical relations is critical for high ligation in thrombosis or varicose disease, and for harvest as a vein graft.

In the subcutaneous fat proximal to the entry of the saphenous vein, the superficial epigastric and superficial circumflex iliac arteries branch from the anterior surface of the femoral artery (either separately or as a common trunk) — vessels now used as axial pedicles for free SIEA and DIEP flaps in plastic surgery. The fascia lata is continuous across the entire thigh and thickens laterally into the iliotibial tract (6–7 cm wide proximally, narrowing distally); it gives off three intermuscular septa — lateral, medial, and posterior — attaching to the linea aspera and anatomically defining the three classical compartments.

In the posterior thigh region the fascia lata forms a dense lamina beneath which the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve runs longitudinally along the midline, piercing the fascia at the proximal border of the popliteal fossa. The sciatic nerve descends between the biceps femoris (laterally) and the semitendinosus/semimembranosus (medially); near the ischial tuberosity it crosses the tendon of the biceps femoris, and distally it lies on the posterior surface of the adductor magnus, covered by the inner edge of the biceps femoris. Between the femur and the adductors the three perforating arteries (from the profunda femoris) emerge: the first pierces the adductor magnus near the gluteal fold lateral to the nerve; the second 6–7 cm distally between the adductor magnus and the short head of the biceps femoris; the third another 6–7 cm distally. These vessels are encountered routinely in posterior femoral approaches and cause significant retraction bleeding if not carefully ligated.

## Approaches to the Femoral Shaft

The femoral shaft is approached for fracture fixation, tumor resection, and biopsy. The principal options: Anterolateral approach to the femur: Standard for plate fixation. Develops the interval between the vastus lateralis and the rectus femoris, with the rectus retracted medially. Provides good exposure with limited disruption of the principal blood supply. Posterolateral approach: Through the interval between the iliotibial band and the biceps femoris, providing posterolateral access to the femur. Used for plate fixation of distal femoral fractures ([Topic Trauma-24](/en/trauma/24/)). Posterior approach: Less commonly used; develops the interval between the medial and lateral hamstrings, with attention to the sciatic nerve. Provides access to the posterior femur. Antegrade and retrograde nailing entry: As described in [Topic Trauma-23](/en/trauma/23/), antegrade nailing uses a piriformis or trochanteric entry, and retrograde nailing uses an intercondylar notch entry through a small parapatellar arthrotomy.

The classical Bulgarian school emphasizes that the choice of approach to the femoral shaft is determined not so much by proximity to a given bony surface as by which part of the quadriceps will be cut or denervated. Because the motor branches of the femoral nerve enter the proximal vasti and divide obliquely across the muscle fibres, splitting the muscle belly of vastus lateralis, intermedius, or medialis denervates the peripheral portions and leads to fibrosis with postoperative extension or flexion contracture of the knee. For this reason the posterolateral approach — between vastus lateralis and the iliotibial tract, with no muscle-fibre cutting — is considered the only one that does not compromise the integrity of the quadriceps and that allows access to almost the entire shaft. Approaches that traverse the vastus intermedius (e.g. the classic Ollier anterior approach, which is not recommended for modern osteosynthesis) leave a fibrous loss of the vastus intermedius and extension contracture — a principal argument for the lateral approach in plate fixation.

## Proximal Lateral Approach

For surgery on the proximal shaft (subtrochanteric region, fractures with intertrochanteric extension, pin–plate or DHS fixation) the skin incision is longitudinal, beginning 5 cm above the tip of the greater trochanter and extending 10 cm distally; the fascia lata is incised over the greater trochanter and along the skin incision. The vastus lateralis is elevated in an L-shape from the tuberculum innominatum: the horizontal arm divides its initial aponeurosis, while the vertical arm continues distally. In the intertrochanteric region the horizontal branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery runs in the deepest layers of the muscle — almost on bone — and is always cut and must be ligated; in the subtrochanteric region, retraction of the posterior border of the vastus lateralis exposes the obliquely oriented terminal tendon of the gluteus maximus. At closure the vastus lateralis is reinserted at its original site to preserve extensor function.

## Approach to the Femoral Artery in the Femoral Triangle, Mid-Thigh, and the Adductor Canal

The femoral artery projects along a line drawn from the midpoint of the inguinal ligament to the adductor tubercle of the femur (the Gunter–Kronlein line). In the femoral triangle the incision bisects the apex of the triangle: the cribriform lamina is opened, the saphenofemoral junction is identified, the artery is atraumatically isolated, and three Deschamps-needle ligatures are passed beneath it. In the mid-thigh the incision follows the projection line — the fascia lata is opened along the medial border of the sartorius, the muscle is retracted laterally, and the neurovascular bundle is exposed. In the adductor (Hunter) canal the incision lies 1–1.5 cm behind and parallel to the projection line: the saphenous nerve is identified, the fascia lata is opened along the anterior border of the sartorius, the vastoadductor membrane is incised just beneath the nerve, and the vessels are exposed. After proximal ligation the limb is supplied by the deep femoral artery and obturator collaterals.

## Classical Intramedullary Nailing after Küntscher

The Küntscher intramedullary nail (Germany, 1939–1940), introduced in Bulgaria in the late 1940s and standardized by the Boychev school, remains the historical reference for modern interlocking nails. Classical indications include: transverse or short-oblique fractures of the femoral shaft in the middle third, a large third fragment in comminuted fractures, two-level fractures, and torsional fractures in which stable internal fixation permits early rehabilitation. Operation is recommended 2–3 days after the injury, with skeletal traction through the tibial tuberosity on admission to overcome longitudinal displacement of the fragments. The patient lies on the unaffected side, secured to the table with thoracic restraints and a belt across the foot and knee of the unaffected limb.

The classical technique is open — the fracture zone is exposed through a posterolateral or middle lateral approach, the distal fragment is freed and cleaned of clot with a curette, and the guide wire is driven into the cancellous bone of the femoral condyles. In skeletally immature patients the wire must not cross the distal physis. After radiographic control of distal fragment length, the proximal fragment is delivered into the wound, the guide wire is advanced into its medullary canal, and is brought out through the tip of the greater trochanter via a small gluteal incision. Over the guide wire the nail is driven, oriented so that the extraction slot faces laterally; the fracture is reduced, aligned, and the nail advanced into the distal fragment. For short-oblique fractures the fragments are pre-clamped with bone holders; when stable interfragmentary compression is not possible a screw or cerclage is added. Postoperatively, for unstable patterns, the classical recommendation was a plaster boot with an anti-rotational "fin" — a concern eliminated by modern interlocking nails. For malunions and nonunions of the femoral shaft, the Küntscher technique is combined with Judet decortication — circular elevation of thin osteoperiosteal lamellae around the pseudarthrosis with a wide chisel, forming a biologically active "osteoperiosteal sleeve" around the ends of the two main fragments, followed by canal reaming (12–16 mm), intramedullary nailing, and cancellous autografting from the iliac crest or tibial condyles.

## Compression Plate Osteosynthesis

Plate fixation of the femoral shaft is indicated for simultaneous femoral neck and shaft fractures, for some comminuted patterns where intramedullary nailing is technically impossible, and for limb-lengthening or shortening procedures. The approach is lateral (15–20 cm), centred at the fracture; the fascia lata is incised, dissection passes behind or through the fibres of vastus lateralis and intermedius to bone (within the muscle-sparing constraints described above), the periosteum is opened longitudinally, and the fragments are economically de-periosted. The plate must be long enough to allow at least four screw holes per main fragment, oriented so that the oblique slots or compression device face the distal fragment. The classical drawback is that the plate, stable at the moment of fixation, later undergoes local osteolysis, so in the historic technique additional plaster (spica) immobilisation is recommended. Modern LCP plates with angular-stable screws obviate the need for plaster when stable fixation is achieved.

## Femoral Lengthening

The classical femoral lengthening described by V. N. Bogoraz (1948) was performed by oblique or segmental osteotomy followed by prolonged skeletal traction to achieve the desired lengthening and plaster immobilisation until full consolidation — a method replaced in current practice by compression-distraction frames. The Ilizarov apparatus (Kurgan, 1954–1971), applied to the femur with a classical mounting of two half-rings (¾ circle) through the proximal and two full rings through the distal fragment, provides stable axial fixation and controlled lengthening; daily distraction is 1 mm in four ¼-mm steps. After reaching the target length the frame is retained until a dense regenerate has formed, followed when needed by a plaster cast through complete remodelling. Wagner lengthening (Germany, 1971) uses a monolateral distractor with secondary plate fixation and cancellous grafting of the distraction gap; the Wagner technique, effective up to 6–8 cm, gives way to circular frames when axial or rotational correction is needed. Later internal (intramedullary) lengthening devices — Albizzia (1990s) with mechanical distraction by rotational manipulations, ISKD, and PRECICE with magnetic/motorised systems — inherit the principle of Bliskunov (USSR, 1983), the first clinically successful intramedullary distraction system.

Lengthening across the distal physis (chondrodiastasis) in children over 6–8 years of age uses a circular frame with crossed wires either side of the growth plate and controlled distraction of 0.5–1 mm daily — a technique now largely supplanted by distraction epiphysiolysis with modern internal lengtheners.

## Femoral Shortening

Femoral shortening is indicated for leg-length discrepancy over 2–3 cm; the maximum allowable shortening without significant functional loss is 5–6 cm. The classical technique requires a lateral approach through the vastus lateralis, segmental resection of the diaphysis with a Gigli saw or oscillating burr, and Küntscher intramedullary nailing (after canal reaming to 12–14–16 mm). Alternatives include mid-diaphyseal segmental resection with an L-plate in the proximal third and an L-plate with transverse arm in the distal third. The Z-shaped (step-cut) segmental osteotomy of Boychev–Imamaliev provides a larger contact area and better rotational stability for subtrochanteric shortening.

## Distal Femoral Osteotomy and Supracondylar Corrections

Corrective supracondylar osteotomy of the femur (varus, valgus, flexion/extension rotational) is indicated for post-traumatic and post-paralytic deformities (genu valgum, varum, recurvatum, flexum) and for arthritis with lateral knee overload (valgus tibiofemoral angle). The approach is lateral. The classical technique uses an L-plate whose transverse arm is driven into the condyles perpendicular to the long axis of the femur, with the longitudinal arm fixed to the shaft with bicortical screws; fluoroscopic control confirms correct centering between both condyles. External compression osteosynthesis (with K-wires and a Greifensteiner clamp or an external fixator) is an alternative in osteoporotic bone or where plate fixation is technically difficult. For excessive recurvatum, supracondylar osteotomy with an anterior closing wedge and plate or external fixation is the method of choice. The distal femoral varising osteotomy is particularly useful in idiopathic or post-valgus genu valgum in young active patients with lateral compartment osteoarthritis, where a high tibial osteotomy would create patellar tilt.

For Salter–Harris type III–IV physeal fractures of the distal femur (Topic Trauma-24), open reduction and fixation with K-wires or cannulated screws perpendicular to the physis is mandatory, since uncorrected physeal injuries lead to growth disturbance and axial deformity; compression across two K-wires in a Greifensteiner clamp accelerates consolidation.

## Skeletal Traction through the Greater Trochanter and the Condyles

Classical skeletal traction through the greater trochanter is indicated for central acetabular fracture-dislocation, Malgaigne pelvic fractures, and as a fixation anchor for femoral lengthening; a 3 mm K-wire is passed through the trochanter via a tunnel guide that grips the trochanter from front and back. Condylar traction is passed extra-articularly using the reference points: the superior edge of the femoral condyles (in profile — the centre of the knee joint); the wire is passed from the medial to the lateral condyle with a guide (or with a Smith-Petersen pin). For lower-traction loads (8–10 kg) — fresh fractures, reduced fracture-dislocations, acetabular floor fractures — traction through the tibial tuberosity is preferred, since it avoids the articular zone.

The classical Bulgarian topographic school divides the regio genu into anterior and posterior areas with a boundary defined by two longitudinal lines descending in a slight curve behind the femoral condyles; the lateral line crosses the biceps femoris tendon at the joint line. The proximal border of the region is a circumferential line 4 cm above the base of the patella (anteriorly) and 11–12 cm above the inferior popliteal fold (posteriorly); the distal border is the level of the tibial tuberosity anteriorly and 7–8 cm below the popliteal fold posteriorly.

In the anterior region the great saphenous vein runs distally along the medial border of the tibia (about 1.5 cm from it), arcs posteriorly around the medial femoral condyle, and ascends along the posterior border of the sartorius. The saphenous nerve and the descending genicular artery emerge superficially behind the posterior border of the sartorius at the level of the upper pole of the patella; the saphenous nerve gives off its infrapatellar branch, which crosses the operative field between the patella and the tibial tuberosity — its transection in surgical approaches is almost inevitable and produces the characteristic numbness over the medial subpatellar region. The arterial network around the patella is formed by four sources — the superomedial, superolateral, inferomedial, and inferolateral genicular arteries — forming a ring around the patella that supplies the joint capsule, patellar tendon, and the Hoffa fat pad. The vertical limbs of the classical Textor horseshoe approach divide all four sources — its principal drawback.

The anterior tibial recurrent artery emerges from depth along the lateral border of the patellar tendon at the level of the tibial tuberosity; the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery pierces the fascia lata above the upper pole of the patella, lateral to the quadriceps tendon. Behind the vastus lateralis the iliotibial tract appears as a broad silvery band separating the anterolateral and posterolateral zones; medially, the tendon of the adductor magnus to the adductor tubercle is an important bony reference for distal femoral osteotomy and supracondylar approaches.

In the posterior region the popliteal fossa is rhomboid; its borders are the biceps femoris (superolateral), semitendinosus/semimembranosus (superomedial), and the two heads of gastrocnemius (inferior). On its floor, in three successive levels, lie the facies poplitea of the femur (proximal), the posterior capsule with the oblique and arcuate popliteal ligaments (at condylar level), and the popliteus muscle with the capsule of the proximal tibiofibular joint (distal). The sciatic nerve usually divides 10–12 cm proximal to the joint line (and occasionally at the level of the sacral plexus); the common peroneal nerve runs along the medial border of the biceps femoris tendon and around the fibular head (Topic Trauma-31); the tibial nerve sits in the central fat between the two heads of gastrocnemius. The popliteal artery lies deepest, on the joint capsule, with the popliteal vein behind it and the tibial nerve still more superficial; from it arise the genicular arteries (superior and inferior, medial and lateral), the middle genicular artery (passing directly through the intercondylar notch into the joint), and branches to the two heads of gastrocnemius. Division of the popliteal artery into the anterior tibial artery and the tibioperoneal trunk lies 2–3 cm distal to the inferior margin of the popliteus, in the zone of the soleus arcus tendineus — a fixed point that explains the vulnerability of the popliteal artery to distal tibial displacement and to knee dislocation.

## Knee Approaches

The classical knee approaches are numerous and bear the names of their authors; familiarity with them is required not only for historical context but because most modern approaches are their direct modifications.

## Textor Horseshoe Approach

The Textor U-shaped ("horseshoe") approach uses a transverse limb along the proximal margin of the patella and two vertical limbs along its sides, beginning at the epicondyles; it gives wide exposure of the entire joint by flipping the patella distally with the patellar tendon. The oblique orientation of the vertical limbs and their proximal extension preserves the proximal patellar blood supply. Because the extensor mechanism is divided, the classical recommendation is osteotomy of the tibial tuberosity rather than transverse division of the patellar tendon; if the tuberosity is osteotomised, the horizontal limb can be displaced distally over it. This approach was historically the basis for total knee arthroplasty before the medial parapatellar approach became standard; it is now used mainly for revision scenarios and wide intra-articular procedures.

## Pütti Horseshoe Approach

The Pütti approach has two parts — a downward-opening horseshoe around the patella, plus a vertical midline limb proximally (6–8 cm). The proximal skin flaps are reflected, and the medial and lateral limbs descend 2–3 cm from the patellar edges anterior to the collateral ligaments to below the joint line. The quadriceps tendon is then split in a Z-shape in the frontal plane, its anterior half divided transversely proximally, and the flap with the patella turned distally — exposing the anterior compartment widely. This approach is essentially an alternative to Textor with better preservation of patellar blood supply.

## Cogswell–Adam Approach (Medial Parapatellar)

The Cogswell–Adam approach uses a longitudinal medial parapatellar skin incision and divides the capsule immediately medial to the patella, reaching the entire joint including the suprapatellar bursa. If necessary, the tibial tuberosity may be osteotomised. The approach has been modified by Payer and Fischer with regard to the skin incision and was among the most used until the standard medial parapatellar approach became dominant.

## Anterolateral Approach of Kocher

The Kocher anterolateral approach uses a parapatellar skin incision along the border of the vastus lateralis, beginning 8 cm above the patella and ending 2 cm below the tibial tuberosity; the interval between vastus lateralis and the quadriceps tendon is sharply developed proximally and the retinaculum and capsule are incised distally. The approach is less convenient than the medial parapatellar — the patella is more difficult to displace medially than laterally because of the asymmetric femoral condyles — so the patellar tendon often has to be released from the tibial tuberosity. Indications are confined to specific scenarios: severe valgus gonarthrosis at TKA, lateral patellar instability, and lateral tibial plateau fracture.

## External Approach of Abbott–Carpenter

The Abbott–Carpenter external approach begins in the distal sulcus femoris lateralis 6 cm proximal to the fibular head, runs distally along the anterior border of the biceps femoris, and at the joint line curves slightly anteriorly in an S-shape past the patellar tendon to the tibial tuberosity. The approach exposes the lateral collateral ligament, the entire lateral side of the joint, and, if needed, the posterolateral zone; its main indications are revision of the posterolateral corner, fibular head fixation, and lateral collateral ligament reconstruction. Identification and protection of the common peroneal nerve along the medial border of the biceps femoris tendon to the fibular head is critical — the nerve is most superficial at this point.


## Medial Parapatellar Approach

The medial parapatellar approach is the classical and most commonly used approach for total knee arthroplasty, knee arthrotomy, and many open knee procedures. The approach uses a longitudinal anterior midline skin incision, with the deep arthrotomy beginning at the medial border of the quadriceps tendon, extending distally along the medial border of the patella, and continuing distally along the medial border of the patellar tendon. The quadriceps tendon is incised approximately 1 to 2 cm medial to the patella to leave a cuff for closure. The patella is then everted (or simply translated) laterally to expose the joint. The approach provides excellent visualization of the entire joint but disrupts the vastus medialis insertion on the patella, with consequent quadriceps weakness in the short term and contribution to extensor mechanism imbalance. The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is partially disturbed, and may contribute to lateral subluxation if not properly reconstructed.

## Midvastus Approach

The midvastus approach preserves a larger portion of the vastus medialis by splitting the muscle in line with its fibers rather than detaching its insertion on the patella. The approach develops a muscle-splitting plane through the vastus medialis obliquus (VMO) from the medial border of the patella for approximately 4 to 5 cm proximally. The approach provides similar exposure to the medial parapatellar with theoretical preservation of extensor mechanism integrity.

## Subvastus Approach

The subvastus approach preserves the entire vastus medialis by elevating the muscle from the medial femur (rather than splitting it) and reflecting it laterally. The approach provides the greatest preservation of extensor mechanism but requires significant elevation of the muscle and has more limited exposure of the lateral compartment.

## Lateral Parapatellar Approach

The lateral parapatellar approach is the mirror image of the medial parapatellar but is less commonly used. It is reserved for specific indications (severe valgus deformity where the medial structures must be approached from outside-in, lateral patellar instability surgery).

## Posterior Approach (Burks-Schaffer Modification of Henderson)

The posterior approach to the knee uses an S-shaped or hockey-stick incision in the popliteal fossa. The approach is used for PCL tibial inlay reconstruction, posterior tibial plateau fixation, posterior capsular procedures, and exploration of the popliteal vessels and tibial nerve. The medial head of the gastrocnemius is retracted laterally to expose the posterior tibia. The popliteal vessels and tibial nerve are identified and protected.

## Specific Approaches for Tibial Plateau Fractures

For lateral tibial plateau fractures (Schatzker I, II, III), the approach is typically through an anterolateral incision with a submeniscal arthrotomy to allow direct visualization of the articular surface ([Topic Trauma-25](/en/trauma/25/)). For medial tibial plateau fractures (Schatzker IV), a medial approach is used. For bicondylar fractures (Schatzker V, VI), dual approaches (medial and lateral) with separate incisions are preferred to avoid the central anterior incision and the associated soft-tissue compromise.

## Approaches for Meniscectomy

The classical approaches for open meniscectomy — predating arthroscopy — were oblique, transverse, posterior-convex, and anterior-convex. The patient lies supine with the knee flexed to 90° and the leg hanging over the table edge. The oblique approaches (Erkes — along a line from the femoral epicondyle to the tibial tuberosity; Mueller–Köller, Hofmann — variants with different obliquity) lie between the anterior border of the collateral ligament and the patellar edge, without descending more than 1–2 cm below the joint line. The transverse approaches (Platt, Charnley, Cohen) with a 5 cm incision at the joint line between the patella and the collateral ligament give good exposure of the anterior and middle thirds of the meniscus. The posterior-convex approaches (Bruser) use an arched incision passing from behind the collateral ligament anteriorly to the tibial tuberosity — particularly useful for isolated posterior horn lesions. The anterior-convex approaches (Smillie; Smillie–Fischer with U-shaped extension) begin at the lateral patellar edge, cross the joint line, and end on the tibial condyle; the proximal limb permits wide inspection of the anterior compartment. Open total medial meniscectomy after Smillie with a horseshoe medial approach and use of the Smillie meniscotome (straight for the anterior and middle segments, curved for the posterior horn) was the standard of the 1950–1980 era — a technique replaced by partial arthroscopic meniscectomy after the Fairbank (1948) demonstration of the radiographic consequences of meniscectomy.

## Posterior Approach to the Popliteal Fossa

The posterior approach to the popliteal fossa (for popliteal artery reconstruction, exploration of the tibial and common peroneal nerves, capsular exploration, removal of a Baker cyst) uses a Z-shaped ("lazy S") incision whose proximal limb runs along the semitendinosus tendon to the joint line, crosses the popliteal fossa transversely along a skin crease for about 5 cm, and descends along the lateral head of the gastrocnemius. After elevating the flaps the fascia poplitea is opened around the small saphenous vein and medial sural cutaneous nerve; in the groove between the two heads of gastrocnemius lies the neurovascular bundle: tibial nerve superficially, popliteal vein, then popliteal artery (deepest, on the joint capsule). The intermuscular approach between the two heads of gastrocnemius (Henderson, as modified by Burks–Schaffer) with medial retraction of the medial gastrocnemius head is standard for PCL tibial inlay reconstruction. The classical approach to the common peroneal nerve (along the medial border of the biceps femoris tendon to the fibular head) is separate — the nerve is easily found by flexing the knee and retracting the biceps tendon either anteriorly or posteriorly.

## Classical Open Meniscectomy

The classical Smillie/Boychev technique for medial meniscectomy uses two approaches — parapatellar (for the anterior and middle segments) and retroligamentous posterior (for the posterior horn). The anterior segment is mobilised with a straight meniscotome, dissected from the paracapsular zone, and retracted with a meniscus holder. The posterior horn is detached through a second retroligamentous incision with a curved meniscotome, which is advanced posteriorly toward the posterior capsule, fixed, and the meniscus is pulled anteriorly against its cutting surface — a technique designed to protect structures in the popliteal fossa (the popliteal artery passes some 5–7 mm from the posterior horn of the medial meniscus). For a bucket-handle tear the central fragment is divided transversely from its anterior attachment, grasped with a Smillie meniscus holder, and pulled forward; the posterior attachment is divided blindly with a curved meniscotome. Lateral meniscectomy is performed in the "figure-of-four" (Cabot) position — hip flexion, knee flexion, and external rotation — to inspect the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus, which is more firmly tethered by the meniscofemoral ligaments (Humphrey anterior, Wrisberg posterior) to the PCL. After De Haven (1979), Jackson and Dandy (1976), and Gillquist (1971), partial arthroscopic meniscectomy and meniscal repair (inside-out, outside-in, all-inside) have become standard; classical open meniscectomy is now essentially abandoned.

Knee arthroscopy was introduced by Masaki Watanabe (Japan, 1959 — Watanabe Arthroscope No. 21) and developed by Robert Jackson, John Joyce, Lanny Johnson, Richard O'Connor, and Heshmat Shahriaree in the 1970s. The classical portals remain the main standard:

— Standard anterior portals: anterolateral (1 cm above the joint line, lateral to the patellar tendon; the standard viewing portal) and anteromedial (1 cm above the joint line, medial to the patellar tendon; the working portal). The anteromedial portal is typically localized by an "outside-in" needle technique under direct arthroscopic visualisation for precise positioning relative to the medial meniscus and the ACL anterior footprint at reconstruction.

— Suprapatellar portals: superolateral (3 cm proximal to the upper pole of the patella, lateral to the quadriceps tendon) and superomedial — for outflow, inspection of the suprapatellar pouch, and patellofemoral joint visualisation in depth.

— Posterior portals: posteromedial (1 cm above the joint line, just behind the medial collateral ligament, in the "soft spot" between the medial femoral condyle and the semimembranosus tendon) — for inspection of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus, the tibial footprint of the PCL, and the posteromedial recess. Posterolateral (between the biceps femoris tendon and the iliotibial band) — mandatory caution regarding the common peroneal nerve (lying about 1 cm proximal to the joint line along the medial border of the biceps tendon).

— Transseptal portal (Gillquist) — through the intercondylar septum between the anterolateral and posteromedial portals — allows access to the posterolateral compartment without a separate posterior incision.

Arthroscopic examination follows a systematic sequence: suprapatellar pouch, patellofemoral joint (patellar cartilage by Outerbridge classification), medial and lateral gutters, medial compartment (meniscus, anteromedial, middle and posterior — with screw-home test, valgus stress for opening), intercondylar notch (ACL, PCL, Hoffa fat pad, lig. mucosum), lateral compartment (meniscus, popliteus tendon, popliteomeniscal fascicles), and, when needed, the posteromedial and posterolateral compartments through the posterior portals.

## Knee Synovectomy

Classical open total/subtotal synovectomy of the knee is indicated for rheumatoid arthritis without major joint destruction (Preston: if no remission after 4–6 months, do not delay), pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS), chronic traumatic and essential synovitis, and historically for the synovial form of joint tuberculosis. The approach is medial parapatellar (Payer) with proximal release of the rectus femoris from the vastus medialis; the bulging synovium is opened along the incision, the patella is retracted laterally, and the anteromedial, anterolateral, and suprapatellar recesses are exposed. Resection begins from the suprapatellar bursa and proceeds distally, around the medial and lateral gutters and the Hoffa fat pad (usually infiltrated by pannus). The posterior horns of the menisci obstruct full posterior recess synovectomy — for which posteromedial and posterolateral incisions are added in about 30% of cases; most authors, however, regard subtotal anterior synovectomy as sufficient for the rheumatoid process. The menisci are preserved unless torn; where pannus invades articular cartilage the affected zones are curetted. Haemostasis with electrocautery; suction drainage for 48 h. Following Christensen (1965) and Storti (1973) the early postoperative mobilisation and factor VIII/IX replacement are mandatory in haemophilic patients. Arthroscopic synovectomy is a technical alternative in current practice and is preferred for younger and selected patients.

## Acute Medial Soft-Tissue Repair of the Knee (Classical Hughston and Boychev Techniques)

For acute anteromedial, posteromedial, or anterior global instability of the knee (traumatic disruption of MCL, ACL, and/or medial meniscus) an extended medial parapatellar approach is used. After elevation of the pes anserinus to inspect the tibial insertion of the MCL, a parapatellar fascial incision is made up to the medial border of the vastus medialis. The medial meniscus, ACL, patellar articular surface, lateral meniscus, and PCL are systematically inspected. Transchondral meniscal tears require removal (or, modern: repair). Torn cruciates are repaired with transosseous Vicryl or wire sutures in the classical way: a femoral tunnel is drilled in the lateral condyle if the femoral footprint of the ACL is avulsed, and the ligament sutures are brought out through it; if the tibial insertion (eminentia intercondylaris) is avulsed, it is fixed with a screw or a transosseous suture.

For medial capsular ligament (MCL) lesions: femoral-end lesions are reinserted with sutures or a staple to the medial epicondyle; mid-substance lesions are repaired with non-absorbable sutures; tibial avulsions are reinserted transosseously. For posteromedial capsule injury the oblique popliteal ligament is reinserted to the adductor tubercle through bone tunnels and a staple (Hughston), and the tibial insertion is repaired through transtibial tunnels and Vicryl or Dexon sutures tied anteriorly (Hughston). With interrupted mattress sutures the oblique popliteal ligament is sewn to the semimembranosus tendon and subsequently to the reconstructed MCL.

## Acute Lateral Soft-Tissue Repair of the Knee

For acute posterolateral instability (traumatic disruption of LCL, popliteus, popliteofibular ligament, or biceps tendon) an anterolateral incision (12–14 cm) is made, beginning 3–4 cm above the patella, along the line of the iliotibial tract, and ending 3 cm below the Gerdy tubercle. The common peroneal nerve is identified and protected. Reinsertion of the popliteus and LCL to the lateral epicondyle is performed "up and forward" with transosseous sutures, gently tensioned at 30° of flexion and external rotation of the tibia — a position that ensures proper tensioning. Posterior to the LCL the posterior capsule and arcuate ligament are reinserted to the tibia through transosseous tunnels. After repair a long-leg cast at 70° flexion and external tibial rotation is applied for 6 weeks.

ACL reconstruction has a century-long history; the literature describes over 200 techniques in three categories: intra-articular (replacement along the anatomical path of the ACL), extra-articular (dynamic stabilisation by posterolateral tenodesis), and combined (central reconstruction + lateral augmentation).

Hey-Groves (1917, England) was first with intra-articular reconstruction using the iliotibial tract through femoral and tibial tunnels. Macey (1939) used a free strip of iliotibial tract. Jones (1963) and Lindemann (1950) described techniques with the central third of the patellar tendon and a bone block from the tibial tuberosity through tunnels — the conceptual ancestor of the modern BPTB (bone–patellar tendon–bone). Lemaire (France, 1967) developed an extra-articular iliotibial tract tenodesis for chronic anterolateral instability. MacIntosh (Canada, 1972, 1976) combined intra- and extra-articular procedures with a strip of iliotibial tract passed through the intercondylar notch — the "over-the-top" MacIntosh. Kennedy and Insall (1979) developed central quadriceps tendon reconstruction (the precursor of LARS).

A special role belongs to the Soviet orthopaedist Landa (Moscow, 1947), who described a technique in which one third of the central patellar tendon is harvested together with part of the quadriceps tendon above the patella and the femoral aponeurosis — a strip passed through tibial and femoral bone tunnels. This operation was "rediscovered" in the West by Kenneth Jones (1963) and is widely cited as the Kenneth–Jones procedure, but the priority is Russo-Bulgarian. The Bulgarian school continued with the Marshall modification (4 cm proximal extension of the tendon part, 180° fold, and overlap), Shoylev (1982) — combining a patellar tendon graft with pes anserinus transposition (Slocum–Larson) and lateral augmentation, with results in 37 patients with chronic anterior global instability — and Lindemann–Ficat (with semitendinosus). Arthroscopic ACL reconstruction with BPTB or hamstring autograft (semitendinosus and gracilis quadrupled) is the current standard; all-inside, double-bundle, and quadriceps tendon techniques are specific variants.

## PCL Reconstruction

PCL reconstruction is indicated in chronic instability with functional impairment (instability on kneeling — the "kneeling test"), diffuse posterior pain, unexplained recurrent effusion, and combined injuries. In chronic ruptures reinsertion and direct repair are almost impossible. The classical palliative techniques:

— Augustine (1956) — central reconstruction with the medial third of the patellar tendon through a tibial tunnel; immobilisation in extension for 6 weeks.

— Hughston (1969) — dynamic reconstruction with the medial half of the medial gastrocnemius head tendon (released from the muscle without detaching the femoral attachment), routed through a medial femoral tunnel and fixed in the tibia — providing a dynamic effect at flexion. J. Hughston (Lyon, 1979; New Orleans) reported satisfactory results in more than 30 patients.

— Trickey (1968) — reconstruction by the tibial inlay technique through a posterior popliteal approach, using a split semitendinosus tendon.

— Insall — reconstruction with the medial meniscus (as a free meniscocapsular graft) when the PCL cannot otherwise be addressed — with variable success.

Modern arthroscopic tibial-tunnel or tibial-inlay techniques with BPTB, Achilles allograft, or quadriceps tendon are standard.

## Pes Anserinus Transposition after Slocum–Larson

For chronic anteromedial rotational instability, pes anserinus transposition after Slocum–Larson (1968) provides dynamic valgus, anterior, and rotational stability. Requirements: intact posterior capsule and intact PCL. The approach is an extended medial; the pes anserinus (sartorius, gracilis, and semitendinosus tendons) is identified, and a tendinous-aponeurotic flap is fashioned from the superomedial tibia. Its free proximal end is folded proximally, passed beneath the MCL, and sewn to the medial border of the patellar tendon with interrupted sutures beginning distally (so that tension is graduated). The free proximal part of the semitendinosus tendon is attached to the periosteum of the medial tibial plateau to prevent displacement. Modern variants add a caudalised insertion of the vastus medialis for additional dynamic stabilisation.

## Tibial Tuberosity Osteotomy — Elmslie–Trillat Technique and Modifications

For anteromedial rotational instability and lateral patellar instability, the Elmslie–Trillat (Trillat, 1964) tibial tuberosity osteotomy and medialisation is the standard adjunct. An anterior medial incision from the proximal vastus medialis insertion around the tibial tuberosity is made 4–5 cm distally; the patellar tendon is released medially and laterally, and with a wide straight osteotome a frontal-plane osteotomy of the tibial tuberosity is performed beginning proximal to the patellar tendon insertion. The tuberosity is rotated medially 8–11 mm and caudalised 4–5 mm (for patella alta correction) before being fixed with one or two compression AO screws. Medialisation requires a lateral retinacular release. The classical Hauser procedure (1938) uses the same principle but with more aggressive medialisation — a technique abandoned because of chronic patellofemoral overload and arthrosis. Maquet (1963, 1976) added ventralisation ("anteromedialisation") with a bone block beneath the tibial tuberosity to unload the patellofemoral joint (1.5–2.5 cm of elevation) — later modified by Fulkerson (1983) into the anteromedialisation osteotomy with an obliquely angled cut for simultaneous anteriorisation and medialisation, optimal for lateral and distal patellar cartilage lesions.

## Classical Procedures for Recurrent Patellar Dislocation

Recurrent and habitual patellar dislocations have been treated with dozens of described techniques. In the Bulgarian curriculum the classical names are:

— Krogius method (Ali Krogius, 1904, Finland) — fashioning a 20 cm medial capsulo-fascial strip incorporating the distal fibres of the vastus medialis, passed through a lateral capsular incision for tightening; the medial space is filled by a lateral parapatellar flap.

— Roux method (1888) and Roux–Goldthwait (Goldthwait, 1899) — the lateral half of the patellar tendon together with a bone block from the tibial tuberosity is released and transferred medially and caudally; the middle third stays in place. This is the conceptual ancestor of modern distal patellar realignment in skeletally immature children (where tuberosity osteotomy is contraindicated).

— Brucknér method — the medial third of the patellar tendon with a bone block from the tibial tuberosity is released and transferred medially over the anteromedial tibia; an adult alternative.

— Boychev method — a tendomuscular flap from the vastus medialis (with a VMO incision) is passed through a longitudinal lateral capsular incision and sewn to the patella and medial capsule. The patellar tendon strip with part of the tibial tuberosity is attached to the anteromedial tibia; the lateral capsule is left open (release).

— Insall method (1972) — proximal realignment by medialisation of the VMO over the patella and lateral retinacular release; combined with Elmslie–Trillat for distal malalignment.

— Madigan method (1975) and Stanisavljević (1976) — for habitual patellar dislocation in children with severe dysplasia: extensive "derotation" of the quadriceps mechanism and tibial tuberosity.

— Shoylev method (1983) — combined technique: lateral retinacular release after Ficat (resection of a 7–8 × 1.5–2 cm rectangle of lateral retinaculum, fibrous capsule, ligamentum alare, and distal vastus lateralis), followed by Elmslie–Trillat medialisation and caudalisation of the tibial tuberosity with compression screws and proximal VMO advancement. Shoylev reported 60 cases with very good clinical outcomes.

In acute patellar dislocation with disruption of the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL — described as a discrete anatomical entity by Warren and Marshall, 1979; its reconstruction has been standard since the 2000s), reconstruction with a gracilis or semitendinosus autograft and anatomic fixation at the Schöttle point on the medial epicondyle is the current standard.

High tibial osteotomy was first described by Jackson (1958) and standardised by Coventry (Mayo, 1965) as a lateral closing wedge for varus gonarthrosis with medial unicompartmental arthritis. The classical Coventry technique: a horseshoe incision below the fibular head, osteotomy of the fibular shaft to permit release (or fibular head resection for severe valgus correction), outlining and gradual removal of a wedge with a base of 1 mm per 1° correction; the medial cortex is perforated with small osteotomes without fracture (hinge stability), and after valgisation the two fragments are fixed with staples anterior to the fibula. Two parallel tunnels are drilled through the fibular head and two more through the tibial fragments, into which a Greifensteiner or Charnley compression clamp is incorporated for compression. Long-leg cast for 6 weeks. The Holevich, Dzherov, and Vladimirov modification (Bulgaria, 1970s) preserves the fibular head, osteotomises the fibula at the level of the diaphyseal beginning with resection of a 1–1.5 cm segment, and fixes the two fibular fragments with a long cancellous AO screw with washer, using the fibular head as a lever to put the lateral collateral ligament and biceps femoris tendon under tension — a real advantage in associated joint instability.

The high ovoid (dome) osteotomy of Maquet–Bardot was performed by an anterior longitudinal approach, periosteal stripping of the tibia on each side of the patellar tendon, dome-shaped osteotomy with an oscillating saw (concavity superiorly), correction to the desired valgus position, and compression fixation with Steinmann pins and a compressor. The high opening-wedge osteotomy after Maquet and Puddu (1990s, with the Puddu plate) opens a medial wedge by gradual distraction and inserts a dedicated plate with a wedge "foot"; classical fillers are tricortical iliac allograft wedges or autografts from the crest. For corrections over 15–20°, the fibula is first osteotomised. Distal femoral varising osteotomy (medial opening-wedge or lateral closing-wedge) is the analogue for valgus gonarthrosis with lateral compartment arthritis in young patients, where HTO would create an oblique joint line.

## Knee Arthrodesis

For resistant joint infection, severe Charcot destruction, failed total knee arthroplasty, and post-paralytic instability, arthrodesis remains a "salvage" option. The classical techniques:

— Charnley arthrodesis (1948) — compression arthrodesis with an external compression frame; the tibia and femur are cut transversely at the joint line, articular surfaces are debrided to cancellous bone and apposed under compression (10° flexion, 10° external rotation). The Charnley external frame with two groups of Steinmann pins and compression screws maintains the position until bony union (6–8 weeks).

— Lucas–Cottrell arthrodesis (1955) — resection of the articular surfaces with intramedullary fixation by a long Küntscher nail passed antegrade from the greater trochanter through the femur, across the joint (with canal reaming), into the tibia; effective in young, active patients.

— Ilizarov arthrodesis — circular external frame with compression and, if needed, simultaneous axis and length correction (particularly useful for septic non-union and significant defects requiring bone transport).

Knee arthrodesis is contraindicated in children under 6–7 years of age because of growth. The fusion position is critical — 10° flexion and 10° external tibial rotation (for optimal gait).

Total knee arthroplasty evolved from Walldius (1951, hinged total replacement), Insall–Burstein (1974, condylar — basis of the modern standard), Charnley (1958, polycentric), the geometric prosthesis (Geomedic, 1973), and the semi-total Robert Brigham (1976). The classical medial parapatellar approach, distal femoral osteotomies (95° or 97° to the mechanical axis), proximal tibial osteotomy (90° to the tibial mechanical axis), and resection of the menisci and cruciate ligaments (in posterior-stabilized designs) are standard. The Bandl approach (Walter Bandl, described in the Bulgarian curriculum) uses an arched incision starting from the medial epicondyle, crossing the tibial tuberosity, and ending at the lateral epicondyle — an alternative to the midline anterior incision in difficult closure scenarios.

In younger patients (under 60 years) with unicompartmental gonarthrosis (medial or lateral) unicompartmental arthroplasty remains a good alternative to TKA — the technique was optimised by Marmor (1972) and Goodfellow and O'Connor (Oxford UKA, 1976) and requires intact ACL and PCL, varus/valgus deformity under 10–15°, flexion contracture under 20°, and range of motion of at least 70°. Patellectomy for severe chondromalacia or chronic patellofemoral arthrosis (as a stand-alone procedure or as part of arthroplasty when patellar pain dominates) was described by Compère (1937) and Boyd (1955); in the classical technique the patella is removed, the extensor expansion is plicated, and the lateral retinacula are approximated at the midline. Patelloplasty (resurfacing with the lateral half of the patella, facet upward) is a classical alternative to patellectomy.

## Distal Femoral and Proximal Tibial Resection with Allograft

For periarticular bone tumours (osteoclastoma, parosteal osteosarcoma) in patients over 50 years, or where a suitable allograft to preserve motion is not available, allograft arthrodesis remains an effective option. The bone is cut in a wedge distal or proximal to the tumour, the opposing articular cartilage and patella are excised, the deep-frozen allograft is shaped to the defect and fixed by a long Küntscher nail (minimum 14 mm) through the intramedullary canal of femur and tibia (antegrade through the greater trochanter). Complications include immune rejection (sterile seroma which may become secondarily infected), allograft infection (requiring evacuation and prolonged antibiotics), and late fixation failure.

When motion is to be preserved (younger patients, suitable allograft) the articular fragment with the tumour is excised, the defect is replaced with an allograft bearing an appropriate articular surface, fixed with two bolts with locknuts or a suitable plate; the joint capsule, MCL (and LCL if released) and the cruciate ligaments are reinserted to the graft. Allograft-prosthetic composites (APC) are the current standard.

## Repair of Quadriceps Tendon and Patellar Tendon

Ruptures of the quadriceps tendon (typically 1–2 cm proximal to the patella) and of the patellar tendon (typically distally, at the patellar insertion) require urgent operative repair. The classical technique uses a transverse arched incision above the upper pole of the patella (or a longitudinal anterior incision for the patellar tendon), identifies the rupture, evacuates haematoma, and approximates the tendon edges in extension. Repair is by interrupted non-absorbable sutures (Tycron, Ethibond) or chromic catgut. In chronic ruptures the Codivilla lengthening (a triangular flap with proximal base, flipped distally and sutured to the distal end) or Scuderi V-Y plasty is standard. A protective wire suture after Bunnell (from the tibial tuberosity or patella) is added in uncertain repairs; the wire is removed at 4 months.

For patellar tendon rupture the suture between the ends is reinforced with a transosseous wire cerclage from the tibial tuberosity through the quadriceps tendon above the patella, fixed without tension. McLaughlin offers a modification with a transverse pin through the patella (or the tibial tuberosity in patellar tendon rupture) and a wire passed through the proximal end of the tendon and fixed to the pin bilaterally. In chronic patellar tendon ruptures with proximal patellar retraction the operation is staged: initial mobilisation of the patella with distal traction for 2–3 weeks, followed by reconstruction with a semitendinosus or Achilles tendon autograft.

## Classical Operations for Flexion Contracture of the Knee

For spastic flexion contracture from hamstring hypertonia, the classical technique is transposition of the flexors from the ischiopubic origin to the femoral condyles (Eggers, 1952; modified by Keat and Smillie): the biceps femoris, semitendinosus, and semimembranosus tendons are released from their tibial insertions, drawn proximally, and sutured to the femoral condyles (medial for semitendinosus/semimembranosus, lateral for biceps), converting them from biarticular to monoarticular hip extensors. For internal tibial rotation the technique is modified — the branches of the sciatic nerve to the semimembranosus and semitendinosus are resected (Hoel resection), preserving the biceps for external rotation. The Baker and Hill operation for correction of internal hip rotation transposes the semitendinosus through a subcutaneous tunnel to the lateral femur, fixed beneath a preformed osteoperiosteal flap.

For quadriceps paralysis with severe knee instability, classical transposition of the flexors to the patella (biceps femoris and semitendinosus tendons routed through two oblique subcutaneous tunnels on either side of the patella and fixed beneath periosteal flaps or in subperiosteal tunnels) is the standard (Compère, 1939). Conforti modifies the technique with two parallel longitudinal tunnels along the full length of the patella, through which split tendon ends are passed with a guide.

For flexion contracture from gastrocnemius hypertonia (particularly in cerebral-palsy children), proximal release after Silfverskiöld is performed — a transverse popliteal incision parallel to the skin crease, the transverse fascia is opened, the tibial nerve and motor branches to both heads of gastrocnemius are identified, and one or two branches are divided. The technique is commonly combined with hamstring lengthening.

## Knee Denervation (after Bateman) and Sensory Branch Resection

Knee denervation (Bateman, 1968) is indicated for severe osteoarthritis in patients who wish to retain motion (an alternative to arthrodesis or to arthroplasty). The sensory branches to the knee are three: a branch from the posterior tibial nerve (to the posterior capsule), a branch from the obturator nerve (to the anteromedial side, arising from the musculotendinous part of the vastus medialis — may be absent), and a branch from the nerve to vastus intermedius (to the suprapatellar bursa, along the midline of the anterior femoral surface). Denervation is performed in two positions — first prone (posterior approach to the popliteal fossa, identification of the tibial nerve and the motor branches to the two heads of gastrocnemius, isolation of the sensory branch to the posterior capsule, division and avulsion), then supine (medial parapatellar approach with identification of the branch to vastus intermedius and, if found, the obturator branch medially). Where the medial branch is preserved the procedure is considered incomplete — but it still allows significant pain relief in moderate arthritis.

## Classical Above-Knee Amputation

The choice of level for above-knee amputation is dictated by modern prosthetic requirements — the optimal level is 10–12 cm above the knee, providing a stump long enough to control the prosthesis in all directions. The distal 7–8 cm of the femur are unsuitable because of prosthetic joint geometry, and stumps under 5 cm because of insufficient muscle control. The classical technique uses two semicircular flaps in a 2:1 anterior:posterior ratio. Bone is divided 8–10 cm above the desired final stump level. Myoplasty — fixing the quadriceps and adductors to the posterior muscle segment to close the femoral canal — provides a load-bearing surface. A drain is brought out distal to the wound.

For severely contaminated trauma, wartime injuries, and gas gangrene an open amputation is performed by the Pirogoff method — a circumferential resection modified with two semicircular skin flaps, the anterior higher and the posterior lower, left open. The wound is dressed with compresses soaked in Vishnevsky balm and planned for delayed primary closure (on day 4–5 if locally favourable) or late secondary closure after granulation. Osteoplastic amputations after Yu. A. Dzhanelidze (1953) using a periosteal-bone cap (osteoplastic coverage) had historical advantages — they are rarely performed today because modern prostheses do not require a bony cap.

Knee arthroscopy is one of the most commonly performed orthopedic procedures — both diagnostic and operative. Arthroscopic procedures span meniscectomy (partial, total, arthroscopically assisted repair — inside-out, outside-in, all-inside), cartilage procedures (Steadman microfracture, OATS, ACI, MACI), ACL reconstruction (single-bundle, double-bundle, BPTB, hamstring, quadriceps tendon), PCL reconstruction (single, double bundle, transtibial, and tibial inlay), MPFL reconstruction for patellar instability, removal of loose bodies and chondral fragments, arthroscopic synovectomy (PVNS, rheumatoid arthritis, haemophilic synovitis), assessment and treatment of posteromedial and posterolateral instability, lateral retinacular release for lateral patellar overload, and assisted fixation of tibial plateau fractures. Classical indications for diagnostic arthroscopy include suspicion of patellar or femoral condylar chondral injury, synovial evaluation (with biopsy), unexplained knee symptoms, meniscal injury, and objective assessment of the cruciate ligaments. Anaesthesia is general or regional (spinal/epidural); the joint is distended with saline or Ringer's solution, rarely with gas (carbon dioxide). In acute trauma, copious lavage reduces the infection risk.

## Compartment Syndrome of the Thigh and Knee

Thigh compartment syndrome is uncommon but described, particularly with high-energy crush injuries, vascular injury with reperfusion, prolonged operative positioning, and anticoagulation-related hematomas. Fasciotomy of the thigh uses two longitudinal incisions (lateral and medial) providing release of the anterior compartment (lateral incision), the posterior compartment (lateral or separate posterior incision), and the medial compartment (medial incision).

The principal neurovascular concerns include: Femoral nerve: Descends through the femoral triangle. Vulnerable in retroperitoneal injuries and during anterior approaches. Femoral artery and vein: Pass through the femoral triangle, then through the adductor canal to become the popliteal vessels. Saphenous nerve: Branches from the femoral nerve in the femoral triangle, accompanies the femoral artery through the adductor canal, and emerges from the canal to descend along the medial side of the leg. Provides sensation to the medial leg. Sciatic nerve: Divides into tibial and common peroneal divisions at the apex of the popliteal fossa.

Common peroneal nerve: Continues laterally to wrap around the fibular neck (vulnerable to injury here, [Topic Trauma-31](/en/trauma/31/)). Tibial nerve: Continues distally through the popliteal fossa. Popliteal artery and vein: Pass through the popliteal fossa. Vulnerable in knee dislocations ([Topic Trauma-26](/en/trauma/26/)) and distal femur/proximal tibia fractures.

The infrapatellar fat pad ("Hoffa pad") — a structure now central to anterior knee pain — was first systematically described by Hoffa himself.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

The thigh has three principal compartments (anterior with quadriceps and femoral nerve, medial with adductors and obturator nerve, posterior with hamstrings and sciatic nerve), with the femoral triangle at the proximal end and the popliteal fossa at the distal end as the principal neurovascular landmarks. The femoral shaft is approached for fracture fixation typically through a posterolateral or anterolateral approach between vastus lateralis and rectus femoris; classical Küntscher intramedullary nailing (1939–1940), plate osteosynthesis, and compression-distraction methods (Ilizarov, Wagner, Bliskunov) are the three historical frameworks for diaphyseal fracture fixation and femoral lengthening. The classical Bulgarian school emphasises that splitting the vasti denervates their peripheral portions and produces extension or flexion contracture — the principal argument for the muscle-sparing posterolateral approach.

The knee joint is accessed through the medial parapatellar approach (the workhorse for TKA and major knee procedures), with muscle-preserving variants (midvastus, subvastus) gaining popularity for selected indications. The classical wide approaches (Textor, Pütti, Cogswell–Adam) are precursors of the modern approaches; the Kocher anterolateral and Abbott–Carpenter external approach are reserved for specific posterolateral indications. The lateral parapatellar approach is reserved for severe valgus gonarthrosis at TKA and for lateral patellar instability. The posterior approach (Henderson, Burks–Schaffer, Trickey) is used for posterior tibial plateau fractures, PCL tibial inlay, exploration of the popliteal vessels, and tibial and common peroneal nerve exposure.

Knee arthroscopy, introduced by Watanabe (1959), through the standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals (with additional suprapatellar, posteromedial, posterolateral, and Gillquist transseptal portals), provides access to the entire joint for meniscal, cartilage, ligament, and synovial procedures. The classical open meniscectomy after Smillie/Boychev has been replaced by partial arthroscopic meniscectomy and meniscal repair. ACL reconstruction has a century-long history (Hey-Groves 1917, Macey 1939, Lindemann 1950, Jones 1963, Lemaire 1967, MacIntosh 1972, Kennedy 1979) with priority contributions from the Soviet orthopaedist Landa (1947) and the Bulgarian modification by Shoylev (1982); PCL reconstruction — from Augustine, Hughston (gastrocnemius), and Trickey (tibial inlay). Treatment of patellar instability includes the full spectrum of classical techniques — Krogius, Roux–Goldthwait, Brucknér, Hauser, Insall, Madigan–Stanisavljević, Boychev, and Shoylev — now combined with MPFL reconstruction and Elmslie–Trillat–Fulkerson distal realignment. The Coventry (1965) high tibial osteotomy with the Holevich–Dzherov–Vladimirov Bulgarian modification, the Maquet–Bardot procedure, and the Maquet–Puddu opening wedge remain valid alternatives to unicompartmental arthroplasty in young patients with medial gonarthrosis. Total knee arthroplasty (Walldius 1951, Charnley 1958, Insall–Burstein 1974, Geomedic 1973) and arthrodesis (Charnley, Lucas–Cottrell, Ilizarov) are end-stage options for severe arthritis or joint destruction. The principal neurovascular structures of the popliteal fossa (popliteal vessels, tibial and common peroneal nerves) are the critical considerations in posterior knee and proximal tibia procedures. The common peroneal nerve around the fibular neck is vulnerable to direct injury in lateral approaches and to traction injury in knee dislocations.
