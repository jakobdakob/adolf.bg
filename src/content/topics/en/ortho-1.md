## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The category of "tumor-like lesions of bone" comprises a heterogeneous group of non-neoplastic or pseudo-neoplastic conditions whose radiographic and sometimes clinical appearance mimics that of true bone tumors. Although none of these entities meets the strict pathological definition of a neoplasm — an autonomous, clonal proliferation of cells with unlimited growth potential — they all share the capacity to produce focal bony lesions that pose diagnostic dilemmas, generate pain, swelling, or pathological fracture, and demand a working differential which encompasses every primary and secondary bone tumor. The chapter addresses the entities in the order in which they appear in the classical orthopedic literature of Jaffe, Lichtenstein, Schajowicz, Campanacci, Dahlin and Unni: simple (unicameral) bone cyst; aneurysmal bone cyst; fibrous dysplasia and its polyostotic variants with endocrine and cutaneous manifestations; non-ossifying fibroma and fibrous cortical defect; Langerhans cell histiocytosis with its localized form eosinophilic granuloma; brown tumor of hyperparathyroidism; the sclerotic lesions — bone island, osteopoikilosis, osteopathia striata, and melorheostosis; myositis ossificans and post-traumatic heterotopic ossification; and finally a small number of conditions — Brodie's abscess, intraosseous lipoma, intraosseous ganglion, osteofibrous dysplasia (Campanacci disease) — that are often considered in the same radiographic differential.

Apley and Solomon, summarizing decades of orthopedic oncologic experience, emphasize that an inadequate distinction between a tumor-like lesion and a malignant neoplasm is the most common error in bone-tumor management; an osteosarcoma curetted as a "cyst" is an essentially irretrievable disaster, while a non-ossifying fibroma resected as a sarcoma is an unnecessary mutilation. The principles that follow govern the whole chapter: every focal bone lesion is interpreted in the context of patient age, exact bone, and exact location within the bone; the radiograph is assessed by the standard Lodwick-Enneking checklist — pattern of bone destruction, zone of transition, periosteal reaction, presence of a soft-tissue mass, matrix; definitive histology is obtained before definitive surgery whenever the diagnosis is not unequivocal.

### The Age — Bone — Location Triad
<details id="triad">
<summary>The Age — Bone — Location Triad</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The age of the patient is the single most important determinant in a focal bone lesion. The first decade is dominated by simple bone cysts, eosinophilic granuloma, and Ewing sarcoma. The second decade adds non-ossifying fibroma, osteoblastoma, fibrous dysplasia, aneurysmal bone cyst, chondroblastoma, and chondromyxoid fibroma. The third and fourth decades emphasize giant cell tumor, enchondroma, brown tumor, and osteoblastoma. Lesions in patients over 40 are predominantly metastatic, myeloma, geode, or high-grade chondrosarcoma.

The exact bone narrows the diagnosis further. The proximal humerus and proximal femur in childhood most often harbor a simple bone cyst. The distal femoral or proximal tibial metaphysis — a non-ossifying fibroma. The small tubular bones of the hand — an enchondroma. The posterior elements of the spine in an adolescent — an aneurysmal bone cyst or osteoblastoma. The calcaneus and ilium in the adult — a latent simple bone cyst.

The exact location within the bone supplies the final decisive piece of information. Diaphyseal lesions classically include osteoid osteoma, Ewing sarcoma, adamantinoma, and fibrous dysplasia. Metaphyseal lesions include almost all of the tumor-like lesions of this chapter, plus osteosarcoma. Epiphyseal lesions are essentially restricted to chondroblastoma, giant cell tumor (after physeal closure), and geode. Central location within the transverse section characterizes simple bone cyst and fibrous dysplasia; eccentric location — non-ossifying fibroma, aneurysmal bone cyst, and chondroblastoma.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Three questions resolve almost every focal bone lesion: patient age, exact bone and exact location within it, and the Lodwick-Enneking radiographic checklist (destruction pattern, transition zone, periosteal reaction, soft-tissue mass, matrix).</li>
    <li>First decade — simple bone cyst, eosinophilic granuloma, Ewing sarcoma; second decade adds NOF, fibrous dysplasia, ABC, chondroblastoma, osteoblastoma; over 40 — metastases, myeloma, geode, and high-grade chondrosarcoma.</li>
    <li>Epiphyseal location with an open physis is essentially chondroblastoma; with a closed physis — giant cell tumor. This simple dichotomy resolves half of all epiphyseal differentials.</li>
  </ul>

### Radiographic Analysis after Lodwick and Enneking
<details id="lodwick">
<summary>Radiographic Analysis after Lodwick and Enneking</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Lodwick (1966) proposed a classification of bone destruction in three categories that remains foundational for assessing lesion aggressiveness. **Geographic destruction (type IA, IB, IC)** shows a narrow or sclerotic zone of transition, corresponding to a slowly growing benign lesion — typically simple bone cyst, non-ossifying fibroma, fibrous dysplasia, bone island. **Moth-eaten destruction (type II)** with multiple small lytic foci and a wide zone of transition is characteristic of more aggressive processes — Langerhans cell histiocytosis, myeloma, infection. **Permeative destruction (type III)** with tiny, pinpoint lytic foci and a wide zone of transition is the typical hallmark of high-grade malignancy — Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma, lymphoma.

Enneking (1980) adds the assessment of periosteal reaction and soft-tissue mass as key components of aggressiveness. A discrete, single, dense periosteal reaction ("a band") points to a benign lesion; lamellar or onion-skin reaction to an indolent subacute process; interrupted or spiculated reaction with a Codman's triangle to a rapidly growing aggressive lesion. The presence of a discrete extracortical soft-tissue mass extending beyond the contours of the bone is practically diagnostic for malignancy or for a severely aggressive aneurysmal bone cyst.

Mirels (1989) proposes a scoring system for the assessment of pathological fracture risk in metastatic disease with four variables — location, pain, lesion type, size — but the score is also applied to the risk in large benign lesions in mechanically loaded bones (NOF, cyst, fibrous dysplasia).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Lodwick IA-IC (geographic, narrow zone of transition) — indolent, benign lesion; Lodwick II (moth-eaten) — subacutely aggressive; Lodwick III (permeative) — high-grade malignant.</li>
    <li>Codman's triangle, spiculated or interrupted lamellar periosteal reaction with an extracortical soft-tissue mass — practically diagnostic for a malignant process.</li>
    <li>Mirels score ≥ 9 is the generally accepted indication for prophylactic fixation of a metastatic lytic lesion in a long bone; the principle extends to extensive tumor-like lesions in the distal femur, distal tibia, and proximal femur.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Biopsy Planning
<details id="biopsy">
<summary>Biopsy Planning</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

When the working differential remains uncertain, biopsy is required. The Mankin and Enneking studies (1982, 1996) remain the touchstone: a poorly planned biopsy compromises the eventual oncological outcome in a disturbingly high proportion of cases — up to one in five requires a change in treatment plan, and the rate of unwanted amputation is statistically linked to biopsy performed in an inadequate center. The principle that "the biopsy should be planned with as much care as the definitive surgery" should govern every case in which a malignant lesion is in the differential.

The anatomical rules are constant. The biopsy tract is placed in the line of the future definitive resection so that it can be excised en bloc together with the operative specimen. The tract crosses one anatomical compartment, never several (avoid transarticular trajectories; avoid internervous planes that would spread tumor contamination into adjacent compartments). Hemostasis is mandatory — the hematoma spreads tumor cells far from the biopsy tract. In a percutaneous trocar biopsy the needle follows the pre-planned path and the sample is taken from the periphery of the lesion, where cellularity is highest and central necrosis least likely. The sample includes material for microbiological culture in addition to the histology specimen, because infection (osteomyelitis, Pott's disease, brucellosis, fungal osteomyelitis) is a frequent differential.

For tumor-like lesions with high diagnostic certainty (typical simple bone cyst of the proximal humerus with the fallen-fragment sign; classical NOF of the distal tibial metaphysis in an asymptomatic child) biopsy may not be necessary before definitive treatment — the curettage provides sufficient material for confirmatory histology. For lesions with malignant suspicion (rapidly growing ABC with soft-tissue mass; atypical fibrous dysplasia with matrix disturbance; vertebra plana in an atypical age group) percutaneous or open biopsy precedes definitive surgery.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>A biopsy tract placed transversely to the compartment or through an internervous plane contaminates adjacent compartments and may convert a resective procedure into an amputation — particularly in the distal femur, proximal tibia, and the adductors.</li>
    <li>Curettage of an apparent "cyst" that turns out to be osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma — a catastrophic oversight with largely fatal consequences. At the slightest suspicion — biopsy with intraoperative frozen section before extended curettage.</li>
    <li>An apparent aneurysmal bone cyst requires careful histological examination of multiple areas to exclude telangiectatic osteosarcoma, which can mimic ABC both radiographically and at low-power microscopy.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Simple (Unicameral) Bone Cyst
<details id="sbc">
<summary>Simple (Unicameral) Bone Cyst</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Epidemiology and pathogenesis.** The simple bone cyst (SBC; synonyms: unicameral bone cyst, solitary bone cyst, juvenile bone cyst) is one of the commonest tumor-like conditions of childhood and adolescence. About two-thirds of patients are under 14 years of age; males are affected approximately twice as often as females (2.3 : 1 in Campanacci's series of 987 cases). The vast majority — well over 80% — occur in the proximal humerus (60%) or the proximal femur (25%); the proximal tibia, calcaneus (os calcis), and iliac bone form the next tier. In adults the SBC is encountered almost exclusively in the calcaneus or ilium, where the lesion may remain dormant for decades. Pathogenesis is not fully established. The most enduring theory, proposed by Cohen (1970) and refined over decades, holds that the cyst represents a focal disturbance of venous drainage from the metaphyseal bone — interstitial pressure rises above arterial inflow pressure, the marrow undergoes infarction and serous transformation, and a cavity forms. Elevated intracystic concentrations of prostaglandins, interleukin-1β, and matrix metalloproteinases support an additional inflammatory component in the bone resorption observed at the cyst wall.

**Pathology.** Macroscopically the cyst is a solitary, unilocular cavity lined by a thin fibrous membrane and filled with serous or serosanguineous fluid. Bony septa may project incompletely from the wall, giving the impression on imaging of a multiloculated structure, but pathologically these septa are best understood as cortical ridges rather than true compartmentalization. Histologically the lining membrane is composed of flattened fibroblastic cells supported by a thin layer of collagen; scattered osteoclast-type giant cells, hemosiderin, xanthoma (foam) cells, and occasional calcified material resembling cementum (so-called "cementicles") may be present. There is no atypia, mitotic activity, or pleomorphism. Differentiation from aneurysmal bone cyst, with which it can coexist, depends chiefly on the absence of blood-filled cavernous spaces lined by fibroblast-like cells.

**Clinical features.** The lesion is almost always asymptomatic until a pathological fracture occurs, which is the presenting feature in approximately two-thirds of cases. The fracture is typically through the proximal humeral metadiaphysis in a child of eight to fourteen years, often after a relatively minor athletic trauma. The humeral location can produce a shortening of 1–4 cm. Pain or slight swelling without fracture is occasionally noticed, and rare cases of limb-length discrepancy or premature physeal closure related to extensive cyst involvement are reported.

**Radiographic findings.** The plain radiograph is usually diagnostic. The cyst appears as a centrally placed, well-circumscribed, expansile lytic lesion of the metaphysis, abutting but not normally crossing the physis. The cortex is thinned but a thin rim of intact cortex usually persists, and the lesion respects the contour of the bone rather than producing an extracortical soft-tissue mass. The diameter of the lesion is approximately equal to or less than the diameter of the adjacent physis — a morphology that distinguishes it from aneurysmal bone cyst, which is typically wider than the physis it abuts. As the bone grows, the cyst migrates away from the physis toward the diaphysis, and is classified (after Jaffe) as "active" when in contact with the physis or "latent" when separated from the physis by a margin of normal bone. After a pathological fracture, a small cortical fragment may settle dependently within the fluid-filled cavity — the classic Reynolds (1969) "**fallen-fragment**" or "fallen-leaf" sign, pathognomonic of SBC and the single feature that reliably distinguishes it from solid tumors at presentation. MRI shows a homogeneous fluid-signal lesion (low T1, high T2) without enhancement of the contents and only a thin enhancing rim. Bone scan shows little or no increased uptake, except in the zone of a consolidating fracture.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The fallen-fragment sign (Reynolds) — a small cortical fragment settling dependently in a fluid-filled cavity after pathological fracture — is pathognomonic of simple bone cyst and the single feature that reliably distinguishes it from solid tumors at presentation.</li>
    <li>The cyst is central, with a diameter equal to or less than that of the adjacent physis; aneurysmal bone cyst is eccentric and typically wider than the physis — the simple morphological distinction between the two.</li>
    <li>An "active" cyst (in contact with the physis) has a higher rate of recurrence after curettage; a "latent" cyst (separated from the physis) recurs rarely.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Treatment.** Many simple bone cysts heal spontaneously after pathological fracture; the management of an asymptomatic cyst is generally observation. When intervention is needed, the historical mainstays have evolved over decades. Scaglietti's (1979) percutaneous injection of methylprednisolone acetate (Depo-Medrol) into the cyst, popularized by the Bolognese school, achieves complete healing in roughly half of cases and partial healing in another quarter; typically 2–5 injections at about 2-month intervals are required, and cumulative response can be improved by aspiration of cyst fluid prior to instillation. Cyst decompression by multiple drilling, with or without autologous bone marrow injection and demineralized bone matrix, has been advocated with healing rates approaching 80%.

For lesions at risk of pathological fracture in mechanically critical locations (particularly the proximal femur) curettage and bone grafting, with or without internal fixation by an elastic intramedullary nail or a paediatric hip plate, is the most reliable method of achieving definitive healing. Pathological fractures of the proximal femur almost always require fixation and stabilization because of the high risks of avascular necrosis, coxa vara, and shortening if conservative management fails. Spontaneous resolution after uncomplicated fracture through the lesion in the proximal humerus has been reported in many series and, in the absence of further symptoms, justifies sling immobilization for these less mechanically critical fractures.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>A pathological fracture through a cyst in the proximal femur is not managed conservatively — the risk of avascular necrosis, coxa vara, and permanent shortening mandates curettage, bone grafting, and intramedullary fixation (paediatric hip nail or elastic Métaizeau nails).</li>
    <li>Active cysts close to the physis in young children — elevated recurrence rate; repeat injections or combined technique with drilling and autologous bone marrow.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Aneurysmal Bone Cyst
<details id="abc">
<summary>Aneurysmal Bone Cyst</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Epidemiology and pathogenesis.** The aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC), described as a distinct entity by Jaffe and Lichtenstein (1942), is a benign but locally aggressive osteolytic lesion characterized by blood-filled cavernous spaces. Three quarters of cases occur in the first two decades of life, with an even or slightly female-predominant distribution. Long-bone metaphyses of the femur, tibia and humerus account for the majority of cases, but ABCs may arise in any bone and have a particular predilection for the posterior elements of the spine (about 10% of cases) and the pelvic flat bones. Historically debated between "primary" and "secondary" categories, the modern molecular understanding distinguishes the two: **primary ABC** harbors a characteristic translocation involving the USP6 gene on chromosome 17p13, demonstrating its clonality and supporting its classification as a true neoplasm despite the long-standing inclusion in tumor-like lesions; **secondary ABC** arises within another pre-existing lesion — most commonly giant cell tumor of bone, chondroblastoma, osteoblastoma, fibrous dysplasia, or osteosarcoma — and represents a reactive vascular cystic change without the USP6 rearrangement. The clinical importance of this distinction is that an apparent ABC must be examined histologically with care to exclude an underlying neoplasm, particularly telangiectatic osteosarcoma, which can mimic ABC radiographically and histologically.

**Pathology.** The lesion consists of cavernous, blood-filled spaces separated by fibrous septa containing fibroblasts, multinucleated osteoclast-type giant cells, scattered foci of woven bone (often with a basophilic mineralization pattern described as "blue bone"), and hemosiderin. The cavernous spaces **lack endothelial lining** — a critical feature, since true vascular lesions do have endothelium and the absence of endothelium distinguishes ABC histologically from a hemangioma. In a small but important fraction of cases, areas of solid ABC predominate over cystic areas, producing the so-called "solid aneurysmal bone cyst," which can be radiographically confused with malignant tumor and histologically with giant cell reparative granuloma.

**Clinical features and radiographic findings.** Patients typically present with pain and progressive swelling at the affected site, often of several months' duration; spinal lesions can produce nerve-root or cord compression. Radiographically the ABC appears as an eccentric, expansile, osteolytic lesion that elevates and progressively erodes the cortex, often with a characteristic "egg-shell" rim of periosteal new bone. The transverse diameter of the lesion typically exceeds the diameter of the adjacent physis (a feature distinguishing ABC from simple bone cyst) and the lesion may break through the cortex into the surrounding soft tissues. MRI demonstrates the cavernous spaces as multiple fluid-filled compartments with characteristic **fluid-fluid levels**, representing the layering of cellular debris and blood products at different stages of degradation; these fluid-fluid levels, although not entirely pathognomonic, are highly suggestive of ABC and are best seen on T2-weighted images. Contrast-enhanced sequences show enhancement of the septa but not the cyst contents. Campanacci's staging of ABC evolution comprises an early osteolytic phase, an active expansile phase, a stabilizing "egg-shell" phase, and a late reparative phase with progressive ossification — spontaneous healing after a simple biopsy incision has been documented in several series.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Primary ABC carries a USP6 translocation (17p13) — a true neoplasm despite the "tumor-like" label. Secondary ABC arises within another lesion (GCT, chondroblastoma, osteoblastoma, fibrous dysplasia, osteosarcoma) without USP6.</li>
    <li>Always examine an apparent ABC histologically to exclude telangiectatic osteosarcoma — the presence of high-grade malignant cells and extensive necrosis are the distinguishing features.</li>
    <li>Fluid-fluid levels on T2 MRI strongly suggest ABC but also occur in telangiectatic osteosarcoma, giant cell tumor with secondary cystic change, and simple bone cyst after fracture — the radiographic sign does not waive the need for histology.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Treatment.** The treatment of ABC must accomplish three goals: confirmation of the diagnosis (with particular attention to excluding telangiectatic osteosarcoma or an underlying neoplasm), eradication of the lesion with an acceptable rate of local recurrence, and preservation of function. Curettage of the lesion, with adjunct measures such as high-speed burring of the cyst wall, phenol cauterization, argon-beam coagulation, or local cryotherapy, followed by bone grafting or polymethylmethacrylate cement, is the standard surgical treatment. Local recurrence after intralesional surgery may approach 20%, and is more common in young children with metaphyseal lesions close to the physis. Selective arterial embolization, either as a definitive treatment for surgically inaccessible spinal or pelvic lesions or as a preoperative adjunct to reduce intraoperative blood loss, has gained importance over recent decades. Sclerotherapy, particularly with intralesional polidocanol or doxycycline foam, has been reported with encouraging results in several pediatric centers. Radiotherapy was historically effective at stimulating cyst calcification but is now reserved for the rare unresectable lesion because of the risk of secondary sarcoma (particularly in the long-lived pediatric population) and growth-plate damage. Spinal aneurysmal cysts may require posterior decompression and stabilization in addition to lesion treatment — particularly in nerve-root or cord compression, or when more than half of the vertebral body, a facet joint or pedicle is involved.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>An apparent ABC with rapid growth, a soft-tissue mass beyond the "egg-shell" rim, or atypical matrix — suspicion of telangiectatic osteosarcoma. Open biopsy with intraoperative frozen section before curettage.</li>
    <li>Radiotherapy of ABC in a child — long-term risk of induced sarcoma; reserved for unresectable, non-embolizable lesions when no alternative is available.</li>
    <li>Curettage without adequate intraoperative wall treatment (burring, phenol, cement) — recurrence may exceed 30%, particularly when the lesion abuts the physis.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Fibrous Dysplasia and the McCune-Albright and Mazabraud Syndromes
<details id="fd">
<summary>Fibrous Dysplasia and the McCune-Albright and Mazabraud Syndromes</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-1/fibrous-dysplasia.png" data-figure-alt="Fibrous dysplasia, ground-glass radiographic appearance" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/ortho-1/fibrous-dysplasia.png" alt="Fibrous dysplasia, ground-glass radiographic appearance" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Fibrous dysplasia: the characteristic "ground glass" radiographic appearance of an intramedullary lesion with mild expansion of the bone.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Basmajoor, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:R%C3%B6ntgenfoto_Fibreuze_Dysplasie.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Definition, genetics, and classification.** Fibrous dysplasia, described as a distinct entity by Lichtenstein (1938) and Lichtenstein-Jaffe (1942), is a benign medullary fibro-osseous lesion in which normal bone is replaced by abnormal fibrous tissue containing scattered, poorly mineralized, immature woven bone trabeculae. Since the 1990s the lesion has been understood as a developmental disorder caused by a post-zygotic activating mutation in the GNAS1 gene, which encodes the α-subunit of the stimulatory G-protein coupling membrane receptors to adenylyl cyclase. The somatic mosaicism produced by this mutation explains the highly variable phenotype, ranging from a single asymptomatic monostotic lesion to widespread polyostotic disease with severe skeletal deformity.

Three principal clinical phenotypes are recognized. **Monostotic fibrous dysplasia** is by far the commonest form, accounting for about 80% of cases. **Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia** involves multiple bones, usually distributed along one side of the body (monomelic or segmental asymmetry). **McCune-Albright syndrome** is the classic triad of polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait skin pigmentation (with characteristically irregular "**coast-of-Maine**" borders, in contrast to the smooth "coast-of-California" borders of neurofibromatosis), and endocrine hyperfunction — most commonly precocious puberty, but also hyperthyroidism, growth-hormone-secreting pituitary adenomas, Cushing's syndrome, and adrenal hyperplasia. **Mazabraud syndrome** describes the association of polyostotic fibrous dysplasia with intramuscular myxomas — a rare but well-documented entity.

**Pathology.** The dysplastic medulla shows a characteristic background of bland, whorled fibrous stroma supporting irregular, curvilinear trabeculae of woven bone that have been likened to "alphabet soup" or "Chinese characters" in their tortuous outline. Critically, these trabeculae **lack an osteoblastic rimming** — unlike the trabeculae of an ossifying fibroma, which they superficially resemble. The interface between dysplastic bone and adjacent normal bone is often well demarcated but may permeate the host trabecular bone. In approximately 1% of monostotic and up to 4% of polyostotic cases, malignant transformation occurs, typically to osteosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, or malignant fibrous histiocytoma; the risk is highest in irradiated lesions, which is one of several reasons radiotherapy is contraindicated.

**Clinical features and radiographic findings.** Monostotic disease is often discovered incidentally on imaging performed for unrelated reasons. When symptomatic, presentation is with pain, deformity, or pathological fracture. The femur is the most frequently involved long bone, and the characteristic "**shepherd's-crook**" varus deformity of the proximal femur — caused by repeated stress fractures with secondary plastic deformation through the weakened bone — is a clinical hallmark of severe polyostotic disease. The craniofacial skeleton (with a characteristic asymmetric leonine appearance in heavy monomelic involvement), ribs, tibia, and pelvis are also commonly involved. In the spine, fibrous dysplasia can produce vertebral deformity, scoliosis, and rarely nerve-root compression. Radiographically the lesion is intramedullary, well demarcated, with a characteristic "**ground-glass**" or "smoky" matrix density that reflects the mineralization of the woven trabeculae; expansion of the bone, cortical thinning, and a sclerotic rim are typical. Long-standing lesions may contain cystic or cartilaginous areas. The bone scan is hot, which is useful in mapping the extent of polyostotic disease.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The GNAS1 post-zygotic activating mutation produces the spectrum: monostotic (~80%) → polyostotic → McCune-Albright (polyostotic + café-au-lait with "coast-of-Maine" borders + endocrine hyperfunction, classically precocious puberty).</li>
    <li>Café-au-lait in McCune-Albright — irregular "coast-of-Maine" borders, often along a monomelic side; in neurofibromatosis — smooth "coast-of-California" borders. The morphology of the border is a classic exam point.</li>
    <li>Radiotherapy is contraindicated in fibrous dysplasia — the risk of induced malignant transformation (osteosarcoma, malignant fibrous histiocytoma, fibrosarcoma) outweighs any therapeutic benefit.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Treatment.** Asymptomatic and small lesions are observed. Symptomatic monostotic lesions can be managed with curettage and bone grafting, although the propensity of the dysplastic process to incorporate or replace any bone graft means that allograft — particularly cortical structural graft — is preferred over autograft, which is converted back to dysplastic bone over time. Internal fixation with intramedullary devices is generally preferred over plate fixation for diaphyseal lesions in long bones, both because the diseased cortex is poor for screw purchase and because the entire length of the bone may be affected by lesional or dysplastic tissue. The shepherd's-crook deformity of the proximal femur is one of the classic orthopedic indications for valgus subtrochanteric osteotomy and intramedullary fixation. Bisphosphonates, particularly intravenous pamidronate or zoledronate, reduce bone pain and biochemical markers of bone turnover in polyostotic disease and McCune-Albright syndrome, although their effect on the radiographic appearance of the lesion is modest. Endocrine manifestations of McCune-Albright syndrome require dedicated medical management in cooperation with a paediatric endocrinologist.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Iliac autograft into a curetted dysplastic cavity is over years converted back into dysplastic tissue — structural cortical allograft is preferred.</li>
    <li>Plate fixation in a diaphyseal dysplastic bone — poor screw purchase, high risk of pull-out; intramedullary fixation is the standard.</li>
    <li>Radiotherapy → sarcomatous transformation in the irradiated dysplastic bone — absolute contraindication.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Non-ossifying Fibroma and Fibrous Cortical Defect
<details id="nof">
<summary>Non-ossifying Fibroma and Fibrous Cortical Defect</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-1/nof.jpg" data-figure-alt="Non-ossifying fibroma of the distal tibia" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/ortho-1/nof.jpg" alt="Non-ossifying fibroma of the distal tibia" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Non-ossifying fibroma: eccentric metaphyseal lytic lesion with a thin sclerotic margin.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Hellerhoff, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:NOF_1.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Concept and nomenclature.** The terms "fibrous cortical defect" (FCD) and "non-ossifying fibroma" (NOF) refer to the same histopathological entity at different sizes and stages: the small, asymptomatic, cortical-based lesion of a growing child is termed a fibrous cortical defect, and as it enlarges and migrates into the medullary cavity it acquires the designation non-ossifying fibroma. Jaffe and Lichtenstein (1942) gave the modern naming, later changed by Jaffe (1958) from "non-osteogenic" to "non-ossifying" fibroma to emphasize the absence of bone formation in the lesion. The lesion is best understood as a developmental anomaly rather than a true neoplasm — a focus of fibrous tissue that persists within the bone before eventually ossifying as the child approaches skeletal maturity. As Apley puts it, the lesion is "asymptomatic and almost always encountered in children as an incidental X-ray finding." Estimates suggest that as many as 30% of normal children have a fibrous cortical defect at some point in their growth, making this by some margin the commonest benign bony lesion of childhood.

**Pathology, clinical features, and imaging.** Histologically the lesion is composed of bland fibrous tissue with scattered osteoclast-type giant cells, foam cells, and hemosiderin-laden macrophages — a pattern that has historically led to the term "fibroxanthoma" being applied. The tissue is arranged in a whorled ("storiform") pattern. There is no atypia. The classical site is the metaphysis of a long bone, particularly the distal femur, distal or proximal tibia, and proximal humerus. The lesion is eccentric, abutting or based on the cortex, and on plain radiographs appears as a well-circumscribed, oval, multiloculated lytic area with a thin sclerotic margin and a long axis parallel to the long axis of the bone. Larger NOFs may attain a "soap-bubble" appearance. As skeletal maturity approaches, the lesion spontaneously ossifies — initially at the periphery, then concentrically — and eventually disappears or leaves behind a residual sclerotic focus.

**Jaffe-Campanacci syndrome.** The association of multiple non-ossifying fibromas with café-au-lait spots, mental retardation, hypogonadism, cardiovascular and ocular anomalies constitutes the rare Jaffe-Campanacci syndrome (Campanacci et al. 1983; Mirra et al. 1982). Its inclusion in the differential of polyostotic café-au-lait associations (alongside neurofibromatosis and McCune-Albright syndrome) is a classic exam point. Distinction from neurofibromatosis (type I, von Recklinghausen disease) rests on the absence of typical neurofibromatous cutaneous and neurological manifestations; from McCune-Albright — on the type of skeletal lesions (NOF vs fibrous dysplasia) and the absence of typical endocrine involvement.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>FCD and NOF are the same histological entity at different sizes: small cortical-based → FCD; large intramedullary → NOF. Up to 30% of children have an FCD at some point.</li>
    <li>Multiple NOFs + café-au-lait + mental retardation + hypogonadism + cardiovascular/ocular anomalies = Jaffe-Campanacci syndrome. An exam-classic distinction from McCune-Albright (fibrous dysplasia, not NOF) and neurofibromatosis (no NOF-type skeletal foci).</li>
    <li>The natural history is spontaneous ossification as skeletal maturity approaches — the great majority of NOFs require no treatment whatever.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Treatment.** The natural history of the NOF is one of spontaneous resolution as the child matures, and the great majority require no treatment. Indications for intervention are limited to large lesions in mechanically loaded bones — typically those exceeding 50% of the bone diameter, or 33 mm in absolute size, especially in the distal femur or distal tibia — where the risk of pathological fracture justifies prophylactic curettage and bone grafting. Internal fixation is rarely required. Symptomatic pathological fracture through a NOF usually unites with closed treatment; secondary curettage of the lesion may be performed if the fracture does not unite or if the lesion remains mechanically significant after union.

</div>
</details>


### Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis and Eosinophilic Granuloma
<details id="lch">
<summary>Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis and Eosinophilic Granuloma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Spectrum of disease.** Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH), formerly named histiocytosis X (a term coined by Lichtenstein in 1953 to unify eosinophilic granuloma, Hand-Schüller-Christian disease, and Letterer-Siwe disease as a single nosological category), comprises a spectrum of disorders characterized by clonal proliferation of bone-marrow-derived Langerhans cells. The spectrum extends from the **localized monostotic bone lesion (eosinophilic granuloma)** at one pole, through **multifocal disease** without systemic involvement and the **multisystem Hand-Schüller-Christian disease** (with the classical triad of diabetes insipidus, exophthalmos, and lytic skull lesions), to the **acute disseminated Letterer-Siwe disease** of infancy with high mortality. Although strictly classified as a clonal disorder, the bone manifestations are frequently included among the tumor-like lesions because their imaging features, behavior in single-system disease, and treatment principles share much with the conditions discussed in this chapter.

**Pathology, clinical features, and imaging.** The pathognomonic Langerhans cell, identified by S-100 and CD1a positivity and by the characteristic cytoplasmic **Birbeck granules** on electron microscopy, is admixed with an inflammatory infiltrate dominated by eosinophils — the source of the historical name "eosinophilic granuloma." Children under 10 years of age account for the majority of cases of single-system bone disease. Bone pain, tenderness, and a soft-tissue mass are usual; spinal involvement, particularly of a thoracic vertebra, can produce the classical **vertebra plana** — symmetrical collapse of the vertebral body to a wafer-thin disc of dense bone, described by Calvé in 1925 as a separate entity before its association with histiocytosis X. Other characteristic sites include the skull (lytic lesions with sharp, beveled edges — the "**geographic skull**" with map-of-an-island appearance), proximal femur, ribs, and mandible. On plain radiographs the lesion is a "**punched-out**," geographic lytic area, sometimes with a sclerotic margin and adjacent periosteal reaction that can be confused with osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma — both critical differentials in a child with an aggressive bone lesion.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Vertebra plana — symmetrical vertebral collapse to a thin dense disc — is the classic LCH manifestation in the thoracic spine of a child. Substantial restoration of height with growth is the rule in the absence of neurological deficit — spinal fusion is rarely required.</li>
    <li>S-100 + CD1a + Birbeck granules (electron microscopy) — confirm the Langerhans cell; the eosinophilic infiltrate is reactive and not diagnostically specific.</li>
    <li>A bone lesion with a "punched-out" appearance, periosteal reaction, and a soft-tissue mass in a child under 10 — mandatorily consider in the differential both LCH and Ewing sarcoma and osteomyelitis before proceeding to extended curettage.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Treatment.** The natural history of single-system bony LCH is spontaneous resolution over months to years, and the majority of solitary lesions require nothing more than confirmation by biopsy or fine-needle aspiration and observation. Symptomatic lesions can be treated by curettage, **intralesional corticosteroid injection** (methylprednisolone 40–80 mg, described by Capanna et al.), low-dose radiotherapy (dose capped at 6–10 Gy to avoid late complications), or systemic chemotherapy in multifocal or systemic disease. Vertebra plana usually reconstitutes a substantial proportion of normal vertebral height with growth, and spinal fusion is rarely required in the absence of neurological deficit. Hand-Schüller-Christian disease with diffuse involvement is managed with prednisolone, vinblastine, and systemic chemotherapy per the LCH-I/II/III protocols of the International Histiocyte Society. Letterer-Siwe disease requires intensive multidisciplinary onco-hematologic care.

</div>
</details>


### Brown Tumor of Hyperparathyroidism
<details id="brown-tumor">
<summary>Brown Tumor of Hyperparathyroidism</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-1/brown-tumor-hands.jpg" data-figure-alt="Brown tumours of the hands in hyperparathyroidism" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/ortho-1/brown-tumor-hands.jpg" alt="Brown tumours of the hands in hyperparathyroidism" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Multiple brown tumours of the hands in hyperparathyroidism: lytic foci of osteoclastic resorption.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Frank Gaillard, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Brown_tumours_of_the_hands.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The brown tumor (so named because of its macroscopic hemosiderin-tinged color) is a focal lytic lesion of bone occurring in severe hyperparathyroidism. Although now uncommon in regions with routine biochemical screening, brown tumors remain a significant differential in patients with end-stage renal disease, in untreated primary hyperparathyroidism (parathyroid adenoma, parathyroid hyperplasia, rarely carcinoma), and in patients from settings where parathyroid disease is not detected until advanced.

Pathophysiologically the lesion is the localized expression of the diffuse process of **osteitis fibrosa cystica (of von Recklinghausen)**: persistent parathyroid hormone elevation drives osteoclastic bone resorption, with replacement of marrow spaces by vascular fibrous tissue, hemorrhage, and reactive giant cells. Apley summarizes the appearance: "Haemorrhage and giant-cell reaction within the fibrous stroma may give rise to brownish, tumour-like masses, whose liquefaction leads to fluid-filled cysts." Radiographically the lesion is an expansile, lytic area, often multiple and often associated with the characteristic features of hyperparathyroidism — **subperiosteal resorption of the radial aspects of the middle phalanges** (a pathognomonic sign of early hyperparathyroidism), terminal phalangeal tuft resorption, a "**salt-and-pepper**" granular skull, and erosion of the distal clavicle. Histologically the brown tumor is on its own indistinguishable from a giant cell tumor of bone or a giant cell reparative granuloma — the diagnosis is suggested only by the biochemical context: elevated serum calcium, elevated parathyroid hormone, and a relative or absolute hypophosphatemia. In renal osteodystrophy, elevated alkaline phosphatase and the typical profile of tertiary hyperparathyroidism are added.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The biochemical profile makes the diagnosis of brown tumor — histologically it is indistinguishable from GCT. Always check serum Ca, P, PTH, and alkaline phosphatase before declaring a "giant cell tumor" in an adult patient with multiple lytic lesions.</li>
    <li>Subperiosteal resorption of the radial margins of the middle phalanges is a pathognomonic sign of hyperparathyroidism — imaging of the hands is a reliable screen in a suspect case.</li>
    <li>Brown tumors regress after correction of the underlying hyperparathyroidism; local treatment is reserved for patients with pathological fracture or progressive local symptoms after metabolic stabilization.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Treatment is predominantly that of the underlying hyperparathyroidism — parathyroidectomy in primary disease, medical treatment (calcium-based phosphate binders, active vitamin D analogues, calcimimetics) and surgery for secondary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism in the patient with end-stage renal disease. Most brown tumors regress after metabolic stabilization. Local treatment (curettage or internal fixation for pathological fracture) is rarely required and is undertaken only after metabolic stabilization, because untreated hyperparathyroidism continues to produce new lesions and compromises healing.

</div>
</details>


### Sclerotic Tumor-like Lesions
<details id="sclerotic">
<summary>Sclerotic Tumor-like Lesions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-1/osteopoikilosis.jpg" data-figure-alt="Osteopoikilosis of the hip" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/ortho-1/osteopoikilosis.jpg" alt="Osteopoikilosis of the hip" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Osteopoikilosis: multiple small sclerotic foci symmetrically distributed throughout the cancellous bone.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Hellerhoff, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Osteopoikilose_HG_ap_und_seitlich.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Bone island (enostosis).** The bone island is a focus of compact, mature, lamellar bone within the cancellous bone of an otherwise normal medullary cavity. It is asymptomatic, an incidental finding on radiographs, and typically presents as a small, dense, round to oval focus of compact bone in the pelvis, proximal femur, ribs, or vertebrae. Characteristic is a "brush-like" or "thorny" periphery — strands of dense bone radiating from the center into the adjacent cancellous bone. On bone scan the lesion is usually cold or normal — a useful feature in distinguishing it from a sclerotic metastasis (from prostate, breast, or lung carcinoma), which is typically hot. Bone islands greater than 2 cm are termed "**giant bone islands**" and may require radiological follow-up to confirm stability, but malignant transformation is not described and they are best regarded as a normal anatomical variant.

**Osteopoikilosis (spotted bone disease).** Osteopoikilosis is an inherited autosomal-dominant condition in which multiple small foci of compact lamellar bone (essentially, multiple miniature bone islands) are distributed symmetrically through the cancellous bone of the long-bone metaphyses, the carpus, tarsus, pelvis, and proximal phalanges of the hands and feet. The condition is asymptomatic, of no clinical consequence, and is identified by its characteristic distribution: small (2–10 mm) round or oval foci of dense bone clustered around the joints. The chief clinical importance is the avoidance of mistaking the appearance for blastic metastases, which it can mimic — although the symmetrical, juxta-articular distribution, the small size, and the negative bone scan are reassuring. Association with collagenous skin nodules (the **Buschke-Ollendorff syndrome**) is well described.

**Osteopathia striata.** Osteopathia striata, also termed Voorhoeve's disease, is characterized by linear vertical striations of dense bone radiating from the physis along the long axis of the long bones, particularly in the metaphyses of the distal femur and proximal tibia. It is usually asymptomatic and incidental, autosomal-dominant inheritance has been described, and the condition is sometimes seen in combination with osteopoikilosis or with cranial sclerosis. Distinction from melorheostosis is by the radiographic morphology: striae rather than the candle-wax appearance of melorheostosis.

**Melorheostosis.** Melorheostosis (Léri, 1922) is a sporadic, often unilateral developmental disorder of bone in which hyperostotic, irregular new bone is deposited along the cortex of long bones in a pattern reminiscent of melting wax dripping down a candle ("**flowing candle wax**" / "melted-wax" / "candle wax"). The condition typically follows a sclerotomal distribution, supporting a hypothesis of segmental developmental error. Pain, stiffness, and limited joint motion are common; soft-tissue contractures and limb-length discrepancy may develop in childhood. Histologically the bone is dense, lamellar, and abnormally organized; the periosteum and adjacent soft tissues are often involved. Treatment is symptomatic: physiotherapy, analgesia, sympathetic blocks, and occasionally surgical release of contracted soft tissues. Bisphosphonates have been tried with variable response. Osteotomies for limb-length discrepancy are sometimes required.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Bone island — single focus of dense bone, "cold" on bone scan; sclerotic metastasis — single or multiple foci, "hot" bone scan. The bone scan is the decisive screen in an adult patient with suspected secondary bone disease.</li>
    <li>Osteopoikilosis — multiple small symmetrical foci around the joints in an asymptomatic patient; Buschke-Ollendorff syndrome when associated with collagenous skin nodules.</li>
    <li>Melorheostosis — unilateral sclerotomal involvement of a long bone with a "flowing candle wax" appearance; a rigid, painful and often contracted limb in childhood and adolescence.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Myositis Ossificans and Heterotopic Ossification
<details id="myositis">
<summary>Myositis Ossificans and Heterotopic Ossification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Myositis ossificans, more accurately described as post-traumatic heterotopic ossification, is the formation of mature lamellar bone within skeletal muscle and soft tissue following a usually clearly identifiable injury — a direct contusion (the "cavalryman's thigh" of classical military terminology), an athletic blunt-force injury, or a tear with a deep hematoma. The condition is included among the tumor-like lesions because both clinically and radiographically it can mimic a soft-tissue sarcoma, particularly **extraosseous osteosarcoma**.

The early phases of the process are characterized by a painful, tender, swollen mass without radiographic abnormality. By two to four weeks, ill-defined flocculent calcification begins to appear within the lesion, and over the subsequent two to four months the lesion matures from the periphery inward, ultimately forming a well-circumscribed mass of mature lamellar bone separated from the underlying skeleton. Characteristic is the radiographic feature of "**peripheral mineralization**" with a more lucent center. This pattern of maturation from the periphery inward, sometimes called the **"zone phenomenon" of Ackerman**, is the diagnostic mirror image of the extraosseous osteosarcoma, where mineralization is densest centrally.

Histologically the same zonal phenomenon is found: mature trabecular bone at the periphery, immature osteoid in the middle, and a central zone of fibroblastic proliferation that can be highly cellular and atypical-appearing in the early phase — a critical pitfall, as a biopsy taken too early from this central zone can be misdiagnosed as sarcoma.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Myositis ossificans matures from the PERIPHERY inward (Ackerman zone phenomenon) — the diagnostic mirror image of extraosseous osteosarcoma (densest centrally). A biopsy taken too early from the cellular central zone can be misdiagnosed as sarcoma.</li>
    <li>Wait for radiographic maturity (typically 4–6 months) before biopsy or excision — serum alkaline phosphatase and bone scan as biomarkers of metabolic activity.</li>
    <li>Excision in the active phase → near-certain recurrence. Prophylactic radiotherapy (700–800 cGy within the first 24–48 hours of excision) and/or indomethacin are standard adjuncts.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Treatment is symptomatic in the early phase: ice, compression, gentle range-of-motion exercise, and avoidance of further trauma. Once mature, the heterotopic bone can be excised if it produces persistent pain or restricts joint motion; excision is best deferred for at least 6 months, ideally until the lesion is metabolically inactive on bone scan and serum alkaline phosphatase has normalized, to minimize the risk of recurrence. Single-dose perioperative radiotherapy (700–800 cGy within 24–48 hours of surgery) and oral indomethacin (75 mg daily for 6 weeks) are the two principal adjuvants used to prevent recurrence after excision and to prevent heterotopic ossification after high-risk operations such as acetabular fracture fixation and total hip arthroplasty. The Brooker (1973) classification of heterotopic ossification after total hip arthroplasty remains the standard for describing severity — class IV (bony ankylosis between the femoral and pelvic sides) is almost an automatic indication for revision excision with prophylaxis.

</div>
</details>


### Additional Lesions in the Differential
<details id="additional">
<summary>Additional Lesions in the Differential</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

A small number of additional lesions deserve mention because they are often considered alongside the conditions above when interpreting a focal bone lesion.

**Brodie's abscess.** A subacute intraosseous focus of indolent staphylococcal infection, typically affecting the tibial metaphysis in children. Presents as a well-circumscribed lytic lesion surrounded by a halo of sclerotic bone, sometimes with penetration across the physeal plate. The "double-line" sign on T2 MRI (inner ring of higher signal — granulation tissue, surrounded by an outer ring of lower signal — reactive sclerotic bone) and elevated inflammatory markers usually distinguish it from a tumor-like lesion, but the clinical and imaging overlap is enough that infection should always be considered. Curettage with drainage and targeted antibiotic therapy (after culture identification of the organism) is the standard treatment.

**Intraosseous lipoma.** A rare benign fatty lesion most commonly of the calcaneus (classically located in Ward's neutral triangle) and the intertrochanteric region of the femur — can mimic a simple bone cyst on plain films; MRI is diagnostic because of the unique fat signal (high T1, dropping on fat-saturation). The Milgram (1988) classification describes three stages: solid fat (I), partial necrosis (II), and complete necrosis with calcifications (III) — stage III can surgically resemble an intraosseous abscess.

**Intraosseous ganglion.** A benign cystic lesion typically subchondral in location at major joints (femoral head, tibial plateau, talus), often associated with degenerative joint disease. Radiographically appears as a small subchondral cyst that can be mistaken for a geode or for early avascular necrosis. Drainage and curettage in symptomatic cases.

**Osteofibrous dysplasia (Campanacci disease).** A fibro-osseous lesion of the anterior tibial cortex in infants and young children, histologically closely related to fibrous dysplasia, but differing in its restricted location, its rim of osteoblasts, and its association with **adamantinoma** — for which it has been proposed as a precursor in a small subset of cases. It develops along the anterior cortex of the tibia, sometimes also of the fibula, and produces a characteristic anterolateral "bowing" of the tibia in a child. Treatment is conservative; biopsy with intraoperative frozen section is performed when adamantinoma is suspected (presentation in later childhood, aggressive behavior, progressive deformity).

**Giant cell reparative granuloma.** A reactive lesion of the small tubular bones of the hands and feet, described by Jaffe (1953). Histologically it resembles giant cell tumor and brown tumor, but it is essentially a hemorrhagic-reparative process — responds well to curettage and bone grafting. May be recognized in the jaws and skull ("central" giant cell granuloma).

**Intraosseous epidermoid cyst.** A rare lesion, located almost exclusively in the distal phalanx of a hand (especially in workers exposed to repetitive trauma) or in the skull. Presents as a sharply circumscribed lytic lesion with a thin sclerotic margin. Curettage is the definitive treatment.

**Massive idiopathic osteolysis (Gorham-Stout disease, "vanishing bone disease").** An extremely rare condition in which progressive, painless osteolysis resorbs parts of or whole bones over months to years, usually replaced by lymphangiomatous tissue. Predominantly affects the shoulder, mandible, pelvis, and spine; described by Gorham and Stout in 1955. Treatment — radiotherapy, bisphosphonates, interferon-α — is unsatisfactory, and the prognosis depends on the location of involvement.

## Complications and Outcomes
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Complications and Outcomes</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Complications in tumor-like lesions of bone arise from three sources: the natural history of the lesion itself, diagnostic oversights, and iatrogenic complications of treatment.

**Natural history.** Pathological fracture is the commonest initial presentation of simple bone cyst (two-thirds of cases), of fibrous dysplasia in a long bone, and of large non-ossifying fibromas. Fractures in the distal femur or distal tibia through a large NOF usually unite well with conservative treatment; fractures in the proximal femur through a simple bone cyst or fibrous dysplasia mandate stabilization because of the risks of avascular necrosis, coxa vara, and permanent shortening. Deformities — varus "shepherd's-crook" in fibrous dysplasia, anterolateral "bowing" of the tibia in osteofibrous dysplasia, limb-length discrepancy in melorheostosis — require specific correction with osteotomies and intramedullary fixation. Neurological compression from a vertebral lesion (ABC, LCH-vertebra plana, brown tumor in the posterior elements) requires decompression with or without stabilization. Malignant transformation — rare but real — is documented in 1% of monostotic and up to 4% of polyostotic cases of fibrous dysplasia (particularly in irradiated lesions), in irradiated ABCs (secondary sarcoma), and in a small percentage of LCH cases with transformation into a malignant histiocytoid neoplasm.

**Diagnostic oversights.** The single most serious oversight is undiagnosed malignancy — telangiectatic osteosarcoma misclassified as ABC; extraosseous osteosarcoma biopsied from the center of a myositis ossificans; giant cell tumor curetted in an undiagnosed parathyroid adenoma; LCH lesion with suspected Ewing sarcoma or osteomyelitis. A well-planned biopsy with intraoperative frozen section and including material for culture is the safeguard against these oversights.

**Iatrogenic complications.** Local recurrence after intralesional surgery (particularly ABC and fibrous dysplasia) is due to incomplete removal of lesional tissue; high-speed burring of the wall, phenol cauterization, or cement are standard adjuncts. Physeal damage during curettage close to the growth plate in a small child leads to progressive deformity and limb-length discrepancy. Radiotherapy in a child with a tumor-like lesion — long-term risk of induced sarcoma, particularly in the long-lived pediatric population; contraindicated in the volume of fibrous dysplasia. Percutaneous biopsy with a poorly chosen tract may force subsequent extended resection or amputation if the underlying lesion turns out to be malignant. Postoperative infection after curettage and bone grafting — rare but catastrophic when an allograft is implanted.

</div>
</details>

**T. P. Vinogradova <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** The classical monograph *Tumors of Bone* (Opukholi kostey, Moscow, 1973) — pathological-anatomic presentation of bone tumors and tumor-like lesions, on which generations of Bulgarian orthopaedists and pathologists have relied. The systematic descriptions of simple bone cyst, aneurysmal bone cyst, fibrous dysplasia, and giant cell tumor remain normative in the Central European curriculum.

**M. V. Volkov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** *Bone Pathology in Childhood* (Kostnaya patologiya detskogo vozrasta, Moscow) — a foundational work in pediatric bone pathology; extended discussion of simple bone cyst, fibrous dysplasia, and non-ossifying fibroma in children, emphasizing a conservative approach (observation, immobilization, corticosteroid injection per Scaglietti) for asymptomatic and spontaneously healing lesions. The surgical principles for pathological fracture through a cyst in the proximal femur remain leading in Bulgarian paediatric practice.

**A. A. Korzh <sup class="natflag">UA</sup>.** The Kharkiv school of Ukrainian orthopaedics, with contributions to the systematic treatment of large aneurysmal bone cysts of the spine and pelvis through a combination of embolization, curettage, and instrumented stabilization; the modified instrumented constructs remain recommended in the Bulgarian post-graduate course in spinal oncological surgery.

**Ya. L. Tsivian <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Contribution to posterior-anterior vertebrotomy concepts, applied to severe vertebral deformity after vertebra plana and after aggressive aneurysmal bone cyst with vertebral collapse.

## Classical Sources
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">
<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical line — the nosology of tumor-like lesions</strong>
  <p>The modern nosology of tumor-like lesions of bone is principally the work of three authors of the second quarter of the twentieth century: Henry L. Jaffe (Hospital for Joint Diseases, New York), Louis Lichtenstein (his collaborator and successor), and Fritz Schajowicz (Buenos Aires, Latin American Registry of Bone Pathology). To them are added: Edwin A. Lodwick — the radiographic classification of bone destruction (1966); William F. Enneking — surgical staging and principles of resection (1980, 1986); Mario Campanacci — the Bolognese orthopaedic-oncologic school, with an extensive monograph (1990, 1999); and K. Krishnan Unni with Carrie Inwards — successors of the classical Lichtenstein-Dahlin series in *Dahlin's Bone Tumors* (6th ed., 2010).</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Jaffe and Lichtenstein 1942</strong> — *Solitary unicameral bone cyst* (Arch Surg) and *Aneurysmal bone cyst* (Arch Pathol): the dual paper defining the simple and aneurysmal bone cysts as distinct entities.</li>
    <li><strong>Lichtenstein 1938</strong> — *Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia* (Arch Surg): the first nosological description of fibrous dysplasia as a distinct entity.</li>
    <li><strong>Lichtenstein 1953</strong> — *Histiocytosis X*: the unifying concept of eosinophilic granuloma, Hand-Schüller-Christian disease, and Letterer-Siwe disease as a single nosological category.</li>
    <li><strong>Jaffe 1958</strong> — *Tumors and Tumorous Conditions of the Bones and Joints*: the integrated textbook of bone-lesion nosology.</li>
    <li><strong>Lodwick 1966</strong> — *A systematic approach to the roentgen diagnosis of bone tumors*: the classification of bone destruction into geographic, moth-eaten, and permeative.</li>
    <li><strong>Reynolds 1969</strong> — description of the fallen-fragment sign in simple bone cyst.</li>
    <li><strong>Enneking, Spanier, Goodman 1980</strong> — *A system for the surgical staging of musculoskeletal sarcoma*: surgical staging, the foundation of contemporary orthopaedic oncological surgery.</li>
    <li><strong>Mankin, Mankin, Simon 1996</strong> — reprise of the classical 1982 assessment of biopsy quality: a poorly planned biopsy compromises the oncological outcome in 17–22% of cases.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Classical descriptions of the lesions in this chapter: **von Recklinghausen 1891** — *osteitis fibrosa cystica* and the description of hyperparathyroid bone manifestations; **Calvé 1925** — vertebra plana as a separate entity; **Léri 1922** — melorheostosis; **Voorhoeve 1924** — osteopathia striata; **Buschke and Ollendorff 1928** — the association of osteopoikilosis with collagenous skin nodules; **McCune 1936** and **Albright 1937** — the syndrome of polyostotic fibrous dysplasia with endocrine hyperfunction; **Mazabraud 1967** — the association of fibrous dysplasia with intramuscular myxomas; **Brooker 1973** — classification of heterotopic ossification after total hip arthroplasty; **Campanacci, Laus, Boriani 1983** — Jaffe-Campanacci syndrome; **Mirels 1989** — the pathological-fracture risk score; **Oliveira et al. 2004** — USP6 translocation in primary aneurysmal bone cyst.

Further classical sources available in facsimile: **Billroth 1882** — *Allgemeine chirurgische Pathologie und Therapie*: the histopathologic vocabulary of bone lesions before the Jaffe era ([Facsimile](https://archive.org/details/dieallgemeinechi1882bill)). **Lexer 1904** — *Lehrbuch der allgemeinen Chirurgie*, vol. 2 ([Facsimile](https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderallg01lexegoog)). **Hoffa 1905** — *Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie* ([Facsimile](https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderortho00hoff)).
