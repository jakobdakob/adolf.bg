## Introduction

The flatfoot — pes planus — encompasses a heterogeneous group of foot morphologies in which the medial longitudinal arch is reduced or absent and the hindfoot assumes a valgus position. The spectrum ranges from the physiological flexible flatfoot of childhood, requiring no treatment in the asymptomatic patient, through the rigid congenital flatfoot of the talocalcaneal coalition and congenital vertical talus, through the symptomatic adult-acquired flatfoot of progressive posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, to the severely deformed flatfoot of neuromuscular disease (myelomeningocele, cerebral palsy, arthrogryposis). The clinical evaluation of the flatfoot must distinguish flexible from rigid forms, identify any underlying neuromuscular condition or systemic joint hypermobility, and determine whether the deformity is producing symptoms or functional limitation. The second major topic — hereditary joint laxity — encompasses conditions in which generalised joint hypermobility is the principal clinical feature: from the asymptomatic generalised joint hypermobility seen in approximately 10–15% of the general population, through the hypermobility syndrome and hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, to the severe connective tissue disorders Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, and the classical Ehlers-Danlos variants. This chapter synthesises content from Mosca, *Principles and Management of Pediatric Foot and Ankle Deformities* (LWW, 2014); Coughlin, Saltzman and Anderson, *Mann's Surgery of the Foot and Ankle* (Elsevier, 2013); Beighton, Grahame and Bird, *Hypermobility of Joints* (4th ed., Springer, 2012); Flynn, Weinstein and Crawford, *Lovell and Winter's Pediatric Orthopaedics* (8th ed., LWW, 2020); and Herring, *Tachdjian's Procedures in Pediatric Orthopaedics* (Elsevier, 2016).

## Anatomy and Development of the Medial Longitudinal Arch

The medial longitudinal arch is formed by the osseous triangle calcaneus–talus–navicular–medial cuneiform–first metatarsal, supported by three static structures — the plantar aponeurosis (a tension-band element of "bowstring" type), the spring (calcaneonavicular) ligament, and the short plantar ligaments — and by three dynamic muscles — the posterior tibial muscle, the flexor hallucis longus, and the short intrinsic muscles of the foot. The arch is absent at birth and develops gradually through childhood through reduction of the subcutaneous fat in the medial arch, maturation of ligamentous tone, and development of muscular control. Definitive morphology is reached approximately between the 8th and 12th year of life. The "flatfoot" appearance in a child under 6–8 years of age is therefore a physiological finding, not a disease. Distinguishing the physiological (flexible, asymptomatic, developing) from the pathological flatfoot (rigid, symptomatic, or progressive in adulthood) is the central clinical task.

## Flexible Flatfoot of Childhood

### Definition and Epidemiology

The flexible flatfoot is the most common form of pes planus and is essentially a physiological variant of normal foot development. The young child (up to age 6–8 years) normally has an apparent flatfoot because of the abundant subcutaneous fat in the medial arch, the relative ligamentous laxity of childhood, and the not-yet-developed muscular control of the longitudinal arch. The prevalence of flexible flatfoot in children below age 5 has been quoted as 25–50%, falling to 5–15% in older children, with the rate of resolution being highest in the youngest age groups. Mosca emphasises that the flexible flatfoot is "an anatomic variant, not a deformity" and that the great majority of children require no treatment. The classical Lovell & Winter review shows that for asymptomatic children with flexible pes planus, there is no evidence that arch supports, orthotics, or special shoes influence arch development, and some studies have shown that aggressive orthotic therapy may actually increase symptoms by restricting the normal development of the intrinsic muscles.

### Clinical Features

The cardinal feature is reduction or absence of the medial longitudinal arch on weight-bearing, with restoration of the arch on non-weight-bearing examination, on passive dorsiflexion of the great toe (the Jack toe-raise test for "winding the bowstring" — the dorsiflexion of the hallux tightens the plantar aponeurosis and tensions the arch through the "windlass" mechanism), and on tip-toe standing — the so-called "flexibility tests." On tip-toe standing, the hindfoot rolls into varus and the medial arch re-forms in the flexible flatfoot, whereas in the rigid flatfoot the hindfoot remains in valgus and no arch develops. Hindfoot valgus (typically 8–15°) and forefoot abduction relative to the midfoot are typical. The foot is supple, asymptomatic, and well-tolerant of ordinary activity in the great majority of children. Mosca describes a special subtype — "flexible flatfoot with short (tight) Achilles or gastrocnemius tendon" — in which the flexible flatfoot is combined with contracture of the gastrocnemius or entire triceps surae (less than 10° dorsiflexion at the ankle with the subtalar joint held in neutral); this subgroup is substantially more symptomatic and responds less well to conservative treatment than the standard flexible flatfoot.

### Investigations

The flexible flatfoot in the asymptomatic child requires no specific investigation. Weight-bearing radiographs (AP and lateral views of both feet) are obtained in symptomatic or atypical cases and demonstrate: increased talocalcaneal angle (>40° on the AP view), plantarflexion of the talus (lateral view), calcaneal valgus, and forefoot abduction (increased talo-navicular coverage angle). The Meary angle (the angle between the long axis of the talus and the first metatarsal on the lateral view) is increased in flatfoot, normally being 0°; values above 10° indicate moderate, and above 20° severe pes planus. The calcaneal pitch (the angle between the plantar surface of the calcaneus and the horizontal) is reduced in flatfoot, normally being 20–30°. The Silfverskiold test — assessment of dorsiflexion with the knee flexed versus extended — distinguishes isolated gastrocnemius contracture (which corrects with knee flexion) from triceps surae contracture (in which dorsiflexion remains restricted in both positions).

### Treatment

The asymptomatic flexible flatfoot of childhood requires no treatment. Reassurance of parents and continued observation is appropriate. The widespread practice of prescribing arch supports, special shoes, and orthotics for the asymptomatic flexible flatfoot has been challenged by long-term studies showing no benefit on foot morphology and no effect on later development; Mosca formulates the principle: "do not treat that which is not a disease." The symptomatic flexible flatfoot — typically with foot fatigue or pain after prolonged activity in the older child — may benefit from supportive orthotic insoles (a soft medial-arch support with hindfoot valgus correction), stretching of a tight Achilles tendon if present, and selective use of activity modification. Mosca emphasises stretching of the gastrocnemius in the short-Achilles subtype — often it is the gastrocnemius contracture rather than the flat arch itself that produces the symptoms. Surgical management is rarely required in the flexible flatfoot of childhood. When symptoms persist into adolescence despite conservative measures, options include:

- **Lateral column lengthening (Evans osteotomy)** — an osteotomy of the anterior calcaneus with a tricortical bone block (tibial autograft or allograft), lengthening the lateral column of the foot and restoring the medial longitudinal arch through the "springing" effect on the spring ligament. This is the standard procedure for the symptomatic flexible flatfoot in the adolescent who has failed conservative treatment; the technique as originally described by Mosca uses a trapezoidal bone block (8–12 mm length on the lateral wall) and is usually combined with a gastrocnemius recession or Achilles tendon lengthening.
- **Subtalar arthroereisis** — placement of an interference implant in the sinus tarsi to limit excessive subtalar pronation. Results are variable and often impermanent; implants can produce sinus-tarsi pain requiring removal. Increasingly displaced by the Evans calcaneal osteotomy as definitive management.
- **Medial column procedures** — Cotton osteotomy (medial cuneiform plantar opening-wedge osteotomy), Hoke arthrodesis (talonavicular and naviculocuneiform fusion), used in cases of marked medial column instability or segmental deformity.
- **Achilles tendon lengthening or gastrocnemius recession** — a mandatory component when a tight heel cord is present.

## Rigid Flatfoot of Childhood — Specific Etiologies

A rigid flatfoot in childhood — that is, a flatfoot that does not correct on tip-toe testing, on the Jack toe-raise, or on manual manipulation, and that lacks the supple character of the flexible flatfoot — is always pathological and requires investigation for an underlying cause. The principal etiologies are tarsal coalition, congenital vertical talus, and acquired conditions including post-traumatic deformity, neuromuscular disease, and inflammatory arthritis.

### Tarsal Coalition

Tarsal coalition is a congenital fibrous, cartilaginous, or osseous union of two or more tarsal bones, producing a rigid hindfoot and the typical "peroneal spastic flatfoot." The talocalcaneal coalition (most commonly involving the middle facet of the subtalar joint) and the calcaneonavicular coalition together account for the great majority of cases. The overall prevalence is estimated at about 1% of the population, but 75% of coalitions remain asymptomatic lifelong; the condition is considered autosomal dominant with variable penetrance. Presentation typically occurs in adolescence (talocalcaneal coalitions — age 12–16 years; calcaneonavicular coalitions — age 8–12 years) as the previously cartilaginous coalition begins to ossify, producing progressive rigidity and pain; younger children with fibrous coalitions may be asymptomatic.

Examination reveals a rigid flatfoot with absent restoration of the medial arch on tip-toe testing, restricted subtalar and midtarsal motion, peroneal spasm with attempted inversion (the "peroneal spastic flatfoot"), and tenderness over the affected coalition — over the sinus tarsi for calcaneonavicular, below the medial malleolus for middle-facet talocalcaneal. Lovell & Winter emphasise that recurrent "ankle sprains" in an adolescent deserve evaluation for an underlying tarsal coalition. Imaging is critical: plain radiographs may show the "C-sign" of talocalcaneal coalition (a continuous arc on the lateral view from the medial talar dome to the inferior surface of the sustentaculum tali, indicating obliteration of the subtalar joint by the coalition), the "anteater nose" sign of calcaneonavicular coalition (elongation of the anterior process of the calcaneus on the lateral view, resembling the snout of an anteater), and the talar beak (a prominent dorsal talar prominence at the talonavicular joint, representing reactive bone-pull from chronic abnormal loading). The 45° oblique radiograph is most useful for calcaneonavicular coalition; CT is the gold standard for the talocalcaneal coalition and quantifies the extent of involvement — critical for the choice between resection and arthrodesis. MRI demonstrates fibrous or cartilaginous coalitions that are not visible on plain radiographs.

Treatment is initially conservative: cast immobilisation (typically below-knee cast for 4–6 weeks), a CAM walker, peroneal lateral-heel wedge, and activity modification. About 30% of symptomatic patients achieve durable relief with conservative treatment. Persistent symptoms despite conservative therapy are an indication for surgery. Surgical options are:

- **Coalition resection with interposition** — in younger patients (talocalcaneal coalition involving <50% of the middle facet on CT, without arthritis in adjacent joints) with interposition of fat or muscle. Calcaneonavicular coalitions respond particularly well to resection with interposition of the extensor digitorum brevis muscle (the Badgley procedure); reported success rates are 75–90% in appropriately selected patients.
- **Arthrodesis** — subtalar fusion (for isolated subtalar joint involvement), triple arthrodesis (for extensive involvement, arthritis, or failed resection). In adults with established arthritis or in coalitions involving >50% of the joint surface, triple arthrodesis is the reliable solution, at the cost of permanent loss of subtalar and midtarsal motion.

### Congenital Vertical Talus

Congenital vertical talus (CVT) is a rare but severe rigid flatfoot deformity in which the talus is fixed in a vertical position with dorsolateral dislocation of the navicular onto the talar neck, producing the characteristic "rocker-bottom" foot of the newborn. The condition is bilateral in approximately 50% of cases. Approximately half of cases are associated with neuromuscular conditions (myelomeningocele, arthrogryposis, chromosomal abnormalities — especially trisomies 13, 15, and 18, distal arthrogryposis); the other half are isolated. Familial cases with autosomal dominant inheritance have been described, some with mutations in the HOXD10 gene.

Presentation is at birth with the rocker-bottom foot — the dorsal skin of the foot creased and tight while the plantar surface protrudes convexly, the forefoot dorsiflexed and abducted, the hindfoot in equinus and valgus, and the foot rigid with severely restricted passive motion. The differential diagnosis is from the much more common "calcaneovalgus foot" (a positional deformity of the newborn that resolves with simple massage and stretching), oblique talus (a milder variant in which the navicular subluxates but reduces with plantarflexion), and the rocker-bottom foot of severe equinovarus correction.

Radiographs in the neonate show the characteristic vertical position of the talus on the lateral view — the talar axis is almost vertical, directed toward the sole, instead of the normal 30–50° to the horizontal. Stress views — forced plantarflexion of the foot in the lateral projection — distinguish CVT from oblique talus: in CVT, the navicular remains dislocated from the talus even with maximal plantarflexion, while in oblique talus the navicular reduces. In neonates in whom the navicular has not yet ossified (ossification begins about age 3), the position is judged by the axis of the first metatarsal, which in CVT remains dorsally displaced relative to the talus even with maximal plantarflexion.

Treatment has been transformed in recent decades by the Dobbs reverse Ponseti technique, described in the late 1990s and now established as the first-line standard. Modelled on the Ponseti method for clubfoot but in reverse, it involves: (1) weekly serial casts in progressive plantarflexion and inversion to correct the dorsolateral dislocation of the navicular — typically 5–7 casts; (2) percutaneous reduction and K-wire fixation of the talonavicular joint in plantarflexion and inversion; (3) percutaneous Achilles tenotomy to correct the equinus component; (4) cast immobilisation for 6 weeks after the procedure; (5) maintenance bracing with a foot abduction boot (Mitchell or Markell type) until age 4 years on a daytime regimen, progressively decreasing. Reported success rates with the Dobbs technique in idiopathic cases are 80–95%; results in syndromic cases (arthrogryposis, myelomeningocele) are more variable. Before the advent of the Dobbs technique, the standard was extensive surgical reconstruction requiring open reduction of the talonavicular joint, Achilles tendon lengthening, peroneal tendon transfer, and capsulotomies — with higher complication rates and adult-life rigidity.

### Skewfoot (Serpentine or Z-Foot)

Skewfoot — also called the serpentine or Z-foot — is a complex three-segment deformity: forefoot metatarsus adductus, midfoot lateral translation of the navicular, and hindfoot valgus. It may arise idiopathically or as residual deformity after incomplete correction of clubfoot (especially after old Achilles tenotomy without midfoot correction). Mosca emphasises that recognising skewfoot requires weight-bearing radiographs — clinical assessment alone may miss it. Treatment in the child under age 4 is serial casting; in the older child with fixed deformity, a combination of lateral column lengthening (Evans osteotomy) with medial cuneiform opening-wedge osteotomy (Cotton osteotomy) or with cuboid opening-wedge osteotomy (for more extensive correction).

### Other Causes of Rigid Childhood Flatfoot

Post-traumatic deformity (typically from talar neck or calcaneal fractures of childhood, or after an untreated tarsometatarsal Lisfranc dislocation) produces a rigid flatfoot through bony deformity and joint malalignment. Neuromuscular flatfoot — particularly in cerebral palsy, spina bifida (myelomeningocele), and the late stages of poliomyelitis — produces a complex deformity with components of valgus, abduction, and equinus that requires individualised assessment and treatment. In cerebral palsy with diplegia or hemiplegia, the valgus foot is frequently combined with gastrocnemius contracture and peroneal spasticity; standard treatment is Evans lateral column lengthening combined with gastrocnemius lengthening and, when indicated, peroneus brevis transfer. Inflammatory arthritis — juvenile idiopathic arthritis — produces progressive deformity of the subtalar and midtarsal joints with painful flatfoot; primary treatment is systemic therapy of the underlying disease (methotrexate, biologic agents) with adjunctive orthotic support, and selective arthrodesis if joint destruction is established.

## Adult-Acquired Flatfoot Deformity and Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction

### Definition and Pathophysiology

Adult-acquired flatfoot deformity (AAFD) is a progressive deformity of the adult foot characterised by collapse of the medial longitudinal arch, hindfoot valgus, forefoot abduction, and eventually first-ray instability with dorsiflexion of the first metatarsal. The principal underlying pathology in the great majority of cases is posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD) — a degenerative tendinopathy and ultimately tendon rupture of the posterior tibial tendon. The posterior tibial muscle is the chief dynamic inverter of the foot and the only muscle capable of elevating the medial arch during heel rise; when it becomes insufficient, the peroneal muscles (antagonists) act unopposed and produce progressive hindfoot valgus and forefoot abduction. With progression, secondary spring (calcaneonavicular) ligament insufficiency develops, followed by elongation of the medial static restraints (deltoid ligament, plantar ligaments) and the formation of a fixed deformity.

Coughlin and Mann describe the histopathology of the ruptured posterior tibial tendon as mucoid degeneration with interstitial splitting, neovascularisation, and hypercellularity — a pattern identical to the degenerative tendinopathies of the Achilles tendon and rotator cuff; inflammation is minimal, and a chronic degenerative process dominates. The tendon is particularly vulnerable in the "hypovascular zone" 2 cm distal to the medial malleolus — an anatomic region with relatively poor blood supply that degenerates over years with repetitive microtrauma. The condition predominantly affects women aged 40–65 years, with risk factors including obesity (BMI >30), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid, psoriatic), previous medial ankle or tendon injury, and a history of corticosteroid injections. The natural history is one of progressive deformity over years; without intervention, a flexible deformity becomes fixed and eventually arthritic.

### Johnson-Strom Classification (Myerson Modification)

The standard classification recognises four stages:

**Stage I**: Tenosynovitis without deformity. The tendon is inflamed but functionally intact; the foot retains normal morphology. Pain is localised to the medial ankle along the course of the tendon. Single-limb heel rise is possible but painful. Imaging shows normal radiographs and MRI evidence of tenosynovitis without longitudinal tear.

**Stage II**: Tendon degeneration with flexible deformity. The flatfoot deformity is present but passively correctable; the hindfoot can be brought into varus and the forefoot abducted to neutral on physical examination. Single-limb heel rise is not possible. MRI shows a longitudinal tear or tendinopathy with or without thickening. Myerson subdivides Stage II into IIa (minimal forefoot abduction, <40% uncoverage of the talonavicular joint on AP) and IIb (significant abduction, >40%) — the distinction has treatment implications, as IIb requires more extensive reconstruction with lateral column lengthening.

**Stage III**: Fixed deformity. The hindfoot is in fixed valgus, the forefoot in fixed abduction, and passive correction is no longer possible. Subtalar arthritis is typically present.

**Stage IV** (Myerson addition): Stage III deformity with ankle valgus (deltoid ligament insufficiency with lateral talar tilt within the ankle mortise), often with degenerative changes at the ankle joint, typically from chronic loading in valgus. Myerson further subdivides Stage IV into IVa (preserved ankle joint surface) and IVb (ankle arthritis) — the distinction determines whether the ankle can be addressed by soft-tissue reconstruction (IVa) or requires arthroplasty/arthrodesis (IVb).

### Clinical Evaluation

Presentation is with medial ankle pain — particularly along the course of the posterior tibial tendon behind the medial malleolus — often progressing over months to years to include lateral hindfoot pain (from subfibular impingement as the calcaneus drifts into valgus), and progressive flattening of the foot. Examination findings include:

- **The "too many toes" sign**: on weight-bearing inspection from behind, more than the normal one-and-a-half toes are visible lateral to the heel — a consequence of forefoot abduction.
- **Inability to perform a single-limb heel rise**: the most reliable functional test of posterior tibial tendon function; in PTTD the patient cannot lift the heel on the affected side and/or the heel does not move from valgus to varus during the rise.
- **Tenderness along the course of the posterior tibial tendon**, especially behind and below the medial malleolus.
- **The "first metatarsal rise" sign**: on tip-toe standing, the medial side of the foot lifts off the ground, even bilaterally, indicating posterior tibial tendon failure.
- Reduced strength of resisted inversion in plantarflexion.

Range of motion of the subtalar and midtarsal joints should be assessed and compared with the contralateral side; loss of motion suggests fixed deformity. The Silfverskiold test for gastrocnemius contracture is mandatory — at least 70% of PTTD patients have a clinically significant gastrocnemius contracture.

### Imaging

Weight-bearing radiographs (AP and lateral foot, AP ankle) provide the principal imaging assessment. On the lateral view: the Meary angle (talus-first metatarsal angle) is increased (positive); the calcaneal pitch is reduced (below 18°); the talar tilt is increased. On the AP foot view: the talonavicular coverage angle is reduced (below 60%); the talus-first metatarsal angle is increased. On the AP ankle view in Stage IV, valgus tilt of the talus within the mortise is visible. MRI is the imaging modality of choice for assessment of the posterior tibial tendon itself, showing tenosynovitis in early disease and tendon thickening, longitudinal splits, and frank rupture in advanced disease. MRI also evaluates concomitant spring ligament lesions, accessory inverter lesions, and any osteoarthritic changes at the subtalar and talonavicular joints. Ultrasonography is a useful alternative for dynamic assessment of the tendon and for image-guided injections.

### Treatment

**Stage I** disease is treated conservatively: rest, NSAIDs, immobilisation in a CAM walker or short-leg walking cast for 4–6 weeks, and physiotherapy directed at eccentric strengthening of the posterior tibial tendon and stretching of the Achilles tendon. Custom orthotics (typically a medial heel post UCBL — University of California Berkeley Laboratory device, or a medial-arch support with hindfoot posting) provide ongoing support. Local corticosteroid injections into the tendon sheath are avoided — they increase the risk of tendon rupture. Persistent disease after 3–6 months of conservative treatment may require operative tenosynovectomy and tendon debridement with continued orthotic management.

**Stage II** disease (flexible deformity) requires reconstructive surgery in most symptomatic patients. The standard reconstruction combines several procedures, individualised to the specific deformity components present:

1. **Flexor digitorum longus (FDL) tendon transfer to the navicular**, providing a new dynamic inverter to replace the failed posterior tibial tendon. The posterior tibial tendon itself, if accessible, is debrided, or if frankly ruptured, excised.
2. **Medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy (MDCO)** — an oblique osteotomy of the calcaneal tuberosity, usually through a lateral approach, with medial displacement of the posterior fragment 8–12 mm to correct hindfoot valgus and reduce the Achilles tendon moment. Fixation is with one or two cannulated screws or a dedicated step plate.
3. **Lateral column lengthening (Evans osteotomy or distraction calcaneocuboid arthrodesis)** in Stage IIb with marked forefoot abduction (>40% uncoverage). The Evans osteotomy is performed through the anterior calcaneus, 12–15 mm proximal to the calcaneocuboid joint, with a tricortical bone block (typically 8–10 mm long) from the iliac crest or as an allograft. It lengthens the lateral column, corrects forefoot abduction, and restores the medial arch by tensioning the spring ligament complex.
4. **Cotton osteotomy** (medial cuneiform plantar opening-wedge osteotomy with bone graft of 4–8 mm) — to correct first-ray dorsiflexion and restore forefoot tripod equilibrium. Performed for residual forefoot supination after hindfoot correction.
5. **Achilles tendon lengthening or gastrocnemius recession** for a positive Silfverskiold test. More than 70% of PTTD patients require this procedure.
6. **Spring ligament reconstruction** for marked insufficiency, typically by direct repair or allograft augmentation.

The recovery period is 8–12 weeks non-weight-bearing, followed by 8–12 weeks in a CAM walker with progressive weight-bearing. Outcomes in appropriately selected patients are good in 80–90%, with significant pain relief and functional improvement.

**Stage III** disease (fixed deformity) requires arthrodesis to address the established malalignment and associated arthritis. The standard procedure is triple arthrodesis (fusion of the talonavicular, subtalar, and calcaneocuboid joints), providing definitive correction of the deformity with permanent loss of subtalar and midtarsal motion. Modified procedures preserving selected joints (e.g., isolated subtalar arthrodesis when subtalar arthritis dominates, or double arthrodesis — talonavicular and subtalar with preservation of the calcaneocuboid — to reduce adjacent-joint arthritis risk at the talonavicular) are used in selected cases.

**Stage IV with ankle involvement** requires a tiered approach: in Stage IVa (preserved ankle joint surface), deltoid ligament reconstruction (direct repair or allograft augmentation) is combined with the described hindfoot reconstruction; in Stage IVb (ankle arthritis), total ankle arthroplasty (in patients with adequate alignment and bone stock) or ankle arthrodesis, combined with appropriate hindfoot reconstruction (tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis with an intramedullary nail in severe cases).

### Other Causes of Adult-Acquired Flatfoot

Although posterior tibial tendon dysfunction is by far the commonest cause of adult-acquired flatfoot, other etiologies must be considered, particularly when the clinical picture does not fit the classical PTTD presentation.

**Charcot neuroarthropathy** in diabetic patients produces a progressive collapse of the midfoot through neuropathic destruction of the joints, often producing a rocker-bottom deformity in advanced disease. The orthopaedic principles are early recognition of the acute phase (a hot, swollen, red foot without overt infection in a diabetic with neuropathy), total contact casting and immobilisation in the acute phase, and reconstructive surgery (typically Charcot reconstructive surgery with extended fusion and intramedullary or plate fixation, sometimes with "beaming" — intramedullary screws across the foot columns) for unstable or unbraceable deformity.

**Spring ligament insufficiency** can produce a flexible flatfoot without significant posterior tibial tendon dysfunction. The condition is now increasingly recognised on MRI and arthroscopy; treatment in symptomatic cases includes spring ligament reconstruction combined with appropriate bony correction (medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy).

**Inflammatory arthritis** — particularly rheumatoid arthritis — produces a progressive flatfoot through synovitis of the talonavicular and subtalar joints with progressive joint destruction. Treatment combines systemic medical therapy of the underlying disease with appropriate surgical reconstruction, usually arthrodesis.

**Traumatic flatfoot deformity** follows midfoot or hindfoot fractures and fracture-dislocations (talar neck, calcaneal, midfoot Lisfranc) and is addressed by reconstruction tailored to the bony deformity — corrective osteotomies or arthrodeses at the deformed joint.

## Hereditary Joint Laxity

### Concept and Spectrum

Hereditary joint laxity encompasses a spectrum of conditions in which generalised joint hypermobility is the principal clinical feature. The spectrum extends from the asymptomatic generalised joint hypermobility seen in approximately 10–15% of the general population (more common in younger people, in women, and in certain ethnic groups — particularly Asian and Afro-Caribbean populations), through the hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD), the hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (formerly Ehlers-Danlos type III), to the more severe forms of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome with specific organ involvement, to the related connective tissue disorders Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Beighton emphasises that distinguishing "benign" hypermobility (without symptoms, without structural pathology) from pathological hypermobility (associated with pain, instability, or structural damage) is clinically foundational: the great majority of hypermobile individuals do not develop a symptomatic syndrome, and hypermobility per se is not a disease.

### Beighton Score

The Beighton score is the standard clinical tool for assessing generalised joint hypermobility, proposed by Peter Beighton in 1973 as a modification of an earlier system by Carter and Wilkinson. Nine maneuvers are scored as positive or negative:

1. Passive dorsiflexion of the fifth metacarpophalangeal joint beyond 90° (one point each side).
2. Passive apposition of the thumb to the volar surface of the forearm (one point each side).
3. Hyperextension of the elbow beyond 10° (one point each side).
4. Hyperextension of the knee beyond 10° (one point each side).
5. Palms flat on the floor with knees fully extended on forward bending (one point).

The maximum score is 9; a score of 4 or more in an adult (below age 50), or 6 or more in a prepubertal child, indicates generalised joint hypermobility. Above age 50 the threshold is reduced to 1–2 points because of the age-related decrease in joint mobility. Beighton notes that the score assesses only five joint regions and misses regional hypermobility (e.g., of the hips, spine, temporomandibular joint); a negative Beighton score therefore does not exclude clinically significant hypermobility, and the Brighton criteria (see below) were developed for a more comprehensive clinical assessment.

### Brighton Criteria for the Joint Hypermobility Syndrome

The Brighton criteria for the joint hypermobility syndrome (JHS) were proposed in 1998 by Grahame, Bird, and Child as a consensus instrument for diagnosing symptomatic hypermobility that does not meet criteria for other connective tissue disorders:

**Major criteria**: (1) Beighton score ≥4/9 (current or historical); (2) Arthralgia for >3 months in 4 or more joints.

**Minor criteria**: (1) Beighton score 1–3/9 (0–3 in adults over 50); (2) Arthralgia (>3 months) in 1–3 joints or back pain (>3 months), spondylolysis/spondylolisthesis; (3) Dislocation/subluxation in more than one joint or in one joint on more than one occasion; (4) Soft-tissue rheumatism — ≥3 lesions (epicondylitis, tenosynovitis, bursitis); (5) Marfanoid habitus (tall stature, long fingers, arachnodactyly, positive Steinberg and Walker-Murdoch signs); (6) Skin features (hyperextensibility, thin skin, papyraceous scars, striae); (7) Eye signs (drooping eyelids, myopia, antimongoloid slant); (8) Varicose veins, hernia, uterine or rectal prolapse.

The diagnosis of JHS requires two major criteria, or one major and two minor criteria, or four minor criteria, or two minor criteria with a positive family history. The criteria are regarded as applying to a subset of the hypermobility spectrum, and in the 2017 international classification of Ehlers-Danlos JHS largely overlaps with the diagnostic categories of hypermobile EDS (hEDS) and the hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD).

### Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome and the Joint Hypermobility Spectrum Disorders

The hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS) and the related joint hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD) share the clinical features of generalised joint hypermobility with chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain, recurrent joint dislocations and subluxations, soft and hyperextensible skin (less pronounced than in classical Ehlers-Danlos), easy bruising, and a variety of systemic features including autonomic dysfunction (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome — POTS, gastrointestinal dysmotility, syncope), anxiety, depression, and chronic fatigue. Beighton and co-authors also describe associated sensory disturbances — impaired proprioception, partial insensitivity to local anaesthetics, and impaired thermoregulation. The genetic basis of hEDS is not yet fully characterised — unlike the other EDS subtypes, hEDS remains a clinical diagnosis without an identified gene defect. The diagnostic criteria are defined in the 2017 International Classification of the Ehlers-Danlos Syndromes and require: (1) generalised joint hypermobility (Beighton score ≥6 before puberty, ≥5 for adults up to age 50, ≥4 for adults over 50); (2) two or more of: systemic features, family history, musculoskeletal complications; (3) exclusion of other EDS subtypes, of Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes, and of other connective tissue disorders.

### Other Ehlers-Danlos Subtypes

The 1997 Villefranche classification and its 2017 revision currently identify 13 EDS subtypes, each with a characteristic molecular defect:

**Classical EDS** (cEDS, formerly Types I and II): caused by mutations in COL5A1 or COL5A2 (rarely COL1A1) encoding type V collagen. Clinical features are: marked skin hyperextensibility, atrophic ("papyraceous") scarring after trivial trauma, joint hypermobility with recurrent dislocations, and easy bruising. Inheritance is autosomal dominant.

**Classical-like EDS** (clEDS): tenascin-X deficiency (TNXB mutations, autosomal recessive), with similar hyperextensible skin without atrophic scarring.

**Cardiac-valvular EDS** (cvEDS): COL1A2 mutations with severe mitral and/or aortic valve involvement.

**Vascular EDS** (vEDS, Type IV): caused by mutations in COL3A1 encoding type III collagen. This is the most dangerous subtype — characterised by thin, translucent skin with visible subcutaneous venous patterns, easy bruising, small-joint hypermobility, characteristic facial features (thin lips, narrow nose, sunken eyes), and the catastrophic complications of spontaneous arterial rupture (especially middle cerebral, aorta, iliac, and renal arteries), bowel perforation, and uterine rupture in pregnancy. Median life expectancy is significantly shortened; patients require routine cardiovascular surveillance, avoidance of intense physical activity, and a preference for endovascular over open vascular surgical approaches because of the friability of the arterial wall.

**Kyphoscoliotic EDS** (kEDS): PLOD1 or FKBP14 mutations, with severe congenital kyphoscoliosis, generalised joint hypermobility, hypotonia, and scleral fragility. Beighton emphasises that this subtype is an important, though rare, cause of severe congenital kyphoscoliosis in newborns.

**Arthrochalasis EDS** (aEDS): COL1A1 or COL1A2 mutations, with bilateral congenital hip dislocation, severe joint hypermobility, tendon rupture, and prominent skin hyperextensibility.

**Dermatosparaxis EDS** (dEDS): ADAMTS2 mutations, with extreme skin fragility and skin redundancy resembling parchment.

Other rare subtypes include myopathic, periodontal, spondylodysplastic, and musculocontractural EDS, each with distinct clinical and molecular features.

### Marfan Syndrome

Marfan syndrome is caused by mutations in FBN1 encoding fibrillin-1 — the principal component of the elastic microfibrils in the extracellular matrix. Inheritance is autosomal dominant with high penetrance and variable expression; about 25% of cases are new mutations. Clinical features include:

- **Skeletal**: tall stature with long extremities and digits (arachnodactyly), increased limb-to-trunk ratio (increased arm-span-to-height and crown-to-pubis ratios), pectus excavatum or carinatum, scoliosis (especially thoracolumbar), dolichocephaly, high palatal arch, pes planus.
- **Cardiovascular**: aortic root dilation with risk of dissection and rupture (the principal cause of premature mortality), mitral valve prolapse, dilation of the aortic arch.
- **Ocular**: lens subluxation (typically upward and outward, distinguishing from Marfanoid syndromes), myopia with high risk of retinal detachment.
- **Dural**: dural ectasia in the lumbosacral region (visible on MRI in over 60% of adults), potentially associated with chronic back pain.
- **Other**: spontaneous pneumothorax, striae, hernia.

The diagnosis follows the Ghent criteria of 1996, revised in 2010, which integrate cardiovascular (especially aortic dilation and lens subluxation as major criteria), skeletal, dural, and family history features. An aortic root Z-score >2 in the absence of family history, or >1 with positive family history, is critical for the diagnosis. Orthopaedic features include scoliosis (often requiring bracing or surgical correction, typically posterior spinal fusion with instrumentation; cervical dura may be dilated, requiring care with subocciput approaches), protrusio acetabuli (bilateral, sometimes requiring arthroplastic reconstruction in adult life), pes planus, hindfoot valgus, and joint hypermobility. The orthopaedic surgeon must be aware of the cardiovascular implications of Marfan syndrome and coordinate care with the cardiologist; any planned operation requires preoperative echocardiography for assessment of the aortic root and mitral valve, and anaesthesia review for the risks of endotracheal intubation (cervical instability with dural ectasia) and of perioperative hypotension (risk of aortic dissection).

### Loeys-Dietz Syndrome

Loeys-Dietz syndrome, described in 2005, is caused by mutations in TGFBR1 or TGFBR2 (or, less commonly, SMAD3, TGFB2, TGFB3) encoding receptors and ligands of the TGF-β pathway. It shares many features of Marfan syndrome (Marfanoid skeletal habitus, aortic involvement, joint hypermobility) but with more aggressive aortic disease — patients are at risk of aortic dissection at a smaller diameter than in Marfan (4.0 cm vs 5.0 cm in the general recommendation) and with wider arterial involvement (cerebral, pulmonary, iliac arteries — formation of "branched aneurysms" throughout the arterial tree). Additional clinical features include hypertelorism (widely-spaced eyes), cleft palate or bifid uvula, craniosynostosis, and sometimes midface anomalies. The same orthopaedic manifestations as in Marfan (scoliosis, pes planus, joint hypermobility) may be present, but cardiovascular implications dominate management and require more frequent monitoring and a lower threshold for surgical intervention.

### Clinical Management of the Hypermobile Patient

The orthopaedic care of the hypermobile patient requires attention to several specific issues.

**Recurrent joint dislocation** — particularly of the patella, shoulder, hip, and temporomandibular joint — is common. Conservative management with bracing, specific physiotherapy focused on proprioceptive retraining and dynamic stabilisation, and activity pacing is the first line. Operative stabilisation of the persistently dislocating joint must consider the higher-than-usual rate of failure of soft-tissue procedures (because of the underlying collagen disorder) and the higher rate of complications generally. Beighton recommends that joint stabilisation in the hypermobile patient should, where possible, include bony procedures (e.g., trochleoplasty for patellar instability, Latarjet bone-block procedures for anterior shoulder instability) in preference to purely soft-tissue procedures.

**Chronic widespread pain** in the hypermobile patient often responds poorly to typical analgesic strategies and benefits from a multidisciplinary approach including specific physiotherapy (aimed at core stabilisation and end-range control rather than stretching), pacing and activity-control strategies, cognitive-behavioural therapy for comorbid anxiety and depression, and where indicated patient-specific drug therapy, generally avoiding opiates given the addiction risk in this often chronically painful population.

**Preoperative considerations** include: cardiovascular risks in Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes (intraoperative aortic dissection is a recognised risk — preoperative echocardiography, perioperative beta-blockade, and strict blood pressure control are required); increased risk of poor wound healing and atrophic scarring in classical Ehlers-Danlos (gentler suture materials, prolonged suture retention, wound taping or specialised dressings); increased risk of subluxation and dislocation under anaesthesia (care with positioning, particularly of head and neck in kyphoscoliotic EDS); and the catastrophic risks in vascular EDS (any invasive procedure requires multidisciplinary review; endovascular approaches are preferred over open vascular intervention; care with central venous puncture).

**Pregnancy and the hypermobile patient** — Beighton notes that pregnancy in hypermobile women often produces an increase in ligamentous laxity with worsening of pelvic-girdle, sacroiliac, and lumbar spine pain; delivery may be more rapid, but with a higher risk of perineal tear and of neonatal hip dysplasia. In vEDS, pregnancy is catastrophically dangerous (risk of uterine rupture, aortic dissection) and patients are counselled to avoid pregnancy or for careful management with multidisciplinary input.

**The hypermobile child** — Beighton devotes a special chapter to childhood hypermobility, observing that so-called "growing pains" are often a manifestation of a hypermobility syndrome; the diagnosis of hypermobility in a child requires the higher threshold of 6/9 on the Beighton score rather than the adult 4/9. Treatment is symptomatic, with encouragement of low-impact stabilising physical activity (swimming, cycling), avoidance of hyperextension manoeuvres in gymnastics and dance in those with marked symptoms, and family support.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

The flatfoot is a heterogeneous group of foot morphologies and disorders ranging from the physiological flexible flatfoot of childhood — requiring no treatment when asymptomatic — through the rigid pathological flatfoot of tarsal coalition and congenital vertical talus — requiring specific surgical management — to the adult-acquired flatfoot deformity of posterior tibial tendon dysfunction — staged by the Johnson-Strom-Myerson classification and treated with progressively more extensive surgery as the disease advances. Mosca asserts the principle "do not treat that which is not a disease" for the asymptomatic flexible flatfoot, and Coughlin and Mann define the modern multi-component approach to PTTD: FDL transfer to the navicular, medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy, Evans lateral column lengthening in Stage IIb, Cotton medial cuneiform osteotomy, and gastrocnemius lengthening as a standard reconstruction. Congenital vertical talus is now treated with the Dobbs reverse Ponseti technique, supplanting the previously extensive open reconstruction.

Hereditary joint laxity, assessed by the Beighton score and the more comprehensive Brighton criteria, extends from the asymptomatic generalised joint hypermobility (10–15% of the population) through the hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and the joint hypermobility spectrum disorders to the more severe connective tissue disorders Marfan and Loeys-Dietz — each with characteristic orthopaedic manifestations and specific perioperative considerations. Beighton emphasises that, unlike the other EDS subtypes, hEDS remains a clinical diagnosis without an identified gene defect. Vascular EDS (Type IV) requires particular respect because of the risk of spontaneous arterial and bowel rupture. Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes require multidisciplinary coordination with the cardiologist; orthopaedic features (scoliosis, protrusio acetabuli, pes planus) are managed by standard principles, but always in the context of the cardiovascular risk.

The clinical evaluation of the flatfoot must distinguish flexible from rigid forms, identify any underlying neuromuscular or systemic condition, and determine whether the deformity is producing symptoms or functional limitation; management must be tailored to the specific etiology, the severity and reducibility of the deformity, and the individual patient's functional demands and comorbidities. Screening for an underlying hypermobility syndrome is mandatory in any patient with bilateral symmetrical foot deformity, recurrent joint dislocation, or an atypical clinical course.
