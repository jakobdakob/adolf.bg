## Introduction

Congenital talipes equinovarus (CTEV) — clubfoot — is among the most recognizable congenital deformities in orthopedics and one of the commonest congenital musculoskeletal anomalies, with an incidence of approximately 1–2 per 1,000 live births worldwide. The classical deformity combines four components summarized by the **CAVE** mnemonic: **C**avus of the midfoot, **A**dductus of the forefoot, **V**arus of the hindfoot, and **E**quinus of the ankle — combined with internal tibial torsion and varying degrees of muscle and soft-tissue contracture. The deformity is not simply positional; it represents a fundamental malalignment of the talocalcaneonavicular and talocalcaneal complex, in which any attempted treatment must address the anatomical relationships rather than the surface-visible components individually.

The treatment of clubfoot underwent one of the most dramatic transformations in pediatric orthopedics in the twentieth century. Until the 1990s, the standard was extensive posteromedial soft-tissue release (PMR), which achieved visual correction at the cost of a stiff, painful, arthritic foot in adulthood. The method of Ignacio Ponseti — serial casting in anatomical sequence, followed by percutaneous Achilles tenotomy and prolonged foot-abduction bracing — has displaced primary surgery almost entirely and is now the global standard for idiopathic clubfoot. This chapter synthesizes content from **Congenital Clubfoot: Fundamentals of Treatment** by Ponseti (1996), **Clubfoot: Ponseti Management** by Staheli (3rd ed., 2009), **Principles and Management of Pediatric Foot and Ankle Deformities and Malformations** by Mosca (2014), **Lovell and Winter's Pediatric Orthopaedics** (8e, 2020), **Tachdjian's Procedures in Pediatric Orthopaedics** (Herring, 2016), **Paediatric Orthopaedics and Fractures** by Sharrard (3rd ed., 1993), and **The Art and Practice of Children's Orthopaedics** by Wenger and Rang (1993). It covers etiology, pathological anatomy, classification, clinical features, the original Ponseti technique, post-Ponseti surgical management of residual or recurrent deformity, the syndromic and complex clubfoot, long-term outcomes, and local contributions.

## Epidemiology and etiology

The incidence of clubfoot varies with population: approximately 0.93–1.5 per 1,000 in white populations of European descent, 0.6 per 1,000 in East Asian populations, 0.9 per 1,000 in the United Kingdom and Western Australia, 1.1 per 1,000 in Norway, 1.2 per 1,000 in Uganda, and as high as 6.8 per 1,000 in Hawaiians, Polynesians, and Maori populations (Lovell & Winter, 8e, ch. 28). The condition is approximately twice as common in boys as in girls. Bilateral involvement occurs in approximately 50% of cases.

A positive family history is present in approximately 25% of cases, supporting a genetic component. Twin studies show substantially higher concordance in monozygotic (about 32.5%) than dizygotic (about 2.9%) twins. Wynne-Davies showed that the occurrence rate in first-degree relatives is 17 times higher than the general population, in second-degree relatives 6 times higher, and approaches the normal population rate in third-degree relatives. Unaffected parents with an affected son have a 1-in-40 (2.5%) chance for a subsequent son; unaffected parents with an affected daughter face a 1-in-16 (6.5%) chance for a subsequent son and 1-in-40 (2.5%) for a subsequent daughter. When one parent and one child are affected, the risk for a subsequent child rises to approximately 1 in 4.

Despite this clear genetic contribution, no single major causative gene has been identified. The inheritance pattern is consistent with a multifactorial polygenic model with environmental contributions. Modern molecular-genetic work has identified variants in the **PITX1–TBX4–HOX** transcriptional pathway (Dobbs and Gurnett, 2012). Carriers of these mutations show structural MRI findings — lateral muscle hypoplasia, increased perimysial fat, and vascular anomalies — that may explain some of the anatomical features of the severe clubfoot.

Several environmental and obstetric factors are associated with increased risk: maternal smoking during pregnancy (especially combined with positive family history — Skelly et al., Honein et al.), solvent exposure (paint thinner and glue remover exposure, OR 1.66 in the Norwegian cohort of Dodwell et al.), oligohydramnios and intrauterine packing, amniocentesis, maternal diabetes, and intrauterine constraint. The interplay between genetic predisposition and intrauterine environment is a central unifying hypothesis.

Associated conditions are important both for diagnosis and prognosis:

- **Myelomeningocele** — clubfoot occurs in approximately 25–50% of patients and is characteristically severe and rigid.
- **Arthrogryposis multiplex congenita** — clubfoot is severe, bilateral, and often resistant to standard casting.
- **Distal arthrogryposis** — variant with characteristic finger and limb contractures and foot involvement.
- **Larsen syndrome** — multiple joint dislocations with foot involvement.
- **Amniotic band syndrome (Streeter dysplasia)** — constrictive rings causing secondary clubfoot.
- **Various chromosomal disorders** (trisomies, deletions).

The systematic newborn examination of a child with clubfoot must include careful spinal examination (for spinal dysraphism and cutaneous markers), full neurological examination (with particular attention to peroneal nerve function — reported deficient or absent in 5–7% of otherwise idiopathic clubfeet by Edmond and Frick), and assessment of all four limbs, face, and thumbs (adducted, contracted thumbs are a marker for arthrogryposis).

Peroneal nerve weakness deserves separate attention. In the randomized multicenter trial of Dobbs et al. on bracing duration in idiopathic clubfoot, of 139 participants, 7% had peroneal nerve palsy and 42% had diminished function. These children have significantly higher treatment requirements — more casts, earlier tenotomy, and nearly 50% relapse rate. Clinically, a "drop toe sign" of the hallux and lack of toe and ankle dorsiflexion should be sought.

## Pathological anatomy

The clubfoot deformity is fundamentally a deformity of the talocalcaneonavicular and talocalcaneal joints, with secondary changes in the ankle, midfoot, and forefoot. Ponseti, based on dissection and anatomical studies, emphasized that the talus is the "keystone" of the deformity:

- **Talus**: The talar neck is medially deviated and plantarflexed relative to the body. The head of the talus is uncovered laterally and rotated medially; on passive examination it is palpable as a solid point over the dorsolateral aspect of the midfoot and serves as the fulcrum during Ponseti casting.
- **Calcaneus**: Rotated medially under the talus (subtalar inversion), positioned in equinus, and uncovered laterally. The talocalcaneal axis is therefore medially rotated.
- **Navicular**: Dislocated medially onto the medial aspect of the talar head. The talonavicular joint is severely incongruent — the navicular tuberosity may lie close to the medial malleolus.
- **Cuboid**: Adducted and medially subluxed on the calcaneus.
- **Forefoot**: Adducted and supinated relative to the hindfoot, with metatarsus adductus and inversion of the midfoot. Cavus is the result of plantarflexion of the forefoot on the hindfoot, not true "arch" elevation.
- **Lower leg**: Internal tibial torsion in most cases, with the bimalleolar axis rotated internally relative to the proximal tibia.

The soft tissues show characteristic contractures that determine the correction sequence in the Ponseti method:

- **Posterior compartment**: Achilles tendon and posterior ankle capsule are shortened and rigid — they produce equinus and are the last barrier to full correction.
- **Medial compartment**: Posterior tibial tendon; flexor digitorum longus; flexor hallucis longus; tibialis anterior; deltoid ligament and spring (calcaneonavicular) ligament complex. These resist correction of the medial displacement of the navicular and of the abduction of the forefoot.
- **Lateral compartment**: The calcaneofibular ligament is shortened and resists correction of equinus; the peronei are relatively stretched and weak.
- **Plantar surface**: Plantar fascia and plantar intrinsic muscles are shortened and produce the cavus.

The bones of the foot show secondary changes that deepen with growth in the untreated foot: the talar head deforms with a flat medial contour; the calcaneus develops a wedge shape; the cuboid deforms; the first metatarsal may become plantarflexed. These changes are important to understand why early treatment is critical — soft-tissue elasticity and cartilage plasticity allow remodeling only in the infant.

Understanding of the pathological anatomy — particularly through the work of Ponseti, Pirani, Cooper and Howard, and Howard and Benson — is the foundation of the Ponseti method, which addresses the components of the deformity in a specific sequence based on the anatomical relationships, not on the surface-visible distribution.

## Diagnosis, imaging, and differential diagnosis

The diagnosis of clubfoot is clinical and is made at birth. Prenatal ultrasound (usually around 18–22 weeks gestation) has sensitivity 60–90% and specificity over 95%, but is known for false-positive findings in early pregnancy. When clubfoot is diagnosed prenatally, referral to a specialist and discussion of the risk of associated anomalies (especially spinal dysraphism and chromosomal disorders) are recommended.

In the newborn, the classical presentation is unmistakably recognizable: a small, shortened foot with a deep medial crease across the midfoot and a posterior crease over the heel (Figure 28-22 in Lovell & Winter); a single (rarely double) deep posterior crease; an empty heel — the calcaneus cannot be palpated beneath the heel skin because of subtalar inversion and the fibrofatty pad; and a characteristic position with supinated forefoot and the foot as a whole rotated downward. The foot and calf are smaller than the unaffected side.

Imaging in the newborn is not generally required. Plain radiographs are limited by the fact that in the infant the tarsal bones are largely cartilaginous and not visualized. When radiographs are taken — usually for assessment of severe deformity or in older children with residual deformity — an AP view in a simulated weight-bearing position with maximal ankle dorsiflexion and a lateral view in maximal dorsiflexion are obtained. The classical parameters are:

- **Talocalcaneal angle (Kite angle) on AP view**: Normal 20–40°; clubfoot <20° due to parallelism of talus and calcaneus in subtalar inversion.
- **Talocalcaneal angle on lateral view**: Normal 35–50°; clubfoot <35° with the same parallelism.
- **Talo-first-metatarsal axis on AP view**: Normal 0–20° (forefoot mildly abducted relative to talus); clubfoot significantly negative (forefoot adducted).
- **Tibiocalcaneal angle on lateral view**: Measure of residual equinus.

MRI is not used routinely in idiopathic clubfoot but is useful in the severe, rigid, or recurrent foot for assessing soft-tissue contractures, muscle hypoplasia, and to rule out tarsal coalition in the older child.

The differential diagnosis of an apparent clubfoot at birth includes:

- **Postural equinovarus**: A positional deformity that is fully passively correctable to a neutral position. The distinction is critically important and is made by careful passive manipulation. Postural equinovarus resolves spontaneously over weeks with stretching, and if needed one or two casts.
- **Metatarsus adductus**: An isolated forefoot deformity with normal hindfoot and no equinus. Classified by reducibility: flexible (passively correctable past the heel bisector), partially reducible (correctable to the heel bisector but not past it), or rigid (not correctable to the heel bisector).
- **Skewfoot (Z-foot, serpentine foot)**: A complex deformity combining metatarsus adductus, hindfoot valgus, and midfoot abduction. Requires individualized management.
- **Congenital vertical talus**: A rigid planovalgus deformity with dorsally dislocated navicular — the inverse of clubfoot. The classical finding is a "rocker-bottom" or "Persian-slipper" foot with fixed dorsiflexion of the midfoot and plantarflexion of the hindfoot.
- **Tarsal coalition**: Does not present in the newborn; presents later in the child or adolescent with a rigid planovalgus foot.

Systematically, every clubfoot warrants exclusion of syndromic associations (see Epidemiology and etiology) with a full somatic and neurological examination, assessment of spinal markers (hemangiomas, dermal sinuses, hair tufts, pigmentations over the lumbosacral region) and, if indicated, spinal ultrasound or MRI.

## Classification

Accurate classification of initial severity has three purposes: (1) to guide treatment decisions; (2) to allow comparison across series and centers; and (3) to provide a prognostic instrument for discussion with parents. Two systems dominate contemporary practice — Dimeglio and Pirani.

### Dimeglio classification

The Dimeglio classification (Dimeglio et al., 1995) scores four principal parameters, each from 0 (no deformity) to 4 (severe deformity):

- **Equinus** in the sagittal plane
- **Hindfoot varus** in the coronal plane
- **Derotation of the foot block** (talocalcaneal axis rotation) in the horizontal plane
- **Forefoot adduction** in the horizontal plane

To these 16 points, 4 modifiers of 1 point each are added:

- Posterior crease
- Medial crease
- Cavus
- Poor muscle condition (atrophy)

The total score is 0–20, divided into four grades: Grade I (mild, score <5), Grade II (moderate, score 5–10), Grade III (severe, score 10–15), and Grade IV (very severe, score >15). The classification is reproducible, correlates with treatment response, and was validated as the most reliable in the comparative study of Wainwright et al.

### Pirani score

The Pirani score (Pirani, Outerbridge et al., 1995; validated 1999), simpler and more widely used in Ponseti-method practice, scores six features distributed three to the midfoot (Midfoot Contracture Score, MFCS) and three to the hindfoot (Hindfoot Contracture Score, HFCS):

Midfoot signs (MFCS, 0–3 total):

- **Curved lateral border** of the foot (CLB)
- **Medial crease** (MC)
- **Lateral head of talus palpable** (LHT)

Hindfoot signs (HFCS, 0–3 total):

- **Posterior crease** (PC)
- **Empty heel** (EH)
- **Rigid equinus** (RE)

Each of the six signs is scored 0 (normal), 0.5 (moderately abnormal), or 1 (severely abnormal). The total ranges from 0 (no deformity) to 6 (most severe).

The Pirani score is a valid tool for initial assessment, for tracking progress during serial Ponseti casting, and for predicting the need for tenotomy. An empirical rule cited by Dyer and Davis: HFCS ≥1 before tenotomy predicts the need for tenotomy; the rate of fall in the total Pirani score is a powerful predictor of outcome — feet that do not show the expected rate of fall (approximately 1 point per cast) identify cases with a higher risk of relapse or incomplete correction.

### Other classifications

The Catterall, the early Ponseti–Smoley, and the Harrold–Walker classifications are described but have limited applicability in contemporary practice due to their lower reproducibility.

## The Ponseti method

### Principles

The Ponseti method, developed by Ignacio Ponseti at the University of Iowa in the 1940s–1960s and popularized globally from the late 1990s, is based on serial casting that addresses the components of the clubfoot deformity in a specific sequence. The order of correction reflects the anatomical relationships of the deformity:

1. **Cavus is corrected first** — by supinating the forefoot relative to the hindfoot (apparently paradoxical: supination is initially deepened, to align the metatarsal axes and correct the plantarflexion of the first metatarsal relative to the heel).
2. **Adduction, varus, and supination are corrected simultaneously** — by abducting the forefoot under the talar head as the fulcrum. This is Ponseti's central insight: the calcaneus rotates externally beneath the talus as the foot is abducted, simultaneously correcting heel varus and forefoot adductus. Attempting separate correction of heel varus by direct eversion is an error and leads to "rocker-bottom" deformity.
3. **Equinus is corrected last** — usually by percutaneous Achilles tenotomy in approximately 80–90% of cases, followed by serial casting in dorsiflexion.

The talus is the central fulcrum of the whole technique. The talar head, visible and palpable over the dorsolateral aspect of the midfoot, is used as the fixing landmark around which the rest of the foot rotates. Pressure is applied here — at the talar head — not on the calcaneus and not on the cuboid. Attempting to "push" the calcaneus into eversion is unphysiological and counterproductive.

### Serial casting technique

Treatment is initiated as soon as possible after birth, ideally in the first week and certainly within the first 6–8 weeks while the soft tissues remain maximally pliable. Delay beyond 9 months significantly reduces the efficacy of conservative treatment because of bony remodeling and soft-tissue stiffening, although Lourenço and Morcuende have shown success of the method in the older child (up to 2–3 years) with some modifications.

**Cast 1 — Cavus correction:**

The forefoot is supinated relative to the hindfoot to align the metatarsals and correct the cavus. This is counterintuitive to the beginner — apparently "deepening" the supination — but the logic is as follows: in clubfoot the first metatarsal is plantarflexed relative to the heel, which with pronation of the forefoot creates a rigid cavus. By supinating the forefoot (dorsiflexing the first metatarsal relative to the heel), the metatarsal axes are aligned and the foot is ready for sequential abduction. Caution: the forefoot is not pronated and the existing supination is not worsened — only the first metatarsal is aligned.

**Casts 2–5 (sometimes up to 7) — Sequential abduction:**

Each subsequent cast abducts the forefoot approximately 10–20° relative to the previous, with counterpressure applied at the head of the talus as the fulcrum. The casts are above-knee long-leg casts in 90° of knee flexion — critical for preventing slippage along the leg (especially in the smaller and sweaty infant), maintaining hip and knee position, and fixing rotation. The cast is changed weekly; before applying the new cast, the old is removed and a re-evaluation and photograph or repeat Pirani scoring is performed when feasible. Typical total number of casts is 5–7. The end-point of abduction is approximately 60–70° of forefoot abduction relative to the lower leg, with the calcaneus externally rotated to a neutral position and the possibility of passive ankle dorsiflexion.

The cast is made of polyester (synthetic) or plaster material; plaster bandage remains preferred in many centers for better molding and pressure distribution, despite the convenience of synthetic bandages. Appropriate padding is critical to avoid pressure sores.

**Percutaneous Achilles tenotomy:**

Once the foot is fully abducted and the heel can be brought to a neutral position, percutaneous Achilles tenotomy is performed in approximately 80–90% of cases to address the residual equinus. The decision is made at HFCS ≥1 or clinically — inability to dorsiflex above 10–15° after full abduction.

The technique is a clean transverse cut of the tendon, performed under local anesthesia in the office setting in many programs (or under light sedation in others). A small blade (#11, with the blunt side toward the Achilles) or a specialized tenotomy knife is inserted percutaneously above the calcaneus, approximately 1–1.5 cm proximal to the calcaneal insertion, and the tendon is cleanly transected transversely. A "pop" sensation or sudden give is the signal of complete transection. Immediately after tenotomy the foot can be dorsiflexed significantly past neutral.

The Achilles tendon regenerates over about 3 weeks while the foot is maintained in dorsiflexion in a final cast. This "post-tenotomy" cast is worn for 3 weeks. Complications of tenotomy are rare with standard technique: bleeding from the posterior tibial artery (more common with inaccurate lateral positioning of the blade — Ponseti therefore recommends a strictly medial approach), injury to the posterior tibial nerve, and incomplete transection (requiring repetition).

### Foot-abduction bracing

After completion of casting and tenotomy, **foot-abduction bracing is essential to prevent recurrence** and is the single most important determinant of long-term success of the method.

The standard brace is the Denis Browne bar — a rigid bar connecting two specialized shoes or foot pieces, fixing the feet in dorsiflexion and external rotation:

- **60–70° external rotation on the affected side**
- **30–40° on the unaffected side** (in unilateral cases)
- **Dorsiflexion 10–15°** at the ankle
- **Bar length** = distance between the child's shoulders (to allow breastfeeding and normal hip motion)

Alternative braces include the Mitchell brace and in recent years standardized products such as the "MD" brace and analogues, developed on Denis Browne principles but with improved adaptability and comfort.

Regimen:

- **First 3 months**: 23 hours per day (only 1 hour free for hygiene and feeding).
- **After 3 months until approximately 4 years of age**: at night and during naps — 12–14 hours per day.

Compliance with bracing is the single most important determinant of long-term success. **Discontinuation of the brace before age 4 produces recurrence rates of 80–100%**, while consistent brace use through age 4 produces recurrence rates of 10–20%. Parental education on this rule is critically important and should begin even before the first cast. Morris, Dresden, Lourenço, and others have shown that parent-education programs — with verbal explanations, written material, video, and regular follow-up visits — raise compliance from ~40% to over 80% and proportionately reduce relapse rates.

### Outcomes

The Ponseti method, when applied correctly, produces excellent foot morphology and function in 95–98% of idiopathic clubfeet. Long-term follow-up by Ponseti's own group (Cooper and Dietz, 1995; Dobbs et al., 2006; Zionts et al., 2010) extending into the fifth and sixth decades of life has shown sustained excellent results in the great majority of patients, with feet that look slightly smaller than normal but are functional, well-tolerated in normal shoe wear, and free of significant late deformity.

Compared with traditional extensive soft-tissue releases (Turco and contemporaries), the Ponseti method produces:

- Better functional outcomes by various assessment instruments (Functional Rating System of Laaveg and Ponseti).
- Greater range of motion at the ankle and subtalar joint.
- Less rocker-bottom deformity.
- Significantly lower rates of foot pain and arthritic changes in adulthood.
- Comparable or better functional outcomes with significantly less surgical intervention.

The method is now the global standard of care for idiopathic clubfoot and has been adopted by the World Health Organization as the preferred approach in resource-limited settings.

## Surgery for recurrence and residual deformity

Despite the success of the Ponseti method, a minority of patients develop recurrence or residual deformity that requires surgical management. Recurrence is most commonly the consequence of inadequate brace compliance and typically presents with recurrent supination and inversion of the forefoot, sometimes with recurrent equinus. Other less common causes of recurrence include severe initial disease, peroneal weakness, syndromic association, and incomplete primary correction. Treatment principles are stage- and age-dependent.

### Recasting and repeat tenotomy

Mild to moderate recurrence, particularly in the younger child (under 2–3 years), often responds to repeat Ponseti casting and, if needed, repeat Achilles tenotomy. The principles of the original method apply, with careful sequential correction of the components. Sometimes only 2–4 casts are required to restore correction.

### Anterior tibialis tendon transfer

Transfer of the anterior tibialis tendon to the lateral cuneiform (or third cuneiform) is the standard surgical procedure for the **dynamic supination deformity** that emerges after the age of 2–3 years. The procedure addresses the muscular imbalance between the medially deviating (inverting) anterior tibialis and the relatively weak lateral foot evertors (peronei). By transferring the tendon laterally, the inversion force is converted to an eversion force, correcting the dynamic component of supination.

Technique (Garceau and Manuel, modified):

- A small incision over the tendon at its insertion into the medial cuneiform and base of the first metatarsal.
- The tendon is released, dissected proximally, and brought distally through a new channel into the lateral cuneiform.
- Fixation with a "pull-out" suture over a button on the sole (Bunnell technique), with an interference screw, or with a bow suture into the cuboid.
- The foot is held in a slightly overcorrected position for 6 weeks in a short-leg cast, followed by a brace for 2 months.

The procedure is highly successful in dynamic supination deformity, with long-term improvement in foot morphology and function. Caveat: **the procedure must be deferred until the lateral cuneiform is ossified** (typically age 3 years or older). In smaller children, transfer to the cuboid is an alternative.

### Posteromedial release (PMR)

The traditional comprehensive posteromedial release surgery (PMR, popularly known as the "Turco release"), the dominant treatment of clubfoot before the global adoption of the Ponseti method, includes:

- Lengthening of the Achilles tendon (Z-plasty).
- Posterior ankle joint capsulotomy.
- Posterior subtalar joint capsulotomy.
- Lengthening of the posterior tibial tendon.
- Lengthening of flexor digitorum longus and flexor hallucis longus.
- Release of the spring (calcaneonavicular) ligament.
- Release of the deltoid ligament.
- Release of the calcaneofibular ligament.
- Talonavicular joint capsulotomy with reduction of the navicular.
- Calcaneocuboid joint capsulotomy.
- As needed, release of the plantar fascia (plantar fasciotomy) and plantar muscles for cavus correction.

The procedure achieves dramatic initial correction but at the cost of a stiff, painful foot with a high rate of late complications: overcorrection and planovalgus, secondary arthritis, chronic stiffness and pain, and impaired foot development. Long-term studies (Aronson et al., Dobbs et al., Karol et al.) show pain rates of over 30% in adults who underwent PMR as children, with significant functional limitations.

The procedure is now reserved for the severe rigid clubfoot that fails Ponseti management, with results substantially better when used as salvage after Ponseti (with prior soft-tissue stretching) than as primary treatment. A "limited" or "à la carte" posteromedial release — performing only the components necessary as identified from the individual pathology — is the modern approach.

### Bony procedures in the older child

For the older child or adolescent with residual or recurrent deformity, bony procedures complement or supplant soft-tissue releases. Options include:

- **Lateral column shortening** — Lichtblau osteotomy (distal calcaneal resection) or Evans osteotomy (opening osteotomy of the lateral column).
- **Medial column lengthening** — opening osteotomy of the cuneiform or navicular.
- **Dorsiflexion osteotomy of the first metatarsal** — for residual cavus.
- **Calcaneal osteotomy** — Dwyer (medial closing wedge) for heel varus; lateral closing wedge for overcorrected valgus.
- **Supramalleolar tibial osteotomy** — for residual internal tibial torsion (indicated for torsion difference >20° from the opposite side and functional complaints).

In the severely deformed adolescent foot, **triple arthrodesis** (of the talonavicular, calcaneocuboid, and subtalar joints) remains the salvage procedure of choice, achieving a plantigrade, stable foot at the cost of stiffness. It is typically performed after age 12 to avoid disturbance of foot growth.

### Distraction techniques

External fixation and circular distraction (Ilizarov apparatus) have been applied to the severely rigid clubfoot in the older child and for residual deformities in nonsurgical candidates. The principles are progressive distraction and gradual repositioning of soft tissues and bone into a new configuration, followed by consolidation. Results are variable; complications — pin-track infections, toe contractures, recurrence — are significant. The technique is reserved for selected complex cases, especially the syndromic foot.

## The syndromic and complex clubfoot

### Clubfoot in myelomeningocele

Clubfoot occurs in approximately 25–50% of children with myelomeningocele and is typically severe and rigid with poor muscle balance. The deformity persists or recurs after standard Ponseti treatment because of the underlying neurological imbalance and the absence of normal muscle function.

Treatment combines:

- **Initial Ponseti casting** — often achieving an acceptable position although with less complete correction than in idiopathic clubfoot.
- **Percutaneous Achilles tenotomy** — frequently necessary and may need to be repeated.
- **Surgical correction for failure** — typically requiring more extensive surgery than the idiopathic case.
- **Talectomy** — surgical excision of the talus — has been used as a salvage procedure for the severely deformed, insensate foot in myelomeningocele. It produces a flatfoot with no plantigrade weight-bearing surface but a foot that can be braced for ambulation.

Sharrard, in his classic work (*Paediatric Orthopaedics and Fractures*, 3rd ed., 1993), emphasizes that the aim in the myelomeningocele clubfoot is not a "normal" foot but a plantigrade, painless foot suitable for shoes and brace-supported ambulation.

### Clubfoot in arthrogryposis

Clubfoot in arthrogryposis multiplex congenita is characteristically severe, bilateral, and resistant to Ponseti management. The foot is stiffer than the idiopathic, with soft-tissue structures less amenable to stretching. Long-term results of the standard method are less predictable.

**The Dobbs modification of the Ponseti method** — adjusting the technique for the stiffer arthrogrypotic foot (a longer casting period, more casts, more aggressive abduction, earlier and frequently repeated tenotomy) and combining it with selected soft-tissue procedures — has produced improved outcomes with over 70% correction in initial Dobbs and Morcuende series.

Many patients ultimately require more extensive surgical correction, and the orthopedic surgeon must coordinate care with the broader management of the arthrogryposis (including the upper extremities, spine, and hips).

### Distal arthrogryposis, Larsen syndrome, and amniotic bands

Distal arthrogryposis, Larsen syndrome, and amniotic band syndrome (Streeter dysplasia) produce clubfoot with specific features. Distal arthrogryposis is an autosomal dominant condition with characteristic finger and foot involvement; the foot is rigid but usually responds reasonably to modified Ponseti casting.

Larsen syndrome is associated with multiple joint dislocations and requires coordinated treatment of the joints in sequence (usually hips first, followed by knees and feet).

Amniotic band syndrome requires assessment of the viability of the foot — constrictive bands may produce secondary lymphedema and circulatory compromise. Treatment combines Z-plasty release of the amniotic rings and standard correction of the foot deformity.

The key principle in all these syndromic forms is recognition of the syndromic association so that the clubfoot can be treated in the context of the broader condition and so that associated anomalies are identified and managed.

## Outcomes, long-term sequelae, and transition to adult care

The long-term outcomes of clubfoot treatment depend on the severity of the initial deformity, the adequacy of initial treatment, and brace compliance. Excellent functional outcomes are achievable for the great majority of idiopathic clubfeet treated by the Ponseti method, with feet that are slightly smaller, slightly stiffer, and slightly weaker than the normal contralateral side but that function well in normal activities, sports, and occupational demands.

Classical late sequelae include:

- **Mild residual internal tibial torsion** — rarely requiring correction; usually clinically insignificant in the adult patient unless associated with footwear or functional complaints.
- **Mild residual forefoot supination** — often correctable by tibialis anterior tendon transfer if it presents as a dynamic component with in-toeing during gait.
- **Residual cavus** — occasionally requiring dorsiflexion osteotomy of the first metatarsal.
- **Overcorrection producing planovalgus** — much less common with Ponseti management than with traditional extensive releases, but described with overaggressive casting, inappropriate tenotomy, or in syndromic cases.
- **Foot size** — slightly smaller, with average shoe-size differences of half a size or less.
- **Limb length** — Little and Aiona report a difference >0.5 cm in 18% of unilateral cases; Spiegel and Loder in 32 of 47 cases in a smaller series.

Long-term ankle motion is slightly less than the normal contralateral side but is generally adequate for full function. The studies of Cooper and Dietz (1995) on 45 patients followed to age 35 show that patients treated with the Ponseti method have significantly better outcomes by objective and subjective measures than patients who underwent primary extensive surgery.

Transition from pediatric to adult orthopedics is essential. Adult patients with a history of clubfoot occasionally present with metatarsalgia, midfoot pain, restricted ankle motion, or progression of cavus. Management is symptomatic: orthotic inserts, modified footwear, subtalar or ankle joint injections, and for significant functional complaints — selective arthrodesis or dorsiflexion osteotomy.

## Local contributions (alphabetical by country)

The historical contribution to clubfoot management has a rich international dimension, reflecting the global spread of the Ponseti method after the 1990s and the preceding work in various schools.

**Bulgaria.** In Bulgarian pediatric orthopedics, the Ponseti method was adopted progressively from the early 2000s, with targeted training in specialized centers and gradual replacement of the earlier routine posteromedial release. The Bulgarian orthopedic community has contributed to national initiatives for standardizing infant treatment and for parental education on brace compliance. The older Bulgarian textbooks (including those of Boychev) described the surgical methods that dominated mid-twentieth-century practice.

**Canada.** Pirani developed the eponymous score. Wright and others published large follow-up studies. Cincinnati Children's and Canadian institutions were among the first in North America to adopt the Ponseti method systematically in the late 1990s.

**France.** Dimeglio developed the eponymous classification and was among the first in Europe to systematically adopt the Ponseti method.

**Guatemala, Malawi, Uganda, and other low- and middle-income countries.** The work of Penny and Lavy in Uganda and of Lavy in Malawi demonstrated the applicability of the Ponseti method in resource-limited settings and led to the establishment of national training and follow-up programs. Honig, Iltis, and Staheli, through the Global Help Organization, published the free online manual that made the Ponseti method accessible in over 100 countries.

**Italy.** Although Dimeglio is French in origin, his classification has been widely adopted in Italy. The Hispano-Italian school of Bensahel et al. contributed significantly to early documentation of Ponseti-method outcomes.

**Switzerland.** Hefti and Brunner in Basel published follow-up series with long-term outcomes that confirmed the method in European settings.

**United Kingdom.** Pirani (Canadian, trained partly in the United Kingdom) developed the eponymous score for severity and progress assessment. Catterall and other classical British orthopedists described classification systems and management approaches that shaped contemporary practice.

**United States.** Ignacio Ponseti, a Spanish-American émigré, developed and established the method at the University of Iowa from 1948 to the end of his life. Cooper and Dietz published the key long-term follow-up. Morcuende, Dobbs, Mosca, and Herring shaped contemporary practice. Mosca, in *Principles and Management of Pediatric Foot and Ankle Deformities*, systematized the modern approach to the complex foot.

## Take-home points and practical pearls

Congenital talipes equinovarus is one of the commonest congenital musculoskeletal anomalies and one of the great success stories of modern pediatric orthopedics. The classical deformity — cavus, adductus, varus, equinus (CAVE) with internal tibial torsion — reflects a fundamental abnormality of the talocalcaneonavicular and talocalcaneal joints with secondary soft-tissue and bony changes. Accurate understanding of the pathological anatomy — with the talus as the keystone and fulcrum — determines success or failure of any treatment.

The Ponseti method of serial casting, percutaneous Achilles tenotomy, and prolonged foot-abduction bracing has displaced the extensive surgical releases of mid-twentieth-century practice and produces excellent outcomes in 95–98% of idiopathic clubfeet. The principles of the method — cavus correction first, sequential abduction with the talar head as the fulcrum, tenotomy for residual equinus, and prolonged bracing for prevention of recurrence — are within the scope of every pediatric orthopedic practice.

Surgical management is reserved for failure or recurrence of Ponseti management, with anterior tibialis tendon transfer as the principal procedure for dynamic supination deformity in the older child and à la carte posteromedial release for the rigid, casting-unresponsive foot.

The syndromic clubfoot — particularly in myelomeningocele and arthrogryposis — is more resistant to Ponseti management and often requires more extensive surgical correction or method modification (the Dobbs modification).

Key practical messages:

- Begin Ponseti casting as soon as possible after birth — ideally in the first week.
- Parental education on the importance of brace compliance is the single most important intervention for long-term success.
- A complete neurological and spinal examination is essential in every clubfoot to exclude syndromic association.
- The Pirani (or Dimeglio) score is used at initial assessment and to track progress.
- Monitor closely for relapse through age 7 and explain to parents that repeat tenotomy may be needed.
- Reserve extensive surgery for failure of conservative management; the à la carte approach is better than routine full PMR.
- Recognize and treat associated anomalies in syndromic cases in coordination with other specialists.
