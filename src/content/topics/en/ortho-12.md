## Introduction

Congenital anomalies of the hand and upper limb encompass a heterogeneous group of disorders arising from disturbances of embryonic limb development between approximately the fourth and eighth gestational weeks. The conditions range from minor cosmetic anomalies requiring no treatment, through syndromic disorders associated with cardiac, renal, and hematologic anomalies, to severe deficiencies profoundly affecting function. The orthopaedic surgeon engaged in pediatric hand surgery must combine an understanding of embryonic development, a working knowledge of classification systems, the ability to recognize syndromic associations, and a thoughtful approach to the timing and selection of reconstructive procedures.

This topic synthesizes content from Green's Operative Hand Surgery (6th edition, edited by Wolfe, Hotchkiss, Pederson, Kozin), Buck-Gramcko's *Congenital Malformations of the Hand and Forearm*, Rayan & Upton's *Congenital Hand Anomalies and Associated Syndromes*, Waters & Bae's *Pediatric Hand and Upper Limb Surgery*, and Tachdjian's *Pediatric Orthopaedics* edited by Herring, supplemented by Apley & Solomon's and Netter's Concise Orthopaedic Anatomy.

## Upper Limb Embryology

### Developmental window and signaling centers

Limb development begins around day 26 of gestation with the appearance of the limb bud — a thickening of mesenchyme covered by ectoderm. By the end of the eighth week the morphology of the hand has assumed the form with which it will present at birth; all structural anomalies — failures of formation, differentiation, duplication — arise within this narrow early window, often before the woman knows she is pregnant. Three signaling centers control limb patterning: the **apical ectodermal ridge (AER)** secretes fibroblast growth factors (FGF8, FGF4) and controls proximodistal growth — its disturbance produces transverse and longitudinal deficiencies; the **zone of polarizing activity (ZPA)** secretes Sonic hedgehog (SHH) and controls anteroposterior (radioulnar) patterning — disturbances produce preaxial polydactyly (overexpression) or ulnar deficiencies (deficit); the **dorsal ectoderm** controls dorsoventral patterning through Wnt7a and Lmx1b — disturbances produce nail and palm anomalies (nail-patella syndrome). Digits individualize between weeks 6 and 8 by BMP-mediated apoptosis of interdigital mesenchyme; failure of this apoptosis produces syndactyly.

### Molecular pathology

Proximal Wassel duplications (Wassel VII, triphalangeal thumb) associate with TBX5 mutations (Holt-Oram). Hox gene expression (Hoxd9-13) specifies individual digits; Hoxa13 disturbances produce hand-foot-genital syndrome. Epidemiologic studies increasingly link "idiopathic" anomalies to maternal factors: diabetes (including gestational), drug exposure (valproate, thalidomide, warfarin), smoking, and chorioamnionitis.

## Classifications

### Swanson / IFSSH classification

The classical Swanson classification, adopted by the IFSSH in 1976, divides upper limb anomalies into seven categories: I — failure of formation (transverse and longitudinal deficiencies, phocomelia); II — failure of differentiation (syndactyly, camptodactyly, clinodactyly, radioulnar synostosis, symphalangism); III — duplication (polydactyly, mirror hand); IV — overgrowth (macrodactyly); V — undergrowth (thumb hypoplasia, brachydactyly); VI — congenital constriction band syndrome; VII — generalized skeletal abnormalities. The system has the advantages of simplicity and wide familiarity, but mixes descriptive and etiologic criteria and does not reflect the discoveries of modern developmental biology.

### Oberg–Manske–Tonkin (OMT) classification

The OMT classification, adopted by the IFSSH in 2010 (refinements 2014 and 2020), organizes anomalies by developmental axis: malformations (disorders of the entire limb, of the limb axis — proximodistal, anteroposterior, dorsoventral — and of the digits); deformations (amniotic bands, trigger thumb); dysplasias (macrodactyly, vascular malformations); and syndromic anomalies. It is the official IFSSH classification and is recommended for new publications, but has not displaced Swanson in clinical practice. This topic uses the Swanson framework for didactic convenience.

### Principles of clinical recognition

The clinical approach follows three steps: (1) accurate description of the anatomic components and extent of involvement (unilateral/bilateral, contralateral limb, spine, head, trunk); (2) categorization into Swanson and OMT category; (3) systematic exclusion of associated syndromes — every child with a major upper limb anomaly requires cardiac echocardiography, complete blood count, renal ultrasound, and genetic analysis where indicated. Radial deficiencies specifically require a Fanconi anemia test (chromosomal breakage analysis with diepoxybutane) because of the severe hematologic consequences of late diagnosis.

## Failure of Formation (Swanson I)

Failures of formation are characterized by absent or hypoplastic skeletal elements and may be transverse (perpendicular to the long axis of the limb) or longitudinal (along the limb axis).

### Transverse deficiencies

The transverse deficiency is an amputation-like absence of the distal limb. The congenital transverse deficiency is typically unilateral, sporadic, and not associated with other anomalies; incidence is approximately 1 in 20 000 live births. The level varies from the proximal arm to the digits; the most common is the proximal forearm. Symbrachydactyly is a special case of "transverse" deficiency with short digit nubbins and frequently associated absence of the pectoralis major (Poland syndrome) — the result of a vascular event (subclavian artery disruption) rather than a true AER failure.

Treatment depends on level. Prosthetics is the mainstay, with the upper-limb prosthesis typically fitted at six months of age when the child develops bimanual function. Many children with transverse deficiencies become highly functional without prostheses, especially those with bilateral involvement who develop remarkable compensatory skills. Surgical reconstruction is rarely needed; options include toe-to-hand transfer in symbrachydactyly with absent digits or distraction lengthening in severe subsegmental deficiency.

### Radial longitudinal deficiency (radial club hand)

Radial longitudinal deficiency — radial club hand or radial dysplasia — is a longitudinal deficiency of the preaxial side of the forearm and hand. The disorder is among the most clinically significant congenital anomalies because of the severity of presentation and the strong association with hematologic and cardiac syndromes. Incidence is approximately 1 in 30 000–100 000 live births; bilateral involvement occurs in about 50%, especially in syndromic forms. The Bayne and Klug classification (1987) distinguishes four types: Type I — short radius; Type II — hypoplastic radius; Type III — partial absence; Type IV — complete absence of the radius (the most common type). James added Type 0 (thumb hypoplasia only) and Type N. Associated thumb hypoplasia is the rule, classified by Blauth.

The systemic associations are the reason every child with a radial deficiency requires systematic evaluation — in 30–60% of cases a systemic condition is found: **VATER/VACTERL** (vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal, limb); **Holt-Oram** (heart-hand syndrome, TBX5 mutations, autosomal dominant); **Fanconi anemia** (autosomal recessive, FANC genes, progressive bone marrow failure and malignancy risk — requires hematopoietic stem cell transplant for survival; chromosomal breakage analysis with diepoxybutane is mandatory in every radial deficiency, even without hematologic findings); **TAR** (thrombocytopenia–absent radius, bilateral deficiency with the thumb PRESERVED — a key distinction from Fanconi); **chromosomal anomalies** (trisomy 13, 18, deletion 4p).

Treatment begins with passive stretching and splinting in the neonatal period. Surgical treatment depends on type: **centralization** of the wrist on the ulna (Manske) — the historical procedure — reorients the wrist but shows long-term recurrence (50–80%) and loss of motion; **radialization** (Buck-Gramcko) shifts the wrist laterally without classical centralization and preserves motion; **distraction frame** is used for preoperative soft-tissue stretching; **vascularized microvascular transfer** of the second MTP from the foot (Vilkki) stabilizes the wrist with a mobile joint. Thumb hypoplasia is addressed according to Blauth (see corresponding section): in severe forms **pollicization of the index** (Buck-Gramcko) is the standard; alternative — toe-to-thumb transfer. Care requires multidisciplinary evaluation.

### Ulnar longitudinal deficiency

Ulnar deficiency is much rarer than radial (1 in 100 000–200 000), unilateral in ~70%, and less commonly associated with systemic syndromes. The Bayne classification distinguishes four types: I — ulnar hypoplasia; II — partial absence with a fibrocartilaginous anlage; III — complete absence; IV — radioulnar synostosis with ulnar hypoplasia. Cole and Manske proposed a modification that incorporates hand status (postaxial digits, syndactyly, thumb deficiency). Associated anomalies include ulnar-sided syndactyly, hypoplasia or absence of the 4th–5th digits, and an unstable or fused elbow. The classical principle (Riordan) is excision of the unstable fibrocartilaginous ulnar anlage in early infancy — the anlage tethers normal elbow and forearm development and produces progressive radial bowing. Additional procedures — corrective radial osteotomy, thumb reconstruction, syndactyly release. Type IV (synostosis) has the least satisfactory outcomes.

### Cleft hand and symbrachydactyly

The "cleft hand" (ectrodactyly) describes a hand with a central deficiency with a typical V-shaped cleft extending into the palm. The typical cleft hand (autosomal dominant, with normal but absent central rays, often with foot involvement) is distinguished from the atypical (sporadic, with hypoplastic remnant digits) — Manske and Halikis introduced the term "symbrachydactyly" for the latter. The typical form associates with SHFM (split hand–foot malformation, at least six loci; SHFM1 is DLX5/DLX6) and with EEC syndrome (ectrodactyly, ectodermal dysplasia, clefting). Treatment combines closure of the cleft, correction of thumb-index syndactyly, and pollicization in severe thumb deficiency; the Snow-Littler procedure is the classical reconstruction. Symbrachydactyly is treated with soft tissue release, toe phalanx transfer (Buck-Gramcko, Vilkki), and prosthetics.

### Phocomelia

Phocomelia is a rare longitudinal involvement with intercalary deficiency of the proximal segments; the hand attaches directly to the trunk or to a shortened upper limb. The condition was classically associated with thalidomide (the 1959–1962 catastrophe with ~10 000 affected children); contemporary cases are sporadic. The Frantz and O'Rahilly classification distinguishes complete, proximal, and distal phocomelia. Treatment is primarily prosthetic.

## Failure of Differentiation (Swanson II)

Failures of differentiation are characterized by skeletal elements that have formed but have not separated properly from one another.

### Syndactyly

Syndactyly — incomplete separation of adjacent digits — is one of the most common congenital hand anomalies (1 in 2 000 live births). It is classified by degree of fusion (complete/incomplete — by whether it reaches the tips) and by tissue involvement (simple — skin only; complex — with bony fusion; complicated — with complex malformations). The most commonly affected webspaces are the 3rd–4th (60% of cases), followed by 4th–5th, 2nd–3rd, and 1st–2nd. It may be sporadic or familial (autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance). Associated syndromes:

**Apert (acrocephalosyndactyly Type I)**: craniosynostosis, midface hypoplasia, complex syndactyly of all four limbs, FGFR2 mutations. The Upton classification (1991) distinguishes three Apert hand types: I (spade — shovel-like, simple syndactyly), II (mitten — complex syndactyly), III (rosebud — most severe with symphalangism). Treatment is staged release with multiple Z-plasties and skin grafts.

**Poland**: unilateral absence of the pectoralis major (sternocostal head), chest wall asymmetry, symbrachydactyly. Sporadic, always unilateral, M:F 2-3:1; etiology — vascular event at 6 weeks.

**Constriction band syndrome**: acrosyndactyly (see corresponding section).

The principle of treatment is surgical release prioritizing the "border" digits (thumb-index and ring-little released by 6–12 months — otherwise differences in length produce progressive deformity); central digits (index-middle, middle-ring) by 12–24 months. Complex syndactyly is treated in several stages. Technique: (1) zigzag (W-plasty) incisions to avoid linear scar contracture; (2) careful separation preserving both neurovascular bundles; (3) webspace reconstruction with a dorsal rotation flap (Flatt, Buck-Gramcko) — provides a more natural shape that does not "creep" with digit spreading; (4) full-thickness skin grafts from the groin for lateral aspects. Principle: only one side of a digit per session — to prevent devascularization. Complications — webspace creep, scar contracture, and in complex cases postoperative digit loss. Long-term results are very good in simple and acceptable in complex syndactyly.

### Camptodactyly

Camptodactyly — non-traumatic flexion deformity of the PIP joint, most common in the little finger. Bilateral in ~70%, familial autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance, population incidence ~1%. Classification: Type I (infantile, in the first year of life, bilateral); Type II (adolescent, in puberty, unilateral, female predominant, progressive); Type III (severe multi-digit, often associated with arthrogryposis, oculo-dento-digital syndrome). Pathology combines skin and subcutaneous contracture, palmar fascial tightness, tendon anomalies (anomalous lumbrical insertion from FDP onto the PIP — producing flexion; hypoplastic FDS; retracted extensor) and joint changes in long-standing deformity. Treatment is initially conservative — dynamic extension splinting 16–20 hours/day for months. Surgery for severe or rigid forms — soft-tissue release, tendon transfer, osteotomy, or PIP arthrodesis in adulthood. Results are variable (30–50% full correction in mild/moderate).

### Clinodactyly

Clinodactyly — angular deformity in the coronal plane, most common in the little finger in a radial direction at the DIP. Incidence 1–10% in mild forms; associated with the delta phalanx — a wedge-shaped middle phalanx with a C-shaped oblique physis whose continued growth pulls the digit in a direction perpendicular to normal. Sporadic or autosomal dominant; a component of Down (trisomy 21), Klinefelter, Russell-Silver. Mild (<10°) requires no treatment. Severe forms are addressed by: excision of the delta phalanx with replacement bone graft (Light, Buck-Gramcko); physeal bar resection with fat interposition (Vickers) — in children with open physes; closing- or opening-wedge osteotomy in adults.

### Radioulnar synostosis

Congenital radioulnar synostosis — failure of separation of the proximal radius and ulna, producing a fixed pronated forearm without rotation. Bilateral in ~60%; incidence ~1 in 200 000. The Wilkie classification (1914): Type I (true proximal bony fusion with absent radial head) and Type II (radial head dislocation with fibrous synostosis, more common in syndromic conditions). Sporadic or autosomal dominant; association with Apert, Carpenter, Klinefelter, fetal alcohol syndrome. Bilateral involvement with severe pronation (>60°) is disabling. Treatment is derotational osteotomy — in the dominant limb mild pronation (5–15°), in the non-dominant mild supination (5–10°). Attempts to release the synostosis with fat or muscle interposition have given disappointing long-term results from recurrence; most centers recommend osteotomy only. Microvascular free muscle transfer (Kanaya) is described in selected patients. Complications: overcorrection, delayed union, compartment syndrome with rotations >70°.

### Congenital dislocation of the radial head

Isolated congenital dislocation of the radial head is rare and often unrecognized until late childhood. The head is typically dislocated posteriorly, hypoplastic and dome-shaped, with a flat capitellum. Isolated or part of nail-patella syndrome (LMX1B), Klinefelter, Cornelia de Lange. Treatment is conservative in asymptomatic patients; in adult-onset pain — radial head resection.

### Madelung deformity

The Madelung deformity (Otto Madelung, 1878) — longitudinal deformity of the distal radius with palmar-ulnar tilt of the articular surface, progressive prominence of the distal ulna, and a bayonet wrist deformity. It presents in late childhood, typically in girls (4:1), often bilateral. The etiology is the hypoplastic or abnormally inserted Vickers ligament (from the palmar-ulnar radius to the ulnar lunate), which tethers the ulnar half of the distal radial physis. Sporadic or familial (often associated with Léri-Weill dyschondrosteosis — SHOX mutations). Also seen in Turner syndrome. Imaging shows palmar-ulnar tilt of the articular surface, pyramidal "carpal pyramid" insertion of the lunate, and increased ulnar variance. Treatment in a child with an active physis includes Vickers ligament release and physiolysis; in adulthood — dorsal opening-wedge radial osteotomy (Watson), ulnar shortening, or Sauvé-Kapandji procedure.

### Symphalangism

Symphalangism — congenital fusion of the PIP joints. Sporadic or autosomal dominant (NOG mutations encoding Noggin — a BMP antagonist). Associated with Apert, Hurler. Treatment is limited — arthroplasty or arthrodesis in a functional position in severe cases.

## Duplication (Swanson III)

### Polydactyly — general

Polydactyly is the presence of supernumerary digits, classified as preaxial (thumb side), postaxial (little finger side), or central. Polydactyly is one of the most common congenital hand anomalies worldwide, although the distribution of types varies significantly by ethnicity.

### Postaxial polydactyly

Postaxial polydactyly is the most common type worldwide (1 in 3 000 in European populations, 1 in 100–300 in African and African-descended populations — where it is usually isolated, autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance). In European populations it is more often associated with syndromes (Bardet-Biedl, Ellis-van Creveld, McKusick-Kaufman) requiring systematic evaluation. Stelling (1963) / Temtamy & McKusick (1978) classification: **Type A** — well-formed extra digit on a stable MCP with neurovascular supply; treatment is formal surgical excision in the first two years with reconstruction of collateral ligaments and tendons. **Type B** (postminimus) — rudimentary digit on a narrow soft-tissue pedicle; classical treatment is neonatal ligation (ischemia and detachment in 1–2 weeks), but reports of neuroma and cosmetic discomfort lead many contemporary centers to formal excision.

### Preaxial polydactyly (duplicated thumb)

Preaxial polydactyly is the most common congenital anomaly in Asian populations (1 in 3 000; 1 in 10 000–30 000 in European and African) and has more serious functional consequences than postaxial. Usually unilateral and sporadic. The Wassel (1969) classification, modified by Hung and Kay, distinguishes 7 types by level: I — bifid distal phalanx; II — duplicated distal phalanx over a common proximal; III — bifid proximal phalanx; **IV — full duplication of proximal and distal over a common metacarpal (most common, ~40%)**; V — bifid metacarpal; VI — full duplication of metacarpal and phalanges; VII — triphalangeal thumb, strongly associated with Holt-Oram, Fanconi, Ellis-van Creveld. Tonkin and Manske added "Wassel VIII" for inter-metacarpal duplications.

Treatment is surgical reconstruction of a functional thumb by 18 months. Principles: (1) selection of the dominant thumb — usually the better developed; if equal, the ulnar (better collateral ligament and innervation); (2) ablation of the lesser thumb with transfer of the collateral ligament, extensor, and thenar muscles to the preserved thumb; (3) joint reconstruction; (4) correction of angular deformities with osteotomy. The **Bilhaut-Cloquet** procedure (1890) combines elements of both thumbs into one composite thumb; the Iwasaki modification gives better results. Pollicization is used when neither component can be reconstructed as a thumb. Complications — Z deformity, collateral ligament instability, requiring revision in 20–30%. The triphalangeal thumb requires middle phalanx resection and reconstruction.

### Central polydactyly and mirror hand

Central polydactyly (involving the middle three digits) is rare and frequently associated with syndactyly (Holt-Oram, Greig cephalopolysyndactyly), requiring individualized treatment. Mirror hand (ulnar dimelia) is an extremely rare anomaly with two ulnae and 8–9 digits in mirror arrangement, without thumb and radius; caused by a double ZPA. Treatment includes pollicization of the most radial ulnar digit and resection of supernumerary rays.

## Overgrowth (Swanson IV)

### Macrodactyly

Macrodactyly is overgrowth of one or more digits, typically affecting soft tissues and skeleton. The condition may be isolated or part of a generalized overgrowth syndrome. Isolated macrodactyly is classified as:

Static: the digit is enlarged at birth and grows proportionally with others (1.5–2× normal) — relatively rare.

Progressive: the digit grows faster than normal, with worsening with age — the more common form. Progressive macrodactyly is described by Barsky as a "lipofibromatous hamartoma" of the median or ulnar nerve in the affected digit.

Syndromic forms of overgrowth include:

**Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1)**: autosomal dominant, NF1 mutations (17q11.2), with café-au-lait spots, axillary freckling, neurofibromas, optic gliomas, and potential plexiform neurofibromas presenting as macrodactyly. Treatment is complex because of continuing growth and malignancy risk.

**Klippel-Trénaunay syndrome**: triad of capillary malformation (port-wine stain), varicose veins, and soft tissue and bony hypertrophy of the affected limb; often produces hemihypertrophy with macrodactyly. Sporadic, in many cases caused by a mosaic somatic PIK3CA mutation.

**Proteus syndrome**: rare, progressive overgrowth of multiple tissues (bony, fatty, vascular, cutaneous) with asymmetric involvement — the "Elephant Man" Joseph Merrick is often cited (though his diagnosis is disputed and often reattributed to neurofibromatosis). The cause is somatic mosaic AKT1 (E17K) mutation.

**PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum (PROS)**: a new category encompassing several formerly eponymous syndromes (CLOVES, MCAP), all with somatic mosaic PIK3CA mutations. Active development of targeted PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitor therapy (alpelisib).

Treatment of progressive macrodactyly is challenging. Options include: (1) **epiphysiodesis** to limit growth — performed in a child in active growth at a calculated age to limit the final length to approximately that of a normal neighbor; (2) **soft tissue debulking** — serial reduction of hypertrophic subcutaneous fat and fibrous tissue, with careful preservation of the neurovascular bundles; (3) **ray resection** in severe cases — complete removal of the digit and metacarpal, leaving a four-digit hand; (4) **amputation** in extreme cases or at malignancy risk. Cosmetic and functional results are often suboptimal and treatment requires careful family coordination and accounting for the underlying syndrome. In syndromes with somatic mosaic mutations (PROS, Proteus) targeted molecular therapies are emerging and may change the approach.

### Hemihypertrophy and systemic overgrowth syndromes

Hemihypertrophy — asymmetric growth of one side of the body — may be isolated or part of syndromes such as Beckwith-Wiedemann, Russell-Silver, Klippel-Trénaunay, Proteus. Isolated hemihypertrophy is associated with increased risk of childhood malignancies (Wilms tumor, hepatoblastoma) — requires serial screening with abdominal ultrasound and α-fetoprotein until about age 7. Orthopaedic treatment of associated limb length differences or dysplasias follows general principles.

## Undergrowth (Swanson V)

### Thumb hypoplasia

Thumb hypoplasia is among the most important congenital conditions for the hand surgeon, because the thumb provides ~50% of hand function, and adequate reconstruction transforms the child's function. The Blauth classification (1967), modified by Manske and McCarroll:

**Type I** — mild generalized hypoplasia with all structures present but small. Treatment: not required.

**Type II** — absence or hypoplasia of thenar muscles, narrow first webspace, ulnar collateral ligament insufficiency at the MCP. The thumb has normal bony structure but does not oppose. Treatment: opponensplasty (abductor digiti minimi transfer by Huber, or FDS of the ring finger by Royle-Thompson), webspace deepening with Z- or 4-flap plasty, UCL reconstruction.

**Type IIIA** — Type II plus extrinsic tendon anomalies with **STABLE** CMC joint. Treatment: as Type II with added tendon transfers.

**Type IIIB** — Type IIIA plus **UNSTABLE** CMC joint. This is the critical transition point: pollicization — NOT opponensplasty — is the recommended treatment.

**Type IV** — floating thumb (pouce flottant) on a narrow skin pedicle. Treatment: ablation with pollicization.

**Type V** — complete absence. Treatment: pollicization.

Pollicization in its modern form — described by Buck-Gramcko (Hamburg, 1971) — is a procedure in which the index finger is detached as a composite flap (preserving its palmar digital arteries, nerves, extensors, and flexors), shortened by removal of the metacarpal, rotated 130–160° on its long axis, and attached at the thumb position. The metacarpal head of the index becomes a new trapezium; the intrinsics are adapted — the first palmar interosseous becomes the new adductor pollicis, the first dorsal interosseous becomes the new abductor pollicis brevis. Principles: preservation of the epiphyseal cartilage for continued growth; rotation ~160° and hyperextension of the new CMC; reconstruction of collateral ligaments; opponens reconstruction (Huber). Results in well-performed surgery are very good — functional opposition and grip with high satisfaction (Manske, Goldfarb, Buck-Gramcko, Vilkki). Microvascular toe-to-thumb transfer is an alternative, especially in Asian centers, with comparable results in experienced hands.

### Brachydactyly

Brachydactyly — shortening of one or more digits, usually autosomal dominant. The Bell (1951) classification, modified by Temtamy and McKusick — five types (A–E): A — middle phalanges; B — distal phalanges and nails (ROR2 mutations); C — index and middle with delta phalanges (GDF5); **D — short thumb (brachydactyly D), HOXD13 — one of the most common isolated types**; E — brachymetacarpia, associated with pseudohypoparathyroidism (Albright). Treatment is rarely needed; in severe functional limitation — distraction lengthening osteotomy.

## Constriction Band Syndrome (Swanson VI)

### Terminology and definitions

The constriction band syndrome — amniotic band syndrome, Streeter dysplasia, amniotic disruption complex, or ADAM complex — is a disorder in which strands of the amniotic membrane wrap around developing fetal structures and produce a spectrum of deformities (amputations, constriction rings, acrosyndactyly). The topic is traditionally included in Swanson VI, although strictly speaking this is a *disruptive* fetal event, not a failure of development; in OMT — the "deformations" category. The incidence is 1 in 1 200–15 000 live births; ~1.5% of fetuses with bands reach delivery. The condition is sporadic, not heritable and not associated with chromosomal anomalies — an important fact for family counseling.

### Etiology and clinical features

The classical "exogenous" theory of Torpin (1965) holds that early amnion rupture (weeks 6–18) releases fibrous strands that entangle developing parts. Evidence: visible amniotic membranes at prenatal ultrasound and delivery; asymmetry not following embryologic lines; association with oligohydramnios and amniocentesis; rarity of familial forms. Streeter's "endogenous" theory (1930) holds that bands are secondary. Current consensus supports the exogenous theory; Higginbottom proposed a unifying "vascular-mechanical" hypothesis. Risk factors: premature amnion rupture, amniocentesis, misoprostol.

Clinical features include: (1) **constriction rings** — from superficial grooves to deep ones passing through fascia and neurovascular bundles with distal edema, lymphedema, ischemia, and intrauterine amputation in extreme cases; (2) **congenital amputations** at the level of constriction, typically distal; (3) **acrosyndactyly** — distal fusion with proximal sinuses or fenestrations (a pathognomonic feature distinguishing from ordinary syndactyly); Walsh classification — mild (1–2 fusions), moderate, severe (all digits fused); (4) **limb length difference**; (5) **clubfoot** with bands of the lower leg; (6) less commonly — craniofacial clefts and abdominal wall defects (limb-body wall complex).

### Treatment

Severe constriction bands with vascular or lymphatic compromise require **emergency release** in the neonatal period to prevent limb loss. Technique includes complete excision of the band with Z- or W-plasty. Historical recommendations for two-stage release (fearing devascularization) are now superseded by contemporary data favoring single-stage complete release with better results. Less severe bands are released between 6 and 18 months. Acrosyndactyly is treated by surgical separation with attention to the fenestrations — technically more demanding than ordinary syndactyly because of the hypoplastic structures. Limb length difference is treated by epiphysiodesis or Ilizarov lengthening. Congenital amputations — by prosthetic fitting and where indicated toe-to-hand transfer. Results are generally good with early treatment.

## Generalized Skeletal Abnormalities (Swanson VII)

Swanson VII includes generalized skeletal disorders with hand manifestations: skeletal dysplasias (achondroplasia, multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, diastrophic dysplasia), mucopolysaccharidoses (Hurler, Hunter, Morquio, Maroteaux-Lamy), osteogenesis imperfecta, arthrogryposis.

Hand features in these conditions include:

In achondroplasia: characteristic "trident" hand with short digits, especially at the 3rd and 4th interdigital spaces, and relative brachydactyly. Treatment is conservative.

In mucopolysaccharidoses: progressive digital stiffness, "claw hand" from carpal tunnel syndrome (mucopolysaccharides deposit in the transverse carpal ligament), palmar fibrosis and tendon contractures requiring carpal tunnel release and tendon release with variable results due to the underlying systemic disease.

In osteogenesis imperfecta: skeletal fragility with multiple childhood fractures. Standard orthopaedic principles apply with attention to systemic considerations (bisphosphonates, joint instability risk).

In arthrogryposis (multiple congenital contractures): the classical hand is with flexed, adducted digits, flexed elbow, adducted internally rotated shoulder. Arthrogryposis is heterogeneous — amyoplasia is the most common form. Treatment is conservative with stretching, splinting, occupational therapy, and surgical with soft tissue release and corrections of specific deformities (humeral osteotomy, congenital elbow dislocation, thumb reconstruction).

Orthopaedic treatment of the hand in these conditions is typically secondary to treatment of the underlying systemic disorder and requires multidisciplinary coordination.

## Pediatric Trigger Thumb and Trigger Digits

Although not strictly a primary congenital anomaly in the embryologic sense, pediatric trigger thumb deserves mention here because of its common presentation in the first year of life. The condition involves a stenotic A1 pulley with a thickened nodule on the flexor pollicis longus (the Notta nodule — described by Adolf Notta in 1850), producing inability to extend the interphalangeal joint. The condition is usually unilateral but bilateral cases occur in ~25%. Incidence is ~0.05% of live births, although many cases present between 6 and 24 months of age.

The natural history of true congenital trigger thumb (present at birth) is uncertain; classical teaching held that spontaneous resolution is rare and surgical release should be performed by 1–2 years, but newer studies show spontaneous resolution rates of 30–60% with observation. Contemporary practice typically includes observation for the first 1–2 years (with extension splinting in older children) and surgical release (open division of the A1 pulley) in cases that do not resolve by age 3.

Surgical treatment is a relatively simple ambulatory procedure with a high success rate. A short transverse or curvilinear incision over the MCP flexion crease permits access to the A1 pulley, which is released under direct visualization. Postoperatively, full functional recovery is expected.

Trigger digits of other fingers (not the thumb) are much rarer in childhood and often associated with syndromic conditions (mucopolysaccharidoses, Marfan syndrome, trisomy 21) or anatomic anomalies (decussation nodule, abnormal A2 pulley). Treatment is more complex and often requires a broader release.

## Syndromologic Evaluation

### Systemic syndromes with hand manifestations

A large number of systemic syndromes present with characteristic hand findings. Recognition of the syndromic context is critical for management, because the associated systemic manifestations (cardiac, renal, hematologic) often have a greater impact on prognosis than the orthopaedic findings.

The syndromes most commonly encountered by the pediatric hand surgeon:

VATER/VACTERL: vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal, limb — sporadic association, usually with radial deficiency.

Holt-Oram: heart-hand syndrome, TBX5 mutations, autosomal dominant.

Fanconi anemia: hematologic syndrome with radial deficiency and risk of bone marrow failure.

TAR (thrombocytopenia–absent radius): bilateral radial aplasia with thrombocytopenia and preserved thumb.

Apert (acrocephalosyndactyly Type I): craniosynostosis + complex syndactyly, FGFR2 mutations.

Poland: unilateral pectoralis major aplasia + symbrachydactyly.

Pfeiffer (acrocephalosyndactyly Type V): craniosynostosis + broad thumbs and great toes.

Carpenter (acrocephalopolysyndactyly Type II): craniosynostosis + polydactyly + syndactyly + cardiac defects.

Crouzon: craniosynostosis without significant hand anomalies (important distinction from Apert).

Saethre-Chotzen: craniosynostosis + mild brachydactyly + partial syndactyly (3-4).

Ellis-van Creveld (chondroectodermal dysplasia): postaxial polydactyly + chondrodysplasia + cardiac defects + ectodermal dysplasia. Common among the Amish.

Bardet-Biedl: postaxial polydactyly + retinitis pigmentosa + obesity + renal anomalies + cognitive impairment.

Down (trisomy 21): little-finger clinodactyly with delta phalanx + hypotonia + cardiac defects.

Russell-Silver: short stature + hemihypertrophy + clinodactyly.

Beckwith-Wiedemann: macroglossia + omphalocele + hemihypertrophy + increased risk of Wilms tumor.

Cornelia de Lange: characteristic face + growth restriction + small or absent upper limbs + cardiac defects.

Klippel-Feil: cervical fusion + Sprengel + ulnar anomalies (sometimes).

Goltz (focal dermal hypoplasia): cutaneous anomalies + limb deficiencies (ulnar) + papillomas.

### Minimal diagnostic panel

Every child with a major congenital hand anomaly requires systematic evaluation:

(1) Detailed history: family, consanguinity, exposure to teratogens during pregnancy (alcohol, valproate, thalidomide, warfarin, misoprostol, chemotherapy), maternal diabetes, infections during pregnancy (TORCH).

(2) Physical examination: the affected limb, contralateral limb, all other limbs, spine, head and face, trunk and back, heart, abdomen, genitalia. Particular attention to: nails and skin (nail-patella), eyes (cataract, colobomas, antimongoloid slant), ears (preauricular tags, microtia, abnormal shape), mouth (clefts, cleft palate), heart (murmur), genitalia (hypospadias, cryptorchidism).

(3) Minimum laboratory and imaging panel:
- Cardiac echocardiography in all severe upper limb anomalies.
- Renal ultrasound in radial deficiencies, in VACTERL.
- Complete blood count with platelet review in radial deficiencies.
- Chromosomal analysis (karyotype) and/or array CGH in multiple or unusual anomalies.
- Chromosomal breakage analysis with diepoxybutane (Fanconi anemia test) in every radial deficiency.

(4) Selective molecular tests on suspicion: TBX5 for Holt-Oram, FGFR2 for Apert, LMX1B for nail-patella, SHOX for Madelung, FBN1 for Marfan, NF1 for neurofibromatosis, PIK3CA for PROS, NOG for symphalangism.

(5) Genetic counseling is recommended for most complex cases, especially with positive family history or suspicion of a monogenic or chromosomal disorder.

## Timing of Surgical Treatment

The timing of reconstructive surgery in congenital hand anomalies is determined by several considerations:

(1) Maturity of the affected structures — anesthetic risk, joint stability, skin tolerance for surgery.

(2) The developmental window for hand function — bimanual function emerges in the second year, fine motor skills in the third and fourth. Reconstructions that change the functional morphology of the hand (pollicization, syndactyly release) should be done by 18–24 months to allow normal cortical-motor assimilation and motor skill development with the new hand configuration.

(3) The risk of growth-related deformity if the anomaly is not corrected. Border digits (thumb-index, ring-little) must be separated early (by 12 months) because the significant length difference causes progressive deformity. Deficiencies that restrict growth (amniotic bands) must be released as soon as it is safe.

(4) Family considerations and the child's psychosocial development. Reconstructions that significantly change hand appearance (pollicization) are best done before the child develops strong self-image awareness (i.e., before age 3).

As a general rule:

Emergency procedures (newborn or early infancy): release of severe constriction bands with vascular compromise.

Early procedures (3–9 months): initiation of passive stretching and splinting in radial deficiency, ligation of postaxial Type B polydactyly.

First year (6–12 months): release of border-digit syndactyly. Centralization or radialization in severe radial deficiency. Release of less severe constriction bands.

Second year (12–24 months): release of central syndactyly. Polydactyly excision. Thumb pollicization. Opponensplasty. Acrosyndactyly release.

Third–fourth year: release of camptodactyly (if conservative treatment fails). Release of complex syndactylies (Apert). Multi-stage reconstructions.

Adolescence: definitive reconstruction of complex cases. Arthrodeses. Digital lengthening. Correction of residual deformities.

## Outcomes and Long-Term Perspectives

Long-term results in congenital hand anomalies depend on: severity of the initial deformity, syndromic context, timing and quality of surgical treatment, and psychosocial support. General principles:

In syndactyly treatment: long-term results are very good in simple syndactyly (95% satisfaction); acceptable in complex syndactyly and Apert (continued need for revisions).

In radial deficiency: centralization gives initially good results but almost universal recurrence of radial deviation in late childhood and adolescence; pollicization gives good functional results in experienced hands (Manske, Goldfarb, Buck-Gramcko, Vilkki).

In polydactyly: long-term results in duplicated thumb depend on Wassel type and quality of primary surgery; transitional problems (Z deformity, collateral ligament instability) require revision in about 20–30%.

In thumb hypoplasia with pollicization: long-term functional evaluation shows the pollicized thumb achieves 60–80% of normal grip strength, with significant functional compensation and patient satisfaction.

In constriction band syndrome: early release of severe bands prevents limb loss; acrosyndactyly reconstruction gives acceptable cosmetic and functional results; limb length difference requires the full spectrum of orthopaedic procedures.

Psychosocial aspects require continuing support. Children with congenital hand anomalies often adapt remarkably, developing astonishing compensatory skills. Psychological and social support of the family, the child's education in self-confidence, and appropriate resource provision are as important as surgical treatment.

## Multidisciplinary Care

Care of the child with a major congenital hand anomaly is best delivered in a multidisciplinary clinic including: pediatric hand surgery, occupational therapy, prosthetics, psychology, social work, and where indicated — genetics, cardiology, hematology, nephrology. The family requires ongoing support and education, and the child's developmental transition requires repeat assessment of function and adaptation of the treatment plan.

The six principles of care for the child with a congenital anomaly (Flatt and James):

(1) Early recognition of the syndromic context with appropriate investigations.

(2) A coordinated multidisciplinary approach.

(3) Realistic and detailed family counseling.

(4) Individualized surgical planning, respecting functional, developmental, and psychosocial considerations.

(5) Long-term follow-up with readiness for revision surgery when indicated.

(6) Education of the child in independence, self-confidence, and a realistic understanding of personal limitations and potential.

## Local Contributions and Contributions of Different Schools

Surgery of congenital hand anomalies has been developed by many schools in different countries. This section notes the main contributions, organized by country, without claim to completeness.

### Bulgaria

The Bulgarian orthopaedic school, founded by Boycho Boychev (1902–1971) — professor and founder of the Institute of Orthopaedics and Traumatology at the Sofia Medical Academy — developed a systematic approach to pediatric orthopaedics, reflected in his classical *Surgical Orthopaedics*. Congenital hand anomalies are treated in the Bulgarian literature as part of the broader field of pediatric orthopaedics. The historical contribution of the Bulgarian school lies in introducing conservative treatment methods and adapting international standards to local conditions. Over recent decades pediatric hand surgery in Bulgaria has developed in specialized departments in Sofia (Sv. Ivan Rilski University Hospital, Prof. B. Boychev SBALTO, N. I. Pirogov Emergency Hospital) and Plovdiv (Sv. Georgi University Hospital), with growing international collaboration, especially with German and Austrian centers. Complex reconstructive cases (pollicization, severe syndactyly, amniotic bands) are often discussed in multidisciplinary consultations and where needed referred to specialized European centers.

### Germany and Austria

The German school is one of the world's leading schools in congenital hand surgery, primarily through Dietrich Buck-Gramcko (1927–2012) of Hamburg. Buck-Gramcko is the author of the modern form of pollicization (1971), described in hundreds of surgical procedures with detailed functional outcomes that have become a world standard. His *Congenital Malformations of the Hand and Forearm* (1998) remains a primary reference in the field. Additional German and Austrian contributions are in systematic classifications (Buck-Gramcko, Pechlaner), long-term outcome evaluation, and techniques for ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction in duplicated thumb. The Hamburg school continues actively through Buck-Gramcko's students (Lanz, Mann, Stütz).

### United Kingdom

The British school has a long tradition in pediatric orthopaedics, represented by centers such as Great Ormond Street and the Royal National Orthopaedic Hospital (Stanmore). Contributions of British surgeons (Smith, Brennen, Bayne) include refinement of syndactyly release technique and description of long-term outcomes. The British Society for Surgery of the Hand plays an important role in standardizing care.

### France

The French school of hand surgery, represented by Marc Iselin (1898–1987), Foucher, and colleagues from centers in Paris, Strasbourg, and Nancy, has significant contributions in thumb reconstruction techniques (including microsurgical transfers), nail apparatus reconstruction, and systematic description of webspace flaps. The French hand surgery society (GEM — Groupe d'Etude de la Main) is the historical organizing body.

### United States

The American school is productive with multiple centers. Adrian E. Flatt (Iowa) authored the classical *The Care of Congenital Hand Anomalies* (1977), still a reference. Paul Manske (St. Louis) authored the widely used classification and radialization technique. Joseph Upton III (Boston) authored the original description of the Apert hand and complex syndactyly reconstructions. Ghazi Rayan (Oklahoma) co-authored with Upton *Congenital Hand Anomalies and Associated Syndromes* (2014). Peter Waters (Boston) and Donald Bae authored *Pediatric Hand and Upper Limb Surgery* (2012). Terry Light (Chicago) has important contributions in thumb surgery and long-term functional outcome evaluation. Charles Goldfarb (St. Louis) is among the leading contemporary authors. The American Society for Surgery of the Hand (ASSH) and the American Pediatric Hand Surgery Association (APSHA) coordinate professional development.

### Sweden and Scandinavia

The Swedish and Scandinavian centers (Karolinska, Gothenburg, Tampere, Helsinki) have a strong microsurgical tradition. Göran Lundborg (Malmö) is internationally recognized for his work on peripheral nerve regeneration and nerve surgery, with direct application in pediatric hand surgery. Simo Vilkki (Tampere) authored the original microvascular second-MTP transfer for wrist stabilization in radial deficiency and complex symbrachydactyly reconstructions. Scandinavian centers also pioneered distraction lengthening.

### Japan

The Japanese school is internationally leading in microsurgical toe-to-hand transfer. Susumu Tamai (Nara) performed the first successful digit replantation (1968) and developed the toe-to-thumb technique in children. Takeshi Kuroshima (Kobe) and Takayuki Tajima (Toyohashi) developed techniques for reconstruction of the severely hypoplastic hand. The Japanese Society for Reconstructive Microsurgery is a leading forum.

### Australia and New Zealand

Michael Tonkin (Sydney) and his collaborators at Royal North Shore Hospital and the Kids Research Institute are the principal authors of the OMT (Oberg–Manske–Tonkin) classification (2010) and continue its updates. The Australian school has a strong academic tradition in the syndromology of congenital hand anomalies.

### Other centers

Significant contributions come from: Italy (Brunelli — Brescia, with major contributions to reconstructive hand surgery), the Netherlands (Hovius — Rotterdam), Canada (Manktelow — Toronto, in microsurgery), Korea (Chang — Seoul), Taiwan (Wei — Chang Gung, in microsurgical transfer), and India (Pawar — Pune).

## Summary and Take-Home Points

Congenital hand anomalies comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders, classified by the Swanson system into failures of formation, failures of differentiation, duplications, overgrowth, undergrowth, constriction band syndrome, and generalized skeletal anomalies. The contemporary OMT (Oberg–Manske–Tonkin) classification provides a more accurate biologically based categorization, although the Swanson classification remains in wide clinical use.

Clinical recognition of the major anomalies — radial and ulnar longitudinal deficiency with their syndromic associations (especially VACTERL, Holt-Oram, Fanconi anemia, TAR), syndactyly with its associations to Apert and Poland syndromes, polydactyly with the Wassel classification in duplicated thumb, hypoplastic thumb with the Blauth classification, and amniotic band syndrome — is the foundation of pediatric hand practice.

Treatment is highly individualized and combines surgical reconstruction (syndactyly release, polydactyly excision, pollicization in thumb hypoplasia, opponensplasty, constriction band release) with prosthetics and occupational therapy. The timing of surgery is determined by developmental, functional, and psychosocial considerations; most complex reconstructions are performed between 6 and 24 months of age.

Recognition of associated systemic anomalies — cardiac, renal, hematologic — and the timely diagnosis of conditions such as Fanconi anemia (requiring chromosomal breakage testing in every radial deficiency) is among the most important contributions of the orthopaedic surgeon to care of the child with a congenital hand anomaly.

Amniotic band syndrome, although devastating in its severe presentations, is sporadic and not associated with chromosomal or heritable disease — a fact that allows the family to be reassured and the focus directed to the specific reconstructive needs of the affected limb. Severe bands with vascular compromise require emergency release in the neonatal period; acrosyndactyly is treated by release in the first year and a half; limb length differences — by epiphysiodesis or Ilizarov lengthening in later childhood.

Care of the child with a congenital hand anomaly requires a multidisciplinary approach, coordinated by an experienced pediatric hand surgeon, with family support and realistic counseling. The principles of Buck-Gramcko, Manske, Flatt, Upton, Waters, and the many other leading authors in the field, reflected in their classical works, remain current: a congenital hand anomaly is an opportunity for the reconstructive surgeon not only to correct anatomy but also to participate in shaping a functionally and psychosocially mature individual capable of meeting the challenges of life.
