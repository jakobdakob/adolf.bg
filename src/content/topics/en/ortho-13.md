## Introduction

Congenital spine anomalies arise from disturbances of embryological vertebral and spinal-cord development occurring approximately between the third and tenth weeks of gestation. They encompass a wide spectrum: from minor radiographic variants without clinical consequence — accessory cervical ribs, lumbosacral transitional vertebrae — to severe deformities producing progressive scoliosis, neurological impairment, and late cardiopulmonary complications, to the dysraphic states (myelomeningocele, meningocele, spina bifida occulta) that combine vertebral malformation with overt or covert neural tube defects. This chapter synthesizes content from the modern standard references in the field: Akbarnia, Yazici & Thompson — *The Growing Spine: Management of Spinal Disorders in Young Children* (2nd ed., Springer 2016), drawing especially on the chapters on congenital scoliosis (Yazici, Yilmaz, Kawakami), myelomeningocele, and hemivertebra resection; Moe/Lonstein/Bradford — *Textbook of Scoliosis and Other Spinal Deformities*, the long-standing reference on classification of vertebral anomalies and their natural history; Newton, Harms, Betz & Samdani — Harms Study Group publications on surgical techniques for early-onset scoliosis and hemivertebra excision; Bridwell & DeWald — *The Textbook of Spinal Surgery*, especially the chapters on congenital and neurogenic scoliosis, cervical instability, and reconstructive osteotomies; together with Tachdjian's *Pediatric Orthopaedics* (Herring, ed.), Apley & Solomon's *System of Orthopaedics and Trauma*, and Lovell & Winter's *Pediatric Orthopaedics* for the general pediatric orthopedic framework. The chapter addresses congenital vertebral anomalies producing scoliosis and kyphosis, Klippel-Feil syndrome of cervical vertebral fusion, cervical ribs and their thoracic outlet implications, and the full spectrum of dysraphic states, with practical emphasis on early recognition, systematic search for associated anomalies, and stage-appropriate surgical management.

## Embryological Background

The vertebral column develops from the paraxial mesoderm, which forms paired somites in cranio-caudal sequence between weeks 3 and 5 of gestation. Each somite divides into a dermatome (skin), myotome (muscle), and sclerotome (vertebra). The sclerotome subsequently divides into a cranial and caudal half, and the vertebrae are formed by fusion of the caudal half of one somite with the cranial half of the next caudal somite — the process of resegmentation. This intricate sequence produces vertebrae and adjacent muscles in different segmental positions, with the spinal nerves emerging between vertebrae. The neural tube closes between days 22 and 28 of gestation; failure of closure produces the dysraphic disorders. Disturbances of sclerotome formation, division, or resegmentation produce the spectrum of failure of formation (hemivertebrae, wedge vertebrae) and failure of segmentation (block vertebrae, unilateral unsegmented bars) that underlie congenital scoliosis.

The molecular basis of vertebral segmentation is increasingly important for understanding congenital scoliosis. The segmentation clock, controlled by the Notch pathways (DLL3, MESP2, HES7, LFNG), drives periodic somite formation; mutations in these genes produce autosomal-recessive spondylocostal dysostosis (Jarcho-Levin syndrome) with multiple segmentation defects. Sporadic copy-number variations (TBX6 haploinsufficiency) have also been identified in a subgroup of patients with congenital scoliosis. These molecular insights support the clinical practice of genetic counseling in patients with multiple vertebral anomalies or family history.

## Congenital Scoliosis

## Classification and Pathogenesis

Congenital scoliosis is scoliosis arising from anomalous vertebral development, in contrast to idiopathic scoliosis (in which the vertebrae form normally but become deformed by an unknown developmental process). The classical Winter and MacEwen classification, subsequently refined by McMaster (Edinburgh, series of 251 children with >5-year follow-up, JBJS 1982), divides the defects into three categories: failures of formation, failures of segmentation, and mixed defects. This classification remains the basis for all modern references, including Akbarnia, Moe/Lonstein, and Bridwell.

Failures of formation: A wedge vertebra is a partially formed vertebra with only one ossification center developed, producing a hemivertebra. The hemivertebra is the commonest cause of congenital scoliosis and is classified by its relationship to adjacent vertebrae: a fully segmented hemivertebra has open disc spaces above and below and produces the greatest progressive deformity (approximately 1–2° per year for an isolated hemivertebra, increasing during periods of rapid growth); a semi-segmented hemivertebra is fused above or below and produces less progression; an incarcerated hemivertebra is fused above and below within an otherwise intact vertebral column and produces minimal progression; a non-segmented hemivertebra (the "block hemivertebra") rarely produces progressive deformity. Akbarnia/Yazici emphasize that the position of the hemivertebra also matters: lumbosacral and thoracolumbar hemivertebrae produce the most severe balance-disturbing effect, because the deformity cannot be compensated by the segments below.

Failures of segmentation: A block vertebra is the failure of separation of two adjacent vertebrae, with bilateral fusion of the disc and bony elements — a stable condition with minimal progression. A unilateral unsegmented bar is the failure of separation on one side of two or more adjacent vertebrae — the single most progressive anomaly because the unaffected side continues to grow while the affected side does not, producing rapid progressive scoliosis at an expected rate of 5–6° per year for a single bar and up to 10° per year when more than two segments are involved.

Mixed defects: Combinations of failure of formation and failure of segmentation — particularly a unilateral unsegmented bar combined with a contralateral hemivertebra — produce the most rapidly progressive deformities (>10° per year by McMaster) and the greatest urgency for early surgical intervention. This constellation is one of the few absolute surgical indications already in infancy.

The etiology of congenital vertebral malformations is multifactorial. Experimental models in small ruminants and rabbits show that prenatal hypoxia within a sensitive window of somitogenesis (Day 14–25 in rabbit, corresponding to ~weeks 3–5 in humans) produces a spectrum of hemivertebrae. Teratogens (valproate, folate antagonists, alcohol), high temperatures, low oxygen saturation, and genetic factors (TBX6, Notch pathway genes) are documented in the literature.

## Natural History

The natural history depends on the specific anomaly and its position. McMaster and Ohtsuka (1982) described the classical model: the risk of progressive scoliosis is greatest with unilateral unsegmented bars (especially when combined with contralateral hemivertebrae, with predicted progression of 5–10° per year), moderate with isolated hemivertebrae (depending on segmentation status), and minimal with block vertebrae or unsegmented blocks. The progression is greatest during periods of rapid skeletal growth — infancy (0–3 years), juvenile years (4–9), and the adolescent growth spurt (10–15). A period of relative quiescence during mid-childhood (5–10 years) between the rapid-growth periods does not mean the deformity will not progress — it will accelerate again with puberty.

Akbarnia emphasizes three summary rules for prognosis: (1) a hemivertebra that demonstrates 6° or more progression over 18–24 months is considered progressive and is an indication for surgery; (2) a unilateral unsegmented bar with a contralateral hemivertebra is always progressive and not amenable to observation; (3) when reliable literature prognosis for a specific configuration is lacking, serial radiographic follow-up at 3-month intervals during the first 18–24 months of life is a sensible approach.

## Associated Anomalies

Congenital scoliosis is associated with a substantial rate of additional anomalies that must be sought systematically: spinal cord anomalies (tethered cord, diastematomyelia, syringomyelia, intraspinal lipoma) in approximately 20–40%; renal anomalies in approximately 25–35% (because the kidney shares mesodermal origin with the vertebra) — unilateral renal agenesis, horseshoe kidney, ectopia; cardiac anomalies in approximately 10–15% — ventricular septal defect, tetralogy of Fallot, others; and other systemic anomalies including limb anomalies and anal atresia. The acronym VACTERL (vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal, limb) describes the most consequential of these associations. Every child with a congenital vertebral anomaly therefore requires investigation of all of these systems: total-spine MRI for the spinal cord, renal ultrasound, cardiac echocardiogram, and examination for limb and anorectal anomalies.

Akbarnia and the Newton/Harms Study Group recommend that MRI be a standard step in every congenital-scoliosis patient prior to surgical intervention; failure to detect tethered cord or diastematomyelia before instrumented distraction may produce catastrophic neurologic injury. A tethered cord is released before scoliosis correction; a diastematomyelic bony spur is resected before instrumented distraction.

## Imaging

Plain radiographs (PA and lateral standing whole-spine views in older children or sitting in infants) demonstrate the vertebral anomalies and measure the Cobb angle. Special views — flexibility flexion films, side-bending films, and occasional traction films under anesthesia — determine the flexibility of the deformity. Three-dimensional computed tomography with reconstructions is increasingly used, especially for complex anomalies and preoperative planning of pedicle-screw instrumentation in the small pediatric pedicle. MRI of the entire spine is mandatory to identify spinal dysraphism and intraspinal pathology. CT angiography or conventional arteriography may be required in complex revision cases when the artery of Adamkiewicz is suspected, as its injury during posterior approach can cause paraplegia.

## Treatment

The treatment principles are: serial observation of deformities at low risk of progression; surgical intervention before significant deformity develops in those at high risk of progression. Bracing has only a limited role in congenital scoliosis because the deformity is structural rather than ligamentous; compensatory curves above or below the structural area may respond to casting or bracing. Serial casting by Mehta's method (elongation-derotation-flexion casts under general anesthesia) has found a niche in early-onset congenital scoliosis, especially in younger children with flexible curves, although most congenital deformities are rigid and respond poorly.

Hemivertebra resection is the procedure of choice for the isolated progressive hemivertebra producing scoliosis. Akbarnia delineates three options (Chapter 31): (1) resection of the bony hemivertebra alone (minimalist); (2) resection of the bony hemivertebra plus the surrounding growth plates of the hemivertebra (intermediate); (3) complete resection of the hemivertebra plus the adjacent vertebral endplates on the convex side (aggressive, guaranteeing closure of the wedge defect). Yazici (Hacettepe University, Ankara) popularized the single-stage posterior approach: pedicled resection through a transpedicular posterior bony approach with pedicle screws in the adjacent vertebrae, removal of the hemivertebral body, pedicles, lamina, and adjacent discs, followed by compression of the instrumented segment to close the defect. The procedure is typically performed in early childhood (1–5 years) and achieves immediate correction at one or two levels with limited overall fusion length. Comparative series from Yazici and the Nagoya group (Kawakami) show ~60–70% correction of the main curve and durable results at 5- to 10-year follow-up, with significantly better outcomes when surgery is performed at younger ages.

Convex hemiepiphysiodesis (convex growth arrest), described by Roaf (UK) and refined in North America and Japan, fuses the convex side of the curve at the apex while allowing the concave side to continue growing; differential growth gradually corrects the curve. The procedure is less effective than hemivertebra excision and is less commonly used in modern practice, but remains an option in selected cases of young children with relatively flexible congenital deformity and substantial residual growth potential.

Posterior in-situ fusion of progressive curves is the historical treatment and remains a reasonable option for moderate progressive curves when excision is not feasible. The fusion arrests progression but does not correct existing deformity. It is often combined with anterior fusion to prevent the "crankshaft phenomenon" — rotation of the segment around the posterior tether of the fusion as anterior growth continues in the skeletally immature child below age 10.

Growth-friendly techniques (growing rods, vertical expandable prosthetic titanium rib — VEPTR, MAGEC magnetically controlled growing rods) are increasingly used for early-onset congenital scoliosis to allow continued spinal growth while controlling deformity. The growing-rod concept was developed by Akbarnia (San Diego); the VEPTR construct was developed by Campbell (San Antonio, Texas) for patients with Thoracic Insufficiency Syndrome (TIS), in which chronic chest-wall insufficiency limits pulmonary development. The MAGEC system allows non-invasive magnetic lengthening in the outpatient setting and reduces the number of anesthetics, though reports of metal debris and delayed fusion have emerged. The procedures require serial lengthenings every 6 months for 4–6 years, followed by definitive correction and instrumented fusion at skeletal maturity or at the limit of distraction.

Definitive correction and fusion with modern segmental instrumentation, sometimes combined with three-column osteotomies (pedicle subtraction osteotomy — PSO, vertebral column resection — VCR), is the standard for late-presenting or established severe deformities in older children and adolescents. Bridwell popularized PSO for fixed sagittal imbalance; Suk (Korea) and Lenke (St. Louis) developed VCR for severe coronal and three-column deformities. The procedures are extensive and carry substantial morbidity — blood loss exceeding 2 L, neurologic injury risk of 5–10%, operative times of 6–12 hours — but produce powerful correction of fixed deformities. Intraoperative neuromonitoring (somatosensory and motor evoked potentials, spontaneous and triggered EMG) is mandatory.

## Congenital Kyphosis

Congenital kyphosis is a sagittal-plane deformity produced by anomalous vertebral development. The classification, parallel to that of congenital scoliosis (McMaster, Winter, and Lonstein), recognizes failures of formation (anterior or anterolateral hemivertebrae producing anterior wedging) and failures of segmentation (anterior or anterolateral unsegmented bars producing anterior tethering). Type I — failure of formation — typically produces the most rapidly progressive deformity, particularly when the anterior vertebral body is absent. Progression is often >10° per year, and the high risk of paraplegia from progressive kyphosis with cord compression makes early surgical intervention essential.

Type II — failure of segmentation — produces a slower, more predictable progression with a lower risk of neurologic deficit, although the cosmetic deformity can be substantial by adulthood. Type III — mixed defects — varies with the specific anomaly. Lonstein (Minneapolis) reported in the classic series of 84 children (1999) the highest rate of neurologic deficit with Type I deformities at the mid-thoracic level (T4–T9), where the spinal cord has the least longitudinal reserve.

Treatment principles emphasize early surgical correction of progressive Type I deformities, often with combined anterior and posterior fusion to provide the necessary anterior support. Akbarnia recommends posterior in-situ fusion in the 2- to 3-year-old before the structural deformity exceeds 50°, followed by observation for further progression and reassessment for osteotomy in later age if the curve progresses. With established kyphosis >50° and cord compression, anterior decompression plus posterior instrumented fusion is required; in selected cases, vertebral column resection via posterior approach is performed for correction of fixed deformity. The risks of paraplegia from progressive kyphosis are sufficient that surgical intervention is recommended even in the very young child with rapidly progressive disease.

## Klippel-Feil Syndrome

## Definition and Epidemiology

Klippel-Feil syndrome, described by Maurice Klippel and André Feil in Paris in 1912 in the post-mortem case of a 46-year-old tailor with "massive congenital fusion of cervical vertebrae," is the congenital fusion of two or more cervical vertebrae. The classical clinical triad — short neck, low posterior hairline, and limited cervical motion — is present in only a minority of cases (approximately 30–50%); many patients have isolated fusion of two vertebrae without the obvious clinical phenotype and are discovered incidentally or because of associated symptoms. Incidence is approximately 1 in 40,000–50,000 live births, with slight female predominance. Bilateral involvement of the associated systemic anomalies (Sprengel, renal agenesis, Duane syndrome) is common.

## Classification

Three Feil types are recognized: Type I — massive fusion of cervical vertebrae often with thoracic involvement and with the most severe clinical phenotype; Type II — fusion of one or two cervical vertebrae (the commonest type, usually C2–C3 or C5–C6); Type III — Type I or II features combined with lower thoracic or lumbar fusion.

The more clinically useful Samartzis classification (2006) groups patients by the pattern of cervical fusion: Type I — single congenitally fused cervical segment; Type II — multiple non-contiguous congenitally fused segments; Type III — multiple contiguous congenitally fused segments. The Samartzis classification has better prognostic value for the development of symptomatic adjacent-segment instability and axial neck pain.

Radiographic markers include associated widening of the intervertebral foramen at the fusion, a "wineglass" appearance from compensatory hypertrophy of unfused segments, omovertebral bony bridges, and cervical spina bifida occulta of C1.

## Associated Anomalies

Klippel-Feil syndrome is strongly associated with multiple systemic anomalies that must be sought systematically: scoliosis (in approximately 50%), Sprengel deformity (elevated, hypoplastic scapula due to failed descent during development, in approximately 30%), renal anomalies (in approximately 35%, with absent kidney being the most common single anomaly), cardiac anomalies (particularly ventricular septal defect, in approximately 5–14%), hearing impairment (approximately 30% — conductive, sensorineural, or mixed; described by Hensinger in the classic 1974 series), and Duane retraction syndrome (in approximately 4%, with limited horizontal eye motion). Other associated anomalies include synkinesis (mirror movements, often due to defective pyramidal decussation), cleft lip or palate, and craniofacial anomalies. The combination of Klippel-Feil and Sprengel deformity, sometimes combined with renal anomalies, constitutes the more complete clinical syndrome.

Hensinger, Lang, and MacEwen (Newington Hospital, Connecticut, 1974) published the landmark study of natural history and associated anomalies in 50 patients with Klippel-Feil; their recommendations for systematic screening of the renal, cardiac, and auditory systems remain standard.

## Clinical Features and Management

The cervical fusion itself is rarely symptomatic in childhood. The principal clinical concerns are: (1) progressive degenerative changes in the unfused segments adjacent to the fused segments, occurring decades earlier than in the normal cervical spine because of increased motion demands and biomechanical loading (Bridwell summarizes that with C2–C3 fusion, typical presentation with disc disease at C3–C4 occurs at age 30–45 rather than the usual 50–65); (2) C2–C3 fusion with adjacent occipito-C1 instability, which can produce catastrophic neurological injury after relatively minor trauma (notable morbidity has been described in patients with the "double anomaly" of Klippel-Feil plus odontoid hypoplasia); (3) associated scoliosis requiring management; and (4) management of associated systemic anomalies.

The clinical implications for the orthopedic surgeon are: avoidance of high-impact activities at risk for cervical injury (high-risk patients — particularly those with C2–C3 fusion combined with occipito-C1 abnormality, and those with the long fusions over three segments described by Hensinger — should be counseled to avoid contact sports and high-speed activities); careful preoperative airway assessment in patients requiring general anesthesia (intubation may be difficult or impossible without fiberoptic guidance because of the restricted cervical motion, with awake fiberoptic intubation recommended in complex cases); imaging of the entire spine for associated congenital anomalies; and renal and cardiac evaluation. Surgical management is reserved for symptomatic adjacent-segment degeneration or instability and follows the principles of cervical spine surgery in general. Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) for adjacent-segment degeneration, and posterior occipitocervical or C1–C2 arthrodesis for upper-cervical instability, are the most commonly applied procedures.

## Sprengel Deformity

Sprengel deformity, described by Otto Sprengel (Grimma, Germany, 1891) and often associated with Klippel-Feil syndrome (~30% of cases), is the congenital failure of normal descent of the scapula from the cervical region to its definitive thoracic position. Normal scapular descent occurs between weeks 9 and 12 of gestation; failure of this process produces a scapula that is elevated, small, often medially rotated, and may be connected to the cervical vertebrae by a fibrous, cartilaginous, or osseous bar — the omovertebral bone (present in approximately 25% of cases). Presentation includes an elevated shoulder, neck asymmetry with "fullness" of the trapezial triangle, and limited shoulder abduction (typically <120° vs. the normal 180°). Imaging — particularly CT with three-dimensional reconstruction — defines the position of the scapula and the presence of the omovertebral bone.

The Cavendish classification grades the deformity clinically: Grade I — very mild (scapula barely palpable, minimal cosmetic impact); Grade II — mild (scapula palpable but not visible in clothing, asymmetry visible with bare shoulders); Grade III — moderate (scapula elevated 2–5 cm above normal position, asymmetry visible in clothing); Grade IV — severe (scapula elevated close to the occiput, marked cosmetic deformity).

Surgical treatment is indicated for severe symptomatic Sprengel (Cavendish III–IV). Three procedures are classically described: the Woodward procedure (Wisconsin, 1961) — release of the medial border scapular muscles (rhomboideus major, rhomboideus minor, levator scapulae) from their spinal attachments, inferior translation of the scapula, and reattachment of the muscles in a more inferior position; the Green procedure (1957) — similar, with additional scapular morcellation to reduce its bulk; and the Mears procedure with resection of the superomedial corner of the scapula for moderate deformities with minimal functional component. Tachdjian/Herring describe a modified Green procedure (Procedure 61 in *Tachdjian's Procedures*) that remains widely used. The procedures should be performed before age 8 because of the high risk of brachial plexus injury with attempted late correction (the brachial plexus tolerates traction during inferior translation poorly in skeletal maturity). Resection of the omovertebral bone, when present, is a critical step to free the scapula for inferior translation.

## Cervical Ribs and Thoracic Outlet Syndrome

## Cervical Ribs

A cervical rib is a supernumerary rib arising from the seventh cervical vertebra. The condition occurs in approximately 0.5–1.0% of the population and is bilateral in approximately 50% of carriers. The cervical rib varies from a small bony stump palpable in the supraclavicular fossa to a complete rib that articulates with the first thoracic rib, and is often connected to the first rib or to the manubrium sterni by a fibrous band. Most cervical ribs are asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally; the clinical importance lies in the small minority (~10% of carriers) that produce thoracic outlet syndrome.

## Thoracic Outlet Syndrome (TOS)

Thoracic outlet syndrome is the constellation of neurological, vascular, or combined symptoms produced by compression of the brachial plexus, subclavian artery, or subclavian vein as they pass through the thoracic outlet between the cervical spine and the upper rib cage. Three principal subtypes are recognized.

Neurogenic TOS (the commonest, accounting for >90% of cases) involves compression of the lower trunk of the brachial plexus (C8 and T1 fibers) against a cervical rib, an anomalous fibrous band (Roos types), or the scalene muscles (scalene triangle syndrome). Presentation includes pain and paresthesia in the medial aspect of the arm and the ulnar nerve distribution of the hand, weakness of the intrinsic hand muscles, and occasionally atrophy. The classical clinical sign is wasting of the thenar eminence ("Gilliatt-Sumner hand") with preservation of the hypothenar musculature in advanced disease — a pattern reflecting predominant compression of the T1 motor fibers to the median nerve. Diagnostic provocative maneuvers include the Roos test (the patient holds the arms in the "stick-up" position with elbows flexed for 3 minutes, with reproduction of symptoms indicating a positive test), the Adson maneuver (loss of radial pulse on inspiration with head turned to the affected side), the Wright test (hyperabduction), and the military-position test; these tests have substantial false-positive rates in normal subjects (Adson — up to 50% in healthy individuals). Nerve conduction studies (decreased ulnar F-wave velocity, fixed slowing in the lower trunk), MRI of the brachial plexus, and CT angiography refine the diagnosis.

Vascular TOS involves compression of the subclavian artery (arterial TOS, often producing a post-stenotic aneurysm with distal embolization — the "blue finger" syndrome) or vein (venous TOS, producing the Paget-Schroetter syndrome of effort-induced subclavian vein thrombosis in young athletic patients, described in the late 19th century by Paget and Schroetter). Vascular TOS is uncommon but consequential, often requiring urgent thrombolysis followed by decompressive surgery.

Treatment of TOS is initially conservative for the great majority of cases: physiotherapy directed at posture correction, scalene stretching, and strengthening of the lower trapezius and serratus anterior; ergonomic modifications; and weight loss. Surgical decompression is reserved for refractory cases or for vascular TOS with established arterial or venous compromise. Surgical options include cervical rib resection (the transaxillary approach described by Roos is classical; the supraclavicular approach gives better access to the brachial plexus), first rib resection, scalenectomy, and combinations of these. Outcomes are variable, with the best results in true neurogenic TOS with objective findings (motor weakness, EMG abnormalities); patients with subjective sensory symptoms only often have disappointing surgical results, and preoperative selection remains a clinical challenge.

## Spina Bifida and the Dysraphic States

## Definitions and Classification

The dysraphic states are a spectrum of congenital malformations of the spinal cord, vertebral column, and overlying tissues, resulting from failure of normal closure of the neural tube between days 22 and 28 of gestation. The principal entities are:

Spina bifida occulta: A bony defect in the posterior vertebral arch (typically L5 or S1) without external evidence of neurological involvement. Often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on radiograph (~10% of lumbosacral radiographs). May be associated with overlying skin findings — hair patch, dimple, hemangioma, lipoma, abnormal dermal sinus — that signal underlying spinal cord tethering and warrant MRI investigation.

Meningocele: A herniation of the meninges through the bony defect, containing cerebrospinal fluid but not neural elements. Typically presents with a sac protruding through the back; neurological function is usually preserved. Surgical closure in the neonatal period is the standard.

Myelomeningocele: A herniation of the meninges and neural elements (spinal cord, nerve roots) through the bony defect, with severe neurological consequences. The neural placode lies open on the surface of the back in the newborn period. Akbarnia devotes Chapter 14 entirely to myelomeningocele, reflecting the complexity of its orthopedic management.

Tethered cord syndrome: Abnormally low position and fixation of the conus medullaris (below the L1–L2 level), often associated with a thickened or fatty filum terminale, intradural lipoma, dermal sinus tract, or split-cord malformation. Mechanical tension on the conus produces progressive neurological deterioration.

Diastematomyelia (split-cord malformation): Splitting of the spinal cord into two hemicords, often divided by a bony or fibrocartilaginous bar (Pang Type I) or by a fibrous septum alone (Pang Type II). The bony type requires surgical release before scoliosis correction.

Myelocystocele, myeloschisis, and other rare variants complete the spectrum.

## Myelomeningocele

Myelomeningocele is the most consequential of the dysraphic states from an orthopedic standpoint. The condition arises from failure of neural tube closure at approximately day 28 of gestation; multiple causes are implicated, with folate deficiency the most easily prevented. Prevalence has fallen dramatically with widespread folate fortification of flour (30–70% reduction in countries that introduced mandatory fortification after 1992), but the condition remains an important cause of childhood disability.

The clinical level of neurological deficit corresponds approximately to the anatomical level of the lesion: thoracic, high lumbar (L1–L2), middle lumbar (L3–L4), low lumbar (L5), and sacral lesions produce different patterns of motor and sensory loss. The Hoffer (Sharrard's level) classification defines functional prognosis: patients with thoracic and high lumbar lesions typically do not achieve community ambulation; those with middle and low lumbar lesions can ambulate with various orthoses (HKAFO, KAFO); those with sacral lesions usually achieve community ambulation but often with characteristic foot deformities.

The orthopedic manifestations of myelomeningocele include: (1) hip deformities — hip dislocation in approximately 50% of midlumbar lesions (L3–L4 level), due to muscle imbalance between flexors-adductors (innervated, spastic) and extensors-abductors (denervated); hip dislocation at thoracic or high-lumbar level is stable and not subject to reduction, whereas unilateral dislocation at L3–L4 in a child with potential for functional ambulation may be reduced; (2) knee deformities — typically flexion contractures or quadriceps weakness depending on level; (3) foot deformities — clubfoot, calcaneus, equinus, valgus, vertical talus, all of variable severity and often requiring serial corrections in childhood; (4) scoliosis — paralytic, neuromuscular, often progressive into adulthood, affecting a large proportion of patients with high lesions (>90% with thoracic level vs. <20% with sacral); (5) hydrocephalus and Arnold-Chiari type II malformation — the latter almost universal in myelomeningocele and producing cerebellar tonsillar herniation; (6) tethered cord — symptomatic in many patients in late childhood and adolescence, requiring untethering surgery; (7) latex allergy — substantially elevated incidence (~30–70%), requiring latex-free environments throughout medical and surgical care; (8) neurogenic bladder and bowel — requiring lifelong management with clean intermittent catheterization, antimuscarinic medication, and sometimes bladder augmentation; (9) pressure sores — from insensate skin, especially over bony prominences; (10) reduced life expectancy and complex medical comorbidity.

Orthopedic management is highly individualized, focuses on functional goals (sitting balance, transfers, ambulation, prevention of pressure sores), and must be coordinated with the broader multidisciplinary team of neurosurgeons, urologists, physiatrists, and orthotists. Surgical priorities include: closure of the neural defect in the first 24–72 hours of life (typically by neurosurgery, with later orthopedic involvement; modern fetal surgery under the MOMS protocol — Management of Myelomeningocele Study, NEJM 2011 — has shown improved neurologic outcomes in selected cases); management of hydrocephalus by ventriculoperitoneal shunt; orthopedic surgery for hip, knee, foot, and spine deformities staged according to functional priorities and the child's growth. Surgical treatment of neuromuscular scoliosis in myelomeningocele is among the most complex and risky orthopedic procedures, with high rates of infection (10–20%), nonunion, and instrumentation failure; extension to the sacrum or pelvis (Galveston, Dunn-McCarthy, or iliac screws) is mandatory because of lack of pelvic stability.

## Tethered Cord

Tethered cord syndrome presents with progressive neurological deterioration in a child or adult with prior dysraphic findings or with subtle dorsal cutaneous findings (hairy patch, dimple, hemangioma, dermal sinus, pigmented nevus). Clinical manifestations include progressive lower-extremity weakness, sensory changes, urinary or bowel dysfunction (increasing incontinence, recurrent urinary infections, encopresis), and back or leg pain, often worsening with flexion. MRI is diagnostic, showing the conus medullaris below the L1–L2 level with associated findings of dermal sinus, lipoma, thickened filum terminale (>2 mm thick), or other tethering structure. Treatment is surgical untethering, with detachment of the conus from the tethering structure. Outcomes are best when surgery is performed before significant neurological deterioration has occurred — reversal of established deficit is inconsistent.

## Other Notable Congenital Spine Anomalies

## Atlanto-occipital Anomalies

Atlanto-occipital assimilation (fusion of the atlas to the occiput), basilar invagination (upward displacement of the dens through the foramen magnum), Down syndrome with associated atlantoaxial instability (~15% of patients), and other craniovertebral junction anomalies may produce upper cervical instability and neurological compromise. The conditions are often associated with skeletal dysplasias (achondroplasia with small foramen magnum and risk of sudden infant death, Morquio syndrome with odontoid dysplasia, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia with C1–C2 instability) and require careful neurosurgical assessment with dynamic flexion/extension radiographs, CT, and MRI. When instability with neurologic symptoms or ADI (atlanto-dental interval) >5 mm in a child is established, occipitocervical or C1–C2 arthrodesis is performed.

## Os Odontoideum

Os odontoideum is a separated ossicle replacing the normal dens, with potential atlantoaxial instability. The condition may be congenital (hypothesized by Fielding as failure of fusion between the os and the body of C2) or post-traumatic (the more modern hypothesis — following missed odontoid fracture in childhood with subsequent nonunion and a separate ossicle forming in its proximal position). Radiographically, two forms are distinguished: orthotopic (the ossicle is in the normal anatomic position of the dens) and dystopic (the ossicle is displaced, often attached to the clivus). Symptomatic instability with myelopathy or ADI >5 mm is treated by C1–C2 fusion or occipitocervical arthrodesis using the Magerl technique (transarticular screw) or the Harms-Goel technique (C1 lateral mass and C2 pedicle screws).

## Lumbosacral Transitional Vertebrae

Sacralization of L5 (partial or complete) and lumbarization of S1 are common variants, mostly asymptomatic but occasionally associated with low back pain (Bertolotti syndrome, described by the Italian radiologist Mario Bertolotti in 1917, in which an enlarged transverse process of L5 articulates with the sacrum and produces focal pain). The Castellvi classification grades the anomalies: Type I — dysplastic transverse process without articulation; Type II — diarthrosis between an enlarged transverse process and the sacrum (unilateral or bilateral); Type III — complete bony fusion; Type IV — mixed. Therapeutic options for symptomatic Bertolotti syndrome include local injections with anesthetic and corticosteroid and, in refractory cases, resection of the pseudoarticulation.

## Local Contributions and International Authors by Country

The field of congenital spinal anomalies has developed through contributions from surgeons and investigators in many countries. The following list orders key contributions by the country where they were initially performed, without claiming to be exhaustive:

Bulgaria: The Bulgarian orthopedic school of the mid-twentieth century, represented by Boycho Boychev, Yordan Holevich, and their successors in *Surgical Orthopaedics* (Boycho Boychev, Yordan Holevich), includes original descriptions of operative techniques for neuromuscular and paralytic spinal deformities reflecting clinical practice in the poliomyelitis era. More recent authors from orthopedic clinics in Sofia and Plovdiv have contributed to the adaptation of VEPTR and magnetically controlled growing rods in central and eastern Europe in series published since 2010.

Canada: Hospital for Sick Children (Toronto) — contributions of Roy-Camille and associated Canadian authors in posterior osteotomies and pedicle fixation; Salter and his trainees — the general concept of early surgical intervention for childhood deformities.

France: Klippel and Feil (Paris, 1912) — the original description of the syndrome; Stagnara (Lyon) — methodology for three-dimensional assessment of scoliosis; Cotrel and Dubousset (Berck-sur-Mer, Saint-Vincent-de-Paul, Paris) — Cotrel-Dubousset instrumentation, which revolutionized scoliosis correction in the late 1980s; Mazda — pediatric hemivertebra excision.

Germany: Otto Sprengel (Grimma, 1891) — the original description of scapular elevation; Harms (Karlsbad) — foundational contributions in thoracoscopic anterior approaches and hemivertebra excision via the posterior approach; Niemeyer — contributions to three-column osteotomies for severe kyphosis; Dietz (Berlin) — modern publications on MAGEC technology.

India: Rajasekaran (Coimbatore) — large series on congenital kyphosis and scoliosis with long-term follow-up; Shetty — techniques for hemivertebra excision in resource-limited settings.

Italy: Bertolotti (1917) — original description of the lumbosacral transitional vertebra and associated pain; Risser (Italian origin before emigration to the USA) — the skeletal-maturity classification applied to surgical indication. Modern institutions in Bologna and Milan have contributed substantially to VEPTR and MAGEC surgery.

Japan: Kawakami (Meijo Hospital, Nagoya) — the chapter on congenital scoliosis in Akbarnia's *The Growing Spine* (2nd ed.) and extensive clinical series; Yamazaki — VCR techniques; Suzuki — contributions in adult posterior osteotomies. The Japanese spinal school is particularly well represented in Akbarnia *The Growing Spine* and Bridwell's *Textbook of Spinal Surgery*.

South Korea: Suk (Seoul) — the vertebral column resection technique for severe fixed deformities; Lee and Cho — contributions in post-traumatic deformity and oscillating correction.

Turkey: Yazici (Hacettepe University, Ankara) — the single-stage posterior hemivertebra resection, which became an international standard; Alanay (Acibadem, Istanbul) — growth-preserving techniques and magnetically controlled growing rods.

United Kingdom: McMaster (Royal Hospital for Sick Children, Edinburgh) — the foundational series of 251 children with >5-year follow-up (JBJS 1982), establishing the classification and natural history of congenital scoliosis that remains the clinical standard; James (Robert James, Royal National Orthopaedic Hospital, Stanmore) — early descriptions of infantile and juvenile scoliosis; Mehta (Royal National Orthopaedic Hospital) — the method of serial elongation-derotation-flexion casting for early-onset scoliosis, applied to selected congenital cases; Tucker (Bristol) — biomechanical investigations of early-onset scoliosis.

United States: Winter and Lonstein (Minneapolis, Twin Cities Spine Center) — the historical series on congenital scoliosis and kyphosis that established the basic surgical principles in the mid-twentieth century; Hensinger (Newington Hospital, Connecticut) — the classic 1974 paper on Klippel-Feil syndrome; Akbarnia (San Diego) — the concept and development of growing rods for early-onset scoliosis, including congenital; Campbell (San Antonio, Texas) — the VEPTR construct for thoracic insufficiency syndrome; Lenke (St. Louis) — the vertebral column resection technique and modern scoliosis classifications; Boachie-Adjei (Hospital for Special Surgery, New York) — complex reconstructive spinal surgery in developing countries (FOCOS, Ghana); Bridwell (St. Louis) — *The Textbook of Spinal Surgery* and pedicle subtraction osteotomy; Newton (Rady Children's, San Diego) — Harms Study Group and modern hemivertebra excision protocols.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

The congenital anomalies of the spine span a remarkable spectrum from minor radiographic variants to devastating myelomeningocele. Congenital scoliosis, classified as failures of formation (hemivertebrae), failures of segmentation (block vertebrae, unilateral unsegmented bars), or mixed defects, requires careful assessment of progression risk according to the McMaster classification; unilateral unsegmented bars combined with contralateral hemivertebrae are the most progressive and warrant early surgical intervention. Associated anomalies — spinal cord (MRI mandatory!), renal, cardiac, and others — must be sought systematically (VACTERL screening). The surgical options span hemivertebra resection (Yazici, Akbarnia), convex hemiepiphysiodesis, in-situ fusion, and modern growth-friendly implants (Akbarnia growing rods, Campbell's VEPTR, MAGEC), culminating in definitive correction and instrumented fusion at skeletal maturity.

Klippel-Feil syndrome of cervical vertebral fusion produces the classical triad of short neck, low hairline, and limited motion, but the principal clinical concerns are adjacent-segment instability (especially with C2–C3 fusion), scoliosis, Sprengel deformity, and renal, cardiac, and auditory anomalies (Hensinger 1974). Cervical ribs are common and usually asymptomatic but can produce thoracic outlet syndrome with characteristic neurological, vascular, or combined manifestations.

The dysraphic states — spina bifida occulta, meningocele, myelomeningocele, and tethered cord syndromes — produce a wide range of orthopedic, neurosurgical, and medical manifestations that require lifelong multidisciplinary management. Across all of these conditions, the principles of early recognition, systematic investigation for associated anomalies, staged surgical intervention according to functional priorities, and coordinated multidisciplinary care produce the best outcomes. The recommended reading list includes Akbarnia *The Growing Spine* (Springer 2016), Moe/Lonstein *Textbook of Scoliosis*, Bridwell *Textbook of Spinal Surgery*, Newton/Samdani/Harms (Harms Study Group), and Tachdjian's *Pediatric Orthopaedics*.
