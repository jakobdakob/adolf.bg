## Introduction and Definitions

Scoliosis is a lateral curvature of the spine in the coronal plane combined with rotation in the axial plane and often with deviation from the physiological sagittal profile, producing the characteristic three-dimensional deformity that distinguishes true structural scoliosis from a postural lateral lean or an antalgic list of radicular origin. The condition encompasses a heterogeneous group of disorders with different etiologies, natural histories, and treatments. In the contemporary Scoliosis Research Society (SRS) classification, the principal categories are: idiopathic scoliosis, divided by age of onset into infantile (0–3 years), juvenile (4–9 years), and adolescent (10–18 years) forms; congenital scoliosis (discussed in detail in the preceding chapter on congenital deformities); neuromuscular scoliosis arising from underlying neurological or muscular disease; the syndromic scolioses associated with connective-tissue disorders, skeletal dysplasias, and other systemic conditions; and adult scoliosis — the persistence or new development of curve in skeletal maturity. This chapter synthesizes content from Moe's Textbook of Scoliosis and Other Spinal Deformities (Lonstein, Bradford, Winter, Ogilvie), Idiopathic Scoliosis: The Harms Study Group Treatment Guide (Newton, Samdani, Shufflebarger), Spinal Deformity — Déformations Du Rachis of Pierre Stagnara, The Growing Spine of Akbarnia, Yazici and Thompson, The Textbook of Spinal Surgery of Bridwell and DeWald, Tachdjian's Pediatric Orthopaedics of Herring, Lovell and Winter's Pediatric Orthopaedics 8th edition, Apley & Solomon's, and Miller's Review. The chapter focuses primarily on idiopathic scoliosis (the commonest form) and on neuromuscular and adult scoliosis, with cross-reference to the preceding chapter for congenital disease.

## Anatomy, Biomechanics, and Pathoanatomy of the Deformity

The scoliotic spine is not simply a laterally curved normal column but a three-dimensionally deformed structure in which the coronal curve is accompanied by axial rotation of the vertebrae toward the convexity, rib rotation that creates the rib hump on the convex side in the thoracic region, lumbar paraspinal prominence on the convex side in lumbar curves, and a characteristic deviation from the physiological sagittal profile — typically thoracic hypokyphosis or even lordosis ("flat thoracic curve") and reduced lumbar lordosis. Pierre Stagnara in Lyon formalized the concept of the "plane of maximum deformity" (plan d'élection), describing that the true apex and maximum deformity lie in a plane oblique to both the coronal and sagittal, and that imaging assessment should be performed in both standard planes and in that oblique plane — a concept that underpins modern three-dimensional thinking about scoliosis.

The pathoanatomy of the structural curve includes wedge-shaped deformity of the vertebral bodies with greater height on the convex side, vertebral body rotation with the concave-side pedicle displaced posteriorly and laterally (visible on the PA projection as asymmetric pedicle position), asymmetry of the disc (taller on the convex side in flexible curves, progressively narrowed in degenerative curves), rib deformity with more vertical ribs on the concave side and more horizontal ribs on the convex side in the thoracic region, and characteristic chest-wall deformity with narrowing on the concave side and expansion on the convex side. The rib hump on the convex side is the result of segmental rotation, not of isolated rib deformity; it becomes structural and fixed with disease progression.

Biomechanically, scoliotic deformity progresses by the Hueter-Volkmann principle — the asymmetric loading of vertebral body growth plates, in which the concave side is under increased compressive load, suppresses growth on the concave side and promotes continued growth on the convex side, producing a vicious cycle of progressively increasing wedge deformity. This principle is the basis both for understanding the natural history of scoliosis in the growing spine and for the treatment strategies that aim to reverse the biomechanical loading (bracing, scoliosis-specific exercises) or surgically modulate growth (growing rods, concave-convex tenodeses, vertebral body tethering).

## Classification of Scolioses

The etiologic classification of the Scoliosis Research Society distinguishes: (1) idiopathic scoliosis — infantile (onset 0–3 years), juvenile (4–9 years), and adolescent (10–18 years); (2) congenital scoliosis — failure of formation (hemivertebra, wedge vertebra), failure of segmentation (unsegmented block, unilateral bar), or mixed; (3) neuromuscular scoliosis — neuropathic (upper motor neuron — cerebral palsy, spinal cord injury, syringomyelia; lower motor neuron — poliomyelitis, spinal muscular atrophy) or myopathic (muscular dystrophies, arthrogryposis); (4) scoliosis in syndromes and connective-tissue disorders — Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos, osteogenesis imperfecta, neurofibromatosis type 1, skeletal dysplasias; (5) posttraumatic scoliosis — after fracture, dislocation, laminectomy, or after thoracotomy in childhood; (6) scoliosis after infection or tumor resection; (7) adult scoliosis — adult idiopathic scoliosis (persistent from adolescence) and de novo (degenerative) scoliosis.

This classification has direct therapeutic significance: each type has a characteristic natural history, imaging features, risks of progression, and treatment options, and the contemporary clinician must establish the etiologic category before formulating a treatment plan.

## Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis — Epidemiology

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is by far the commonest form of scoliosis, affecting approximately 2–3% of the adolescent population to some degree (curves >10°), with curves significant enough to require treatment (>30°) occurring in approximately 0.3–0.5% of adolescents. The condition predominantly affects girls, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 1.5:1 for curves above 10° and approximately 8:1 for curves requiring treatment (>30°). The right thoracic curve is the typical pattern; left thoracic curves are atypical and should raise suspicion of intraspinal pathology. The peak age of presentation is 11–14 years in girls and 12–15 years in boys, corresponding to the adolescent growth spurt during which curve progression is most rapid. Familial aggregation is significant — children of patients with AIS have a risk approximately 7 times higher than the general population, and twin studies demonstrate significant concordance between monozygotic twins, higher than between dizygotic.

## Etiology and Pathogenesis

The etiology of idiopathic scoliosis remains unclear, and the term "idiopathic" itself reflects this gap in understanding. Multiple theories have been proposed and the accumulated evidence supports a multifactorial model.

Genetic susceptibility is well established. Family and twin studies have demonstrated a substantial genetic component with estimated heritability of 40–80%. Multiple loci have been identified through GWAS studies, including variants in LBX1, GPR126, PAX1, ADGRG6, and BNC2; rare variants in connective-tissue and fibrillin-2 genes have also been identified. However, there is no single major gene — the disease follows a polygenic model with multiple contributing loci of small effect, potentially interacting with environmental factors.

Hormonal factors have been a long-standing area of investigation. The female predominance and the timing of peak progression at puberty implicate hormonal regulation. The melatonin deficiency hypothesis (Machida) — based on experimental models of pinealectomized chickens that develop scoliosis — was influential but has not been consistently confirmed in human series. Leptin, estrogen receptors, and vitamin D have all been studied. The pubertal hormonal regulation more likely reflects a temporal window of maximum growth velocity rather than a specific pathogenic mechanism.

Biomechanical factors — asymmetric loading of the immature spine in the frontal and sagittal planes — interact with the Hueter-Volkmann principle to maintain and progress the deformity. The growth-spurt-related acceleration of progression is well documented and supports the biomechanical hypothesis.

Neurological factors — subclinical brainstem abnormalities, proprioceptive dysfunctions, postural control asymmetry, and abnormalities of vestibulo-visual integration — have been demonstrated in selected series. Burwell's concept of disturbed neuroaxial growth (a mismatch between spinal cord and vertebral column growth) offers one explanatory framework.

Connective-tissue factors — asymmetry of paraspinal muscle fibers, abnormalities of collagen and the elastin network of the annulus — have been documented, although their interpretation (causal or secondary to deformity) remains controversial.

The current consensus is that AIS is a multifactorial condition with multiple contributing factors operating in genetically susceptible individuals within a specific growth window.

## Clinical Presentation and Physical Examination

Most AIS is detected by school screening, by parents noticing asymmetry (uneven shoulders, prominent scapula, unequal waist crease in swimwear), or during routine medical examinations. Symptoms in adolescence are typically minimal — mild back pain is reported in 20–30% of patients, a rate similar to that in normal adolescents; severe back pain, especially nocturnal, and neurological complaints should raise suspicion of an alternative diagnosis (intraspinal tumor, syringomyelia, spondylolisthesis, discitis).

The classical clinical features are: asymmetric shoulder height with the shoulder elevated on the convex side of the thoracic curve; asymmetric scapular prominence with the convex-side scapula more prominent; waist asymmetry with the concave-side waist crease appearing more concave; breast asymmetry in older adolescents; and the most reliable sign — the rib hump (or lumbar hump) seen on Adams's forward bend test.

Adams's forward bend test is the gold standard physical examination for scoliosis screening. The patient stands with feet together, knees straight, and arms hanging freely, and bends forward toward the floor with gradual flexion of the spine from the neck down to the pelvis. The examiner views from behind in a tangential position and observes for asymmetric prominence of the ribs (thoracic curve) or paraspinal muscles (thoracolumbar or lumbar curve). The scoliometer — a simple inclinometer placed transversely over the apex of the rib hump — quantifies the rotational deformity (angle of trunk rotation, ATR); a measurement of 7° or greater warrants further radiographic evaluation, while values <5° are generally not associated with a clinically significant curve.

Additional elements of the physical examination include: assessment of the sagittal profile (hypokyphosis in typical thoracic AIS, kyphosis or flat spine); assessment of pelvic obliquity and limb length inequality (apparent scoliosis from limb-length inequality does not show rotation on Adams's test and is corrected by leveling the pelvis); neurological examination — reflexes, strength, cutaneous sensation, abdominal cutaneous reflexes (an asymmetric abdominal reflex is a sensitive sign for intraspinal pathology, especially syringomyelia); assessment of joint hypermobility and stigmata of connective-tissue disorders (Marfanoid habitus, skin extensibility, scars, blue sclerae, arachnodactyly); and examination of the skin for café-au-lait spots, axillary freckling, or other stigmata of neurofibromatosis type 1.

Accurate assessment of skeletal maturity at the time of examination is critical for prognosis and treatment planning. Tanner stages of pubertal development, the menarcheal status in girls, height, and peak growth velocity from anthropometric measurements provide a clinical assessment of pubertal stage.

## Imaging and Measurement

The standard radiographic assessment of scoliosis is a long-cassette posterior-anterior (PA) standing whole-spine radiograph and a lateral standing whole-spine radiograph. PA rather than AP projection is used to reduce the radiation dose to breast tissue and the thyroid gland in the predominantly female adolescent population — a reduction of approximately 7- to 10-fold for these tissues. Radiographs are obtained in neutral position, without shoes, with the gaze straight ahead and the arms relaxed or placed on the clavicles.

The Cobb angle is the standard quantitative measurement. A line is drawn along the superior endplate of the most-tilted upper end vertebra and along the inferior endplate of the most-tilted lower end vertebra of the curve, perpendiculars are erected to these lines, and the angle between the perpendiculars is the Cobb angle. The end vertebrae are those most tilted into the concavity of the curve (i.e., whose endplates have the greatest tilt relative to the horizontal). Reliable measurement requires consistent identification of the same end vertebrae; the inter- and intraobserver variability is approximately 3–5° for experienced observers, so clinically significant progression is defined as an increase of at least 5°. Beyond the Cobb angle, the assessment must include: vertebral rotation (by Nash-Moe, based on pedicle position relative to the lateral vertebral body borders, on a 0–4 scale; or more accurately by Perdriolle with a torsion meter); sagittal parameters (T5–T12 thoracic kyphosis, T12–L1 local kyphosis, L1–S1 lumbar lordosis); pelvic parameters (pelvic tilt, pelvic incidence, sacral slope); and the coronal and sagittal balance (plumb line from C7 relative to the central sacral vertical line — CSVL).

The Risser sign, evaluated from the iliac crest apophysis, is the principal radiographic indicator of skeletal maturity. The iliac apophysis ossifies from lateral to medial, with Risser 0 (no apophysis visible), Risser 1 (25% of the crest), Risser 2 (50%), Risser 3 (75%), Risser 4 (complete excursion), and Risser 5 (fusion of the apophysis to the iliac crest). Risser 0–2 indicates substantial remaining growth potential and high progression risk; Risser 4–5 indicates near-complete skeletal maturity.

The Sanders skeletal maturity classification, based on hand and wrist radiographs, provides finer resolution of skeletal maturity, particularly useful in distinguishing the pre-peak from the post-peak growth velocity periods. The Sanders scale (stages 1–8) correlates with peak height velocity and with the risk of curve progression better than the Risser sign; Sanders 1–3 correspond to the pre-peak period with high progression risk, Sanders 4 to the peak, Sanders 5–6 to the post-peak deceleration phase, and Sanders 7–8 to near-complete skeletal maturity.

Other key imaging parameters include the status of the triradiate cartilage of the acetabulum (closed at Risser 4–5), the status of the apophysis of the olecranon, the height of the ischial tubercle, and the proportion between seated and standing height from anthropometric assessment.

Curve flexibility is evaluated by dynamic projections: bending films from the left and right (lateral bending films) demonstrate correction on passive or active lateral flexion and form the basis for distinguishing structural from non-structural curves; traction films (under anesthesia or under light traction) give an idea of passive flexibility; push-prone films and fulcrum bending films identify focally flexible segments. These films are essential for surgical planning and for the Lenke classification.

MRI is not required for typical adolescent idiopathic scoliosis but should be obtained for atypical features that suggest an underlying condition: left thoracic curves; atypical neurological findings (asymmetric abdominal reflex, hyperreflexia, upper motor neuron findings, palm asymmetry); painful curves, especially with nocturnal awakening or systemic symptoms; juvenile-onset curves; rapidly progressing curves (>10° in 6 months); and curves in boys (in whom intraspinal pathology is more common than in the typical female adolescent population). Common MRI findings include syringomyelia, tethered cord, Chiari malformation type I, diastematomyelia, and intraspinal tumor. Systematic series demonstrate intraspinal pathology in 4–8% of patients with "idiopathic" scoliosis, justifying the low threshold for MRI in atypical presentations.

Computed tomography with three-dimensional reconstruction is reserved for specific situations — before complex revision surgery, for planning of osteotomies, in congenital anomalies with complex anatomy — because of the significant radiation dose. The EOS system for biplanar simultaneous recording of PA and lateral projection of a standing patient with three-dimensional reconstruction and significantly reduced radiation dose is a modern alternative that is increasingly used in specialized centers.

## King-Moe and Lenke Classifications

The King-Moe classification (1983) is the historical classification of thoracic curves, describing five types based on the location of the thoracic and lumbar curve. Types I, II, III, IV, and V are described in detail and guided surgical planning for more than two decades with Harrington and Cotrel-Dubousset instrumentation. The principal limitations of the classification — insufficient interobserver reliability, limited applicability to lumbar and thoracolumbar curves, and lack of a sagittal component — motivated the development of a new system.

The Lenke classification (2001) of Lawrence Lenke and the Spinal Deformity Study Group is the current standard for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis. It classifies curves by three components: (1) Curve type (types 1–6) — based on which curves are structural: type 1 — major thoracic curve with non-structural lumbar; type 2 — double thoracic (upper thoracic and major thoracic both structural); type 3 — double major (structural thoracic and lumbar/thoracolumbar, with the thoracic larger); type 4 — triple major (all three curves structural); type 5 — thoracolumbar/lumbar (major with non-structural thoracic); type 6 — thoracolumbar/lumbar with major thoracic (major thoracolumbar/lumbar, but also structural thoracic). The structural criteria for the minor curve are: residual flexibility on bending >25° or specific sagittal criteria — thoracic kyphosis T5–T12 >20° or thoracolumbar kyphosis T10–L2 >20°. (2) Lumbar modifier (A, B, or C) based on the position of the lumbar curve relative to the central sacral vertical line (CSVL): A — CSVL passes between the pedicles of the stable vertebra; B — CSVL passes medial to the concave side of the pedicles of the apical lumbar vertebra; C — CSVL is completely lateral to the apical lumbar vertebra. (3) Thoracic sagittal modifier (-, N, or +) based on the T5–T12 sagittal profile: – (hypokyphosis <10°); N (normal kyphosis 10–40°); + (hyperkyphosis >40°).

The Lenke system has 42 possible combinations (6 types × 3 lumbar modifiers × 3 sagittal modifiers), has high inter- and intraobserver reliability, and provides clear surgical recommendations. Lenke 1A is the commonest combination — major structural thoracic curve with non-structural lumbar and a lumbar curve in which the CSVL passes through the stable vertebra; selective thoracic fusion is the standard for it. Lenke 5 (major thoracolumbar/lumbar) in appropriate patients can be treated with a selective anterior or posterior lumbar fusion with the potential to save spinal levels.

## Natural History of Idiopathic Scoliosis

The natural history of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is one of variable progression depending on curve magnitude, skeletal maturity, and curve pattern. The classic data of Lonstein and Carlson (1984) from Minneapolis established the risk of progression as a function of curve magnitude and skeletal maturity: curves under 20° in patients with Risser 0–1 progress in 22% of cases; curves 20–29° in the same group progress in 68%; curves over 30° at Risser 0–1 progress in over 90% of cases. In patients with Risser 2–4 the risk of progression is significantly lower for each curve size. Once skeletal maturity is reached (Risser 4–5), the risk of further significant progression in adolescence falls to less than 5%.

The natural history into adulthood is more complex and was defined by Weinstein and colleagues in Iowa, who followed a cohort of patients with untreated AIS for over 50 years (the so-called Iowa late-follow-up study). Curves over 50° at maturity tend to progress slowly over decades, with rates of 0.5–1° per year on average and substantial individual variability; curves under 30° at maturity typically remain stable; thoracolumbar and lumbar curves progress more rapidly in adulthood than isolated thoracic curves. Thoracic curves over 100° produce pulmonary restriction with significantly reduced vital capacity, increased risk of right-sided cardiac failure, and ultimately increased mortality from respiratory causes; curves over 50° in the thoracolumbar region may produce significant pain and disability in middle age. Cosmetic concern, pulmonary function, pain, and quality of life remain important considerations in long-term outcome.

## Treatment — Ladder of Therapeutic Options

The treatment of AIS follows a sliding scale based on curve magnitude, skeletal maturity, and progression risk.

Observation: Curves under 20–25° in skeletally immature patients and curves under 50° at skeletal maturity are followed serially with clinical and radiographic assessment at intervals of 4–12 months depending on growth status. The decision for observation rests not on static magnitude but on assessment of progression risk: small curves in skeletally mature patients can be followed less often, while small curves in a patient in the pre-peak growth period (Risser 0, Sanders 1–3) require closer follow-up.

## Bracing — Indications, Types, and Evidence

Bracing is the standard conservative treatment for flexible curves of 25–45° in skeletally immature patients (Risser 0–2). The goal of bracing is not correction of the existing curve but prevention or delay of progression below the surgical threshold (50°) until skeletal maturity is reached.

Different brace types have specific indications:

The Milwaukee brace (a cervicothoracolumbosacral orthosis, CTLSO), developed by Walter Blount and Albert Schmidt in Wisconsin in the 1940s, was the first modern scoliosis brace and continues to be used for high thoracic curves (apex above T7) or for significant hyperkyphosis. The brace contains a pelvic girdle, two posterior vertical bars, an anterior thoracic pad, and a neck ring with chin and occipital touch points. It provides active correction by patient pushback against compressive pads.

The Boston brace, developed by Bill Miller and Bertram Hall in Boston in the 1970s, is the most widely used TLSO in North America, with an upper boundary typically at T7–T8. It is a modular anterior-opening TLSO with standard modules that can be ordered and customized for the individual patient, with pads placed according to the apex and pattern of the curve.

The Charleston bending brace, developed in Charleston, South Carolina, is worn only at night and applies overcorrection to the lumbar curve. It is used primarily for lumbar curves up to 35° in prepubertal patients. Evidence for its efficacy compared with full-time TLSO wear is mixed.

The Providence brace, developed in Providence, Rhode Island, is another night-worn brace with an overcorrection principle.

The Chêneau brace (developed by Jacques Chêneau in France) and its derivatives — the Rigo-Chêneau and the modern three-dimensional braces — are based on the principles of three-dimensional correction, with asymmetric pads and expansion zones, and integrate the concepts of scoliosis-specific exercises (Schroth). These braces are gaining popularity in Europe and increasing acceptance in North America, with data for better in-brace correction than traditional Boston-type braces in selected curves.

The Wilmington brace and its derivative the Boston-Wilmington are anterior-opening TLSOs with principles similar to the Boston brace, used in the central and eastern United States.

The BrAIST trial (Bracing in Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis Trial, 2013, published in the New England Journal of Medicine by Weinstein, Dolan, and colleagues) provided the strongest evidence for the efficacy of bracing in idiopathic scoliosis. The trial was multicenter, partly randomized and partly observational (because of ethical concerns with full randomization to bracing vs. observation), with follow-up to skeletal maturity or surgical intervention. The principal results demonstrated that bracing significantly reduced the rate of progression to the surgical threshold (50°) compared with observation (72% vs. 48% success, defined as a curve <50° at maturity), with a clear dose-response relationship: more hours of brace wear produced better outcomes, with full-time wear (>18 hours daily, documented with temperature sensors in the brace) producing the best results. This was a landmark trial, because before it the efficacy of bracing was contested.

Adherence monitoring with temperature-sensitive sensors built into braces (for example iButton, Orthotimer) has clarified that real brace wear is significantly less than reported by families and that adherence is the principal determinant of efficacy. This has informed family counseling and has produced a more objective assessment of the treatment process.

Bracing does not reliably correct existing curves but rather slows or prevents further progression. Patients must be prepared for realistic expectations: the goal is to prevent surgical intervention, not to "cure" the curve. The psychosocial impact of bracing in adolescence requires attention and support.

## Surgical Treatment — Indications and Principles

Curves >45–50° in skeletally immature patients and curves >50° at skeletal maturity are typical surgical indications. The decision for surgery is based on assessment of progression risk, cosmetic concerns, pulmonary function, and patient preference, not on static measurement alone.

The standard surgical treatment is posterior spinal fusion with instrumentation. The goals of the operation are: three-dimensional correction of the deformity, rebalancing of the coronal and sagittal profile, achievement of solid bone fusion for long-term maintenance of correction, and optimization of pulmonary function and cosmesis. The selection of fusion levels follows several principles: the fusion should include all structural curves; the fusion should end at a stable vertebra (the vertebra most closely bisected by the central sacral vertical line); the fusion should end at a neutral vertebra (one without rotational asymmetry); and the fusion should end one or two levels above and below the structural curve to allow compensation. The Lenke classification guides these decisions for each curve type. Selective thoracic fusion in Lenke 1A with a flexible non-structural lumbar curve is well established and saves lumbar levels.

## Surgical Instrumentation — Historical and Contemporary

The historical evolution of surgical treatment is instructive. Before instrumentation, surgical treatment consisted of prolonged plaster immobilization and posterior bone grafting, described by Hibbs in 1911 in New York, achieving some fusion but no substantial correction of the deformity.

The Harrington system, developed by Paul Harrington in Houston, Texas in 1962, revolutionized surgical treatment. It consisted of a single distraction rod fixed with hooks at the upper and lower boundaries of the curve, providing distraction correction in the coronal plane. The standard adjunct to the Harrington rod was a long posterior fusion with autogenous iliac crest bone graft and postoperative immobilization in a cast or specialized brace (Risser cast) for months. The results were significant improvements in coronal correction compared with preceding methods, but there were several major limitations: single-point fixation with a high risk of hook pull-out; single-plane correction that led to loss of physiological thoracic kyphosis and produced a "flat-back" deformity; and lack of rotational correction. Late complications included low back pain from a flat sagittal profile and adjacent segment disease.

The Cotrel-Dubousset (CD) system, developed by Yves Cotrel and Jean Dubousset in Paris and introduced in 1984, was a quantum leap in instrumentation. It introduced segmental double-rod instrumentation with multiple hooks and/or sublaminar wires, allowing both distraction and compression along different segments and achieving simultaneous three-plane correction (coronal, sagittal, and rotational). The rod-derotation maneuver — straightening an initially laterally curved rod converts the lateral deformity into rotational correction — was a revolutionary contribution by Dubousset.

The Luque segmental instrumentation, developed by Eduardo Luque in Mexico City in 1976, used double L-shaped rods fixed with sublaminar wires at each segment, providing stable segmental fixation. This technique was particularly useful for neuromuscular scoliosis with long fusions to the pelvis. The Galveston modification of the Luque construct, developed by Allen and Ferguson in Galveston, Texas, included curved distal ends of the rods driven into the iliac wings, providing pelvic fixation without the need for screws.

Pedicle-screw constructs, introduced into widespread practice through the 1990s (with significant contributions from Suk in Korea, Roy-Camille in France, and others), have become the dominant standard. The pedicle screw provides three-column fixation (anterior, middle, and posterior columns) and substantially greater biomechanical stability than hooks or sublaminar wires. Advantages include greater corrective force, less need for external immobilization, shorter fusions in selective curves, and better rotational correction. Modern correction techniques combine direct vertebral derotation (DVD), segmental compression and distraction, and translational maneuvers to achieve three-dimensional correction.

The concept of direct vertebral derotation — placing a derotation axis on the apical screws and actively derotating the apex of the curve toward a neutral position — is a significant modern contribution, associated with Suk and colleagues in Korea, which allows significant improvement in rotational correction and reduction of the rib hump.

Anterior surgical approaches (anterior thoracoscopic or open thoracotomy with anterior instrumentation) were popular in the 1990s and early 2000s for selective fusion of thoracolumbar and lumbar curves, with the theoretical advantage of saving fusion levels; current practice has largely returned to posterior-only surgery with pedicle screw instrumentation, achieving comparable correction with greater versatility. Anterior thoracoscopic fusion has found a niche in selected cases.

Growing rods and related growth-friendly techniques are discussed in the section on infantile and juvenile scoliosis.

Vertebral body tethering (VBT), introduced as a non-fusion alternative by the group of Crawford and Lonner in the late 2010s, is a modulation technique in which a flexible polyethylene cord is fixed with lateral screws on the convex side of the thoracic or thoracolumbar curve, providing asymmetric growth suppression on the convex side and gradual correction via the Hueter-Volkmann principle. The technique is reserved for patients with residual growth (Sanders 2–4), flexible curves <65°, and an adequate sagittal profile. Long-term results are still accumulating, with promising short-term data but also reports of overcorrection, tether breakage, and ultimately conversion to fusion.

## Complications of Scoliosis Surgery

Specific complications of scoliosis surgery include:

Blood loss is substantial in major spinal surgery — typically 500–1500 mL for a standard posterior fusion for AIS, with greater loss for long fusions to the pelvis and for neuromuscular scoliosis. It is reduced by intraoperative cell salvage, antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid), hypotensive anesthesia (sustained mean arterial pressure 60–65 mmHg), prone positioning with minimal abdominal compression, and meticulous surgical technique.

Neurological injury — rare but devastating. Risk is monitored by intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM), including somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), motor evoked potentials (MEPs), and electromyography (EMG) from key muscles. The Stagnara wake-up test — developed by Pierre Stagnara in Lyon in the late 1960s — is the historical confirmation: intraoperative awakening of the patient and testing of voluntary motor activity after placement of the instrumentation. It remains applicable in specific cases even today. When a change in motor or sensory evoked potentials occurs, an intervention protocol is applied that includes: stopping the surgical maneuvers, checking anesthesia, raising arterial pressure, checking the instrumentation, and if the change persists — the wake-up test, partial release of the instrumentation, or its removal.

Pseudarthrosis and instrumentation failure — incidence 2–5% with pedicle-screw constructs in AIS, higher in neuromuscular and adult disease. May require revision surgery with additional fusion.

Infection — superficial (2–4%) and deep (1–3%) infection after posterior fusion. Late peri-instrumentation biofilm formation by slow-growing organisms (Cutibacterium acnes) is a specific challenge in delayed infections.

Adjacent-segment problems — proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) — occur in 20–40% of patients after long posterior fusion in adults, less commonly in adolescent surgery. May progress to proximal junctional failure (PJF) with mechanical failure. Prophylaxis includes selection of the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) at a stable level, preservation of paraspinal muscles and interspinous ligaments in the zone above the UIV, and optimization of sagittal balance.

The crankshaft phenomenon — described by Dubousset — is continued growth of the anterior vertebrae after posterior fusion in skeletally immature patients, producing late rotational and lateral deformity. It is reduced by delaying posterior fusion until later in growth (after Risser 1–2 in girls), by combining with anterior fusion (anterior apophyseodesis + posterior fusion), or by using segmental pedicle-screw constructs that provide better rotational stability.

Surgical mobility loss and chronic low back pain after long fusion are important long-term considerations, especially with fusions extending into the lumbar spine.

## Infantile Idiopathic Scoliosis

Infantile idiopathic scoliosis (onset 0–3 years) is the rarest form in North America but is more common in Europe and in the United Kingdom, where it was described in detail by Min Mehta in London. Most cases (approximately 80%) resolve spontaneously, but a proportion (about 20%) progress relentlessly and can reach severe deformity with pulmonary compromise.

The rib-vertebral angle difference (RVAD) of Mehta is the principal predictor of progression. The RVAD is measured from the rib-vertebral angles (between the line of the upper border of the apical vertebra and the line of the midpoint of the ribs on both sides): RVAD >20° on the convex side compared with the concave side at presentation predicts progressive disease with high sensitivity, while RVAD <20° typically predicts spontaneous resolution. Phase I (no rib overlap on the apical vertebra) and Phase II (overlap) are additional landmarks.

Treatment of progressive infantile scoliosis has historically been problematic because the small spine of an infant cannot accommodate definitive fusion without producing severe truncal disproportion at maturity, pulmonary compromise, and thoracic insufficiency syndrome. The Mehta serial casting technique — sequential application of elongation-derotation-flexion (EDF) casts on a traction table that mold the deformity over months with cast change every 2–3 months, followed by maintenance bracing — has produced excellent results in selected cases and in appropriate groups can achieve a durable curative effect.

Growth-friendly techniques are used when casting fails or when severe disease demands earlier intervention. They include: the vertical expandable prosthetic titanium rib (VEPTR), developed by Robert Campbell in San Antonio for thoracic insufficiency syndrome (including congenital deformities); traditional growing rods, introduced by Akbarnia and colleagues, with mechanical distraction every 6 months through an open approach; and magnetic growing rods (MAGEC, Magnetic Expansion Control), introduced by Cheung and Luk in Hong Kong, allowing non-invasive external magnetic distraction in outpatient conditions at intervals of 1–3 months. These techniques allow continued spinal growth with controlled deformity until later definitive fusion in late adolescence.

The concept of thoracic insufficiency syndrome (TIS) of Campbell unifies congenital and acquired deformities in which limitation of thoracic volume compromises lung growth and final pulmonary capacity. The three-dimensional chest-wall volume and lung area on MRI or pulmonary function tests are more significant parameters than the Cobb angle alone.

## Juvenile Idiopathic Scoliosis

Juvenile idiopathic scoliosis (onset 4–9 years) has features intermediate between the infantile and adolescent forms. The risk of progression is high because of the long residual growth, and the disease often reaches significant magnitude before treatment is started. Bracing is the main conservative intervention and in appropriate cases can significantly delay progression. Surgical treatment is delayed as long as possible, but when the curve progresses above the surgical threshold, growing-rod techniques or (more often) growth-friendly methods are applied to maintain spinal growth into late adolescence, followed by definitive fusion.

## Neuromuscular Scoliosis — Etiology and Pathogenesis

Neuromuscular scoliosis arises from underlying neurological or muscular conditions that compromise the muscular control of the spine. The SRS classification of neuromuscular scolioses divides etiologies into neuropathic (upper or lower motor neuron) and myopathic. The principal etiologies include:

Cerebral palsy (CP) is the commonest cause of neuromuscular scoliosis in current practice. The prevalence correlates with GMFCS (Gross Motor Function Classification System) level: GMFCS I–II — about 5%; GMFCS III — 10–25%; GMFCS IV — 50%; GMFCS V — 70% or more. Patients with tetraplegia or dystonia have higher rates.

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) — especially types II and III — produces progressive scoliosis in almost all cases. The introduction of new disease-modifying therapies (nusinersen, onasemnogene abeparvovec, risdiplam) from 2016 onward has substantially altered the natural history of SMA and accordingly the natural history of scoliosis in this population, though the ultimate implications for surgical treatment are still emerging.

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) — almost all patients who reach loss of ambulation (typically 9–13 years) develop progressive scoliosis.

Myelomeningocele (spina bifida) — scoliosis in this population is related to the level of involvement; patients with upper lumbar or thoracic involvement have the highest risk.

Friedreich ataxia, Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, and other hereditary neuropathies — rarer but well-documented causes of scoliosis.

Poliomyelitis — now historical in most regions because of vaccination programs, but remains a significant cause in areas with limited access to vaccination and in historical cohorts. Scoliosis from post-polio weakness was the principal model of neuromuscular scoliosis in the mid-twentieth century.

Traumatic spinal cord injury — especially in childhood; the risk of progressive scoliosis in a patient injured before 10 years of age is almost 100%.

Arthrogryposis, Rett syndrome, and other neurological and muscular conditions.

## Neuromuscular Scoliosis — Clinical Features and Assessment

Neuromuscular scoliosis differs from idiopathic scoliosis in several important respects: (1) the curve patterns are typically long C-shaped curves spanning the entire spine, with pelvic obliquity producing functional sitting difficulties and seating ulceration over the ischial tuberosities; (2) progression continues into and after skeletal maturity, in contrast to idiopathic scoliosis; (3) pulmonary compromise from chest-wall deformity is more frequent and more severe, often complicated by respiratory muscle weakness, kyphoscoliosis, and cardiomyopathy; (4) bone quality is often poor (steroid-related osteoporosis in DMD, disuse osteoporosis in non-ambulatory patients, vitamin D deficiency); (5) the underlying disease produces additional medical complexity that affects perioperative management (nutritional status, gastroesophageal reflux, epilepsy, aspiration risk).

Assessment requires a complex multidisciplinary approach involving orthopedic surgery, neurosurgery, neonatology/pediatrics, pulmonology, cardiology, gastroenterology, occupational therapy, and physical therapy. Imaging includes, beyond standard radiographs, cardiac echocardiography, pulmonary function tests (forced vital capacity, blood gases), nutritional assessment (albumin, prealbumin, BMI), and overnight oximetry in patients at risk of sleep-related respiratory failure.

## Neuromuscular Scoliosis — Treatment

Bracing has a limited role in neuromuscular scoliosis: it does not prevent progression of the underlying paralytic curve, but it can provide truncal support that improves sitting balance in non-ambulatory patients and can delay progression to a point when the patient is a more appropriate surgical candidate (greater age, greater height, optimized nutrition). Braces for neuromuscular scoliosis are typically soft or semirigid TLSOs with anterior and posterior shells.

The decision for surgery is based not on curve magnitude alone but on functional considerations including sitting balance, pulmonary function, pain, progression of the underlying disease, and caregiver burden. Surgery is indicated for curve progression above 50°, for significant pelvic obliquity causing sitting difficulty or ulceration, and for progressive loss of pulmonary function.

Surgical treatment requires longer fusion than for idiopathic scoliosis, often extending to the pelvis. Pelvic fixation is achieved by: iliac screws (standard since the 1990s), S2-alar-iliac (S2AI) screws (a more contemporary standard with better biomechanics and less screw-head prominence), or the Luque-Galveston technique with single or double rods (historical standard, still used in some centers). The principles of segmental instrumentation, attention to sagittal balance, and correction of pelvic obliquity are critical.

The complication rate in neuromuscular scoliosis is substantially higher than in idiopathic scoliosis: infection (10–20%), pulmonary complications (20–40% — pneumonia, prolonged ventilator support, atelectasis), gastrointestinal complications (paralytic ileus, superior mesenteric artery syndrome, gastroparesis), blood loss significantly greater than in idiopathic scoliosis, and the specific medical complications of the underlying disease. Thirty-day mortality is 0.5–3% depending on the underlying disease and severity.

Several specific conditions deserve special consideration:

Duchenne muscular dystrophy produces progressive scoliosis after loss of ambulation. The disease is also associated with cardiomyopathy and respiratory compromise. Surgical fusion is recommended in many centers when the curve reaches 20–30° in a patient who has become non-ambulatory; delaying surgery until the curve is larger increases risk because of progressive cardiopulmonary compromise. Fusion is typically T2–pelvis with segmental instrumentation. The introduction of corticosteroid therapy (prednisone, deflazacort) from the 1990s onward has somewhat altered the natural history, slowing the progression and delaying loss of ambulation, but the role of corticosteroids in delaying scoliosis is uncertain.

Spinal muscular atrophy types II and III produce progressive scoliosis. The use of newer disease-modifying therapies — nusinersen (antisense oligonucleotide), onasemnogene abeparvovec (AAV9 gene therapy), and risdiplam (oral SMN2 splicing modifier) — has substantially altered the natural history; the implications for scoliosis surgery in this population are still being defined, but early reports describe less aggressive scoliosis in patients treated early in life.

Cerebral palsy scoliosis ranges from mild curves in ambulatory patients to severe pelvic-oblique curves in non-ambulatory GMFCS V patients. The decision for surgery balances the functional improvement in sitting balance and care against the substantial perioperative risk and the underlying medical complexity. Long fusion T2–pelvis with pelvic fixation is the standard.

Myelomeningocele requires attention to specific problems including infected shunts, poor soft-tissue quality, tethered cord requiring neurosurgical release before scoliosis correction, and latex allergy.

## Syndromic Scolioses

Syndromic scolioses arise from underlying connective-tissue disorders, skeletal dysplasias, and other systemic conditions. They have specific features that require additional assessment and modified treatment.

Scoliosis in Marfan syndrome (FBN1 mutations) is common — 60–70% of patients develop scoliosis. Curves are typically progressive in adulthood, with high risk of progression and frequently accompanied by dural ectasia. Cardiothoracic problems (aortic dilation, dissection) are a priority and must be coordinated promptly with the cardiologist. Bracing is less effective because of joint hypermobility and poor bone quality. With surgery, a higher complication rate, greater blood loss, greater risk of dural tear, and greater need for revision should be anticipated.

Scoliosis in neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1, NF1 mutations) has two distinct types: non-dystrophic (60% of cases, behaving and treated similarly to idiopathic) and dystrophic (40%, with a typical short sharp curve in the upper or middle thoracic spine, often with kyphoscoliosis and rib-vertebral scalloped bodies). Dystrophic curves have a high risk of progression and a poor outcome; they require early surgical intervention with combined anterior and posterior fusion and careful assessment of intraspinal pathology (plexiform neuromas, dural ectasia). The decision for fusion in NF1 must include assessment of scalloped vertebral bodies, the width of the spinal canal, and the presence of intraspinal pathology.

Scoliosis in Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (type III/hypermobile and type IV/vascular) is highly prevalent and shows a strong tendency to progression. Bracing is less effective because of joint hypermobility, and surgery is associated with increased risk of vascular complications (especially in type IV), increased blood loss, and a higher risk of pseudarthrosis.

Scoliosis in osteogenesis imperfecta (COL1A1/COL1A2 mutations) is common, especially in the more severe types III and IV. Poor bone quality makes surgery particularly challenging, with a high risk of instrumentation pull-out and fracture. Bisphosphonate therapy (pamidronate, zoledronate) has improved bone quality and outcomes. Bracing is often of limited efficacy.

Scoliosis in skeletal dysplasias (achondroplasia, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, mucopolysaccharidoses) has specific aspects requiring planning by an experienced multidisciplinary team, including foramen magnum stenosis assessment in achondroplasia and systemic complications in the mucopolysaccharidoses.

## Adult Scoliosis

Adult scoliosis encompasses adult idiopathic scoliosis (the persistence of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis into adulthood, often with progression) and de novo (degenerative) adult scoliosis (the new development of curve in skeletal maturity due to asymmetric degenerative changes).

Adult idiopathic scoliosis typically progresses slowly through adult life — approximately 0.5–1° per year on average — and may produce progressive cosmetic, pulmonary, and pain symptoms. Sagittal imbalance (positive sagittal vertical axis with the C7 plumb line passing in front of the femoral heads) is a more important determinant of disability than coronal deformity in this population. The SRS-Schwab classification for adult scoliosis (2012) includes the curve type (T, L, D, N) and three modifiers — pelvic tilt (PT), pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis (PI-LL), and sagittal vertical axis (SVA) — which prognostically predict pain and quality-of-life impairment.

De novo (degenerative) adult scoliosis results from asymmetric degenerative disc disease, facet joint arthritis, and rotational subluxation, typically producing a thoracolumbar or lumbar curve with associated stenosis and radicular symptoms. The presentation is typically with back pain, radicular pain, and neurogenic claudication, rather than with cosmetic concerns. It is a typical disease of patients 60–80 years old, often with comorbid systemic conditions.

Surgical management of adult scoliosis is substantially more complex than adolescent surgery, with greater blood loss, longer fusion segments, higher complication rates, and worse outcomes. Indications include progressive deformity, neurological compromise, intractable pain, and sagittal imbalance. Modern surgical techniques include osteotomies for sagittal deformity correction (Smith-Petersen osteotomy, pedicle subtraction osteotomy — PSO, vertebral column resection — VCR), lateral or anterior lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF, ALIF) for additional correction and anterior column reconstruction, and posterior segmental fixation. The complication profile — proximal junctional kyphosis and failure, neurological injury, infection, pseudarthrosis, and medical complications from advanced age and comorbidities — demands careful patient selection, optimization of comorbidities, and detailed informed consent.

## Sagittal Balance and Spinal Alignment

The concept of sagittal balance has shifted attention from coronal deformity in modern adult spinal surgery. The sagittal alignment parameters include: pelvic incidence (PI) — an anatomic parameter of pelvic geometry, individual to each patient; pelvic tilt (PT) — a positional parameter reflecting compensatory pelvic retroversion; sacral slope (SS) — a positional parameter; lumbar lordosis (L1–S1); thoracic kyphosis (T5–T12); sagittal vertical axis (SVA) — the distance of the C7 plumb line from the posterior-superior corner of S1.

The Schwab formula: PI = PT + SS, and ideal LL ≈ PI ± 9° provides reference values for surgical planning. The correction goals in adult spinal deformity are: PI-LL <10°, PT <20°, SVA <5 cm. Achievement of these goals is associated with better clinical outcomes and lower risk of proximal junctional kyphosis.

The concept of global sagittal alignment, developed by the group of Le Huec and Roussouly in France, adds a classification of normal sagittal profiles (Roussouly types 1–4 based on the form of lumbar lordosis and sacral slope) and emphasizes that correction must restore the normal anatomic profile for the specific patient.

## The Schroth Method and Scoliosis-Specific Physiotherapies

In the past two decades, scoliosis-specific physical therapy methods (Physiotherapeutic Scoliosis-Specific Exercises, PSSE) have gained increasing acceptance as adjuncts or alternatives to bracing in mild to moderate adolescent idiopathic scoliosis.

The Schroth method, developed by Katharina Schroth in Germany from the 1920s and elaborated by her daughter Christa Lehnert-Schroth, combines specific corrective exercises directed at the three-dimensional deformity, postural retraining, and breathing techniques (so-called "rotational angular breathing"). The method uses three core concepts — curve type assessment, individualized correction, and active self-correction — and requires an extended training program for patients.

The SEAS method (Scientific Exercise Approach to Scoliosis), developed in Italy by the ISICO group (Italian Scientific Spine Institute) under the leadership of Stefano Negrini, is another widely used method with a strict scientific approach. It is based on the principle of active self-correction in all three planes and is integrated into daily activities.

The Side-Shift method, developed in London, is a simpler approach focused on lateral translation of the patient toward the convex side of the lumbar curve.

Evidence for efficacy is growing, with several randomized trials demonstrating improvements in cosmesis, pain, and quality of life, and some demonstrating stabilization of the Cobb angle. The effect on radiographic progression is less pronounced than that of bracing, but the method has additional benefit in patients wearing a brace. The method is most commonly used in conjunction with bracing or in the observation range (10–25° curves in skeletally immature patients). SOSORT (Scoliosis Orthopaedic and Rehabilitation Treatment) — the international society dealing with conservative management of scoliosis — publishes consensus recommendations on scoliosis-specific physiotherapies that standardize the approach.

## Local Contributions by Country

The history of scoliosis as a subdivision of orthopedics has been significantly enriched by contributions from surgeons and schools in various European countries. This section lists selected contributions by country.

Bulgaria: Bulgarian contributions to scoliology are best documented in the textbooks of Boyan Boychev — "Surgical Orthopedics" — who in the mid-twentieth century described the clinical assessment and conservative treatment of scoliosis in the context of the practice of his time. The Bulgarian school was influenced by the Soviet school and by the French school of Stagnara. Modern scoliosis surgery in Bulgaria is performed in specialized centers in Sofia (Military Medical Academy, University Hospital "Saint Anne", University Hospital "Sofiamed"), Plovdiv, and Varna, with the application of modern pedicle-screw constructs and participation in European scoliosis registries.

Former Czechoslovakia (Czech Republic and Slovakia): The influence of the Czech school of Bedřich Frejka and his pupils has left a mark on the region. Frejka is better known for his work in conservative treatment of hip dysplasia, but his pupils contributed to the treatment of scoliosis. Contemporary contributions from Prague and Brno are associated with the introduction of modern surgical techniques.

France: The French school has left a central mark on scoliology. Pierre Stagnara in Lyon formulated the concept of the "plane of maximum deformity" and of flexibility testing; he is the author of the classical "Spinal Deformity — Déformations Du Rachis" (1985). Stagnara also introduced the wake-up test for intraoperative monitoring. Yves Cotrel and Jean Dubousset in Paris developed the Cotrel-Dubousset instrumentation (1984), revolutionizing segmental instrumentation and introducing the concept of rod derotation. Jacques Chêneau developed the Chêneau brace, the basis of modern three-dimensional braces. The school of Jean-Charles Le Huec in Bordeaux contributes to the modern understanding of sagittal balance.

Germany: The German school has made fundamental contributions through the method of Katharina Schroth (1894–1985) — the founder of scoliosis-specific exercises; through the work of Heinrich Heine on biomechanics; and through modern centers in Karlsbad and Munich. Hans-Rudolf Weiss in Bad Sooden-Allendorf continues to develop the Schroth method.

Italy: The Italian contribution includes the development of the SEAS method by Stefano Negrini and the ISICO group, and their significant leadership in SOSORT and in modern consensus recommendations for conservative treatment.

Spain: Spanish contributions include the development of the Rigo brace by Manuel Rigo in Barcelona — a modern three-dimensional brace — and the work of the scoliosis group in Barcelona.

Switzerland: Centers in Zurich and Bern and the canton of Valais have contributed to modern spinal surgery with contributions to pedicle screws and to consolidated practice.

United Kingdom: The influence of Min Mehta in London through the description of RVAD and the technique of serial EDF casts is the basis of modern treatment of infantile scoliosis. The Great Ormond Street school is a significant center.

Scandinavia: Swedish and Norwegian contributions include early classifications and centers of specialized surgery.

Former USSR and Russia: The Chaklin and Abalmasova school in Moscow (the book "Scolioses and Kyphoses," 1973) has contributed to the classification and to the conservative treatment in the post-Soviet area; although the original Russian texts are not widely accessible in the West, their influence is significant in Eastern Europe.

Poland: Polish contributions are associated with centers in Krakow, Warsaw, and Łódź.

Turkey: The school of Muharrem Yazici in Ankara has made significant contributions to modern treatment, especially in the area of infantile scoliosis and growing rods, and is among the authors of the standard text The Growing Spine (Akbarnia, Yazici, Thompson).

North America: Contributions from North America include: John Cobb (New York, 1948) — the Cobb angle; Walter Blount and Albert Schmidt (Wisconsin) — the Milwaukee brace; Joseph Risser (Los Angeles) — the Risser sign; Paul Harrington (Houston) — the Harrington instrumentation; Eduardo Luque (Mexico City) — Luque segmental instrumentation; Allen and Ferguson (Galveston) — the Galveston technique; John Lonstein and colleagues in Minneapolis — natural history and serial casting technique; Lawrence Lenke (St. Louis) — the Lenke classification; Stuart Weinstein (Iowa) — natural history in adulthood and the BrAIST trial; Robert Campbell (San Antonio) — VEPTR; Behrooz Akbarnia (San Diego) — growing rods. This is an incomplete list but illustrates the concentration of major technological and scientific contributions in North American centers in the second half of the twentieth century and the beginning of the twenty-first.

Korea: Suk and colleagues in Seoul have made significant contributions to pedicle-screw constructs and to direct vertebral derotation.

Hong Kong: Cheung and Luk in Hong Kong developed the magnetic growing rods (MAGEC).

## Summary and Take-Home Points

Scoliosis is a heterogeneous disease whose adolescent idiopathic form constitutes the majority of practice in most centers. Clinical recognition by Adams's forward bend test, the Cobb angle measurement, the Risser and Sanders staging of skeletal maturity, and the Lenke classification of curve patterns provide the foundation of assessment. Treatment follows a sliding scale: observation for small curves; bracing (with adherence as the key determinant of efficacy, demonstrated by the BrAIST trial) for moderate curves in skeletally immature patients; and surgical posterior fusion with segmental pedicle-screw instrumentation for curves above 45–50°.

The natural history of untreated AIS is variable but predictable from curve magnitude and skeletal maturity; long-term progression in adult life is most concerning for curves above 50° at maturity. Infantile and juvenile idiopathic scoliosis require specific approaches including Mehta casting and growth-friendly techniques (VEPTR, traditional growing rods, MAGEC).

Neuromuscular scoliosis arises from underlying neurological or muscular disease, produces characteristic long C-shaped curves with pelvic obliquity, progresses into adulthood, and requires long instrumented fusion often to the pelvis with substantially higher complication rates. Syndromic scolioses in Marfan, NF1, Ehlers-Danlos, and osteogenesis imperfecta require modified treatment and specific re-assessment.

Adult scoliosis — whether persisting from adolescence or arising de novo from asymmetric degeneration — requires individualized assessment of pain, function, sagittal balance, and surgical risk. The SRS-Schwab classification and the principles of sagittal alignment (PI-LL <10°, PT <20°, SVA <5 cm) are the basis of modern planning.

Across all forms of scoliosis, the principles of three-dimensional assessment, careful selection of treatment based on age, progression risk, and underlying etiology, and attention to the goals of correction, balance, and stability determine successful outcomes. The development of scoliology has been enriched by contributions from many schools in Europe and North America, and the contemporary clinician is the heir to that rich tradition.
