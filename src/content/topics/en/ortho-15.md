## Overview

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a group of non-progressive disorders of movement and posture arising from injury to the immature developing brain. By international consensus (Rosenbaum et al., *Dev Med Child Neurol* 2007;49 Suppl 109:8–14), the diagnosis requires three features: a central origin for the disorder; a non-progressive (although clinically evolving) brain injury; and onset within the developmental period — most narrowly before age 2 years. The incidence in developed countries is approximately 2–3 per 1,000 live births; in very-low-birth-weight cohorts the rate exceeds 70 per 1,000.

The chapter treats the orthopedic manifestations of CP as the result of three interacting processes — spasticity, muscle imbalance, and continuing bone growth — and addresses paralytic hip dislocation and the paralytic foot specifically as the two principal sites for orthopedic intervention. The algorithm rests on the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) of Palisano and Rosenbaum (1997), on the systematic hip surveillance programmes (the Australian Hip Surveillance Program and the Swedish CPUP), on computerized gait analysis with the Rodda and Winters–Gage–Hicks classifications, and on the principle of single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS) developed by Browne, Graham and colleagues.

The topic synthesizes content from Freeman Miller's *Cerebral Palsy* (Springer, 2nd ed. 2020), Eugene Bleck's *Orthopaedic Management in Cerebral Palsy* (1987; 2nd ed. 2008), James R. Gage and colleagues' *The Identification and Treatment of Gait Problems in Cerebral Palsy* (Mac Keith Press 2009), Tachdjian's *Procedures in Pediatric Orthopaedics* (Herring, Elsevier 2016), *Lovell and Winter's Pediatric Orthopaedics* (8th ed. 2020), and Sharrard's *Paediatric Orthopaedics and Fractures* (3rd ed. 1993), with classical Bulgarian and Russian contributions.

## Etiology and Classification
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="etiology-classification">
<details>
<summary>Etiology and Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Etiology by gestational age
<details id="etiology">
<summary>Etiology by gestational age</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The causes of cerebral palsy vary with the gestational age at the time of injury. Miller (*Cerebral Palsy*, ch. 2) and Bleck (*Orthopaedic Management in CP*, ch. 1) emphasize that etiologic heterogeneity directly governs the clinical picture and prognosis.

**Prematurity and periventricular leukomalacia.** The strongest single risk factor. Injury to the periventricular white matter affects principally the descending corticospinal fibers to the lower limbs, accounting for the classical spastic diplegia of the prematurely born child. Risk rises exponentially with falling gestational age: below 32 weeks, more than tenfold over term; below 28 weeks, more than thirtyfold. Improved neonatal care in developed countries has paradoxically increased the absolute number of children with CP from very-low-birth-weight backgrounds — more fragile infants survive.

**Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy at term.** Acute intrapartum hypoxia accounts for approximately 10–20% of cases — substantially less than was historically attributed (Nelson and Ellenberg, *N Engl J Med* 1986;315:81–86). Establishing this lower fraction carries major medicolegal significance: most cases of CP are *not* the result of an obstetric error.

**Other causes.** Intracerebral hemorrhage (especially periventricular hemorrhage of prematurity); congenital brain malformations; intrauterine infections (TORCH: toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex); kernicterus (now rare in developed countries owing to Rh prophylaxis and phototherapy); postnatal infection (meningitis, encephalitis); trauma; thrombophilias and inherited vascular conditions.

**Idiopathic CP.** In a substantial proportion of cases (approximately 10–20% in modern series), thorough investigation fails to identify a clear cause. Contemporary genetic studies are identifying monogenic causes in a growing share of these "idiopathic" cases, particularly when classical perinatal risk factors are absent.

</div>
</details>


### Classification by motor pattern
<details id="motor-pattern">
<summary>Classification by motor pattern</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Hagberg and Bax classification (Surveillance of Cerebral Palsy in Europe, SCPE) distinguishes three principal motor patterns:

**Spastic CP (approximately 75% of cases).** The dominant clinical picture: increased muscle tone (velocity-dependent resistance), hyperreflexia, clonus, positive Babinski sign, and progressive contractures from continuous unopposed muscle pull on growing bones. Subdivided by distribution: *spastic diplegia* (predominantly lower-limb involvement, the classical pattern of premature birth); *spastic hemiplegia* (one side of the body, often associated with a focal perinatal vascular lesion); *spastic quadriplegia* (all four limbs with severe associated cognitive, behavioral and sensory impairment); and the less commonly used *monoplegia* and *triplegia*.

**Dyskinetic CP (approximately 15%).** Involuntary movements: *chorea* (rapid, irregular, jerky movements), *athetosis* (slow, writhing movements often passing into positional dystonia), or *dystonia* (sustained muscle contractions producing twisting postures). The pathology affects the basal ganglia — typically following kernicterus or severe hypoxic-ischemic injury at term. Orthopedic contractures are less characteristic, but fixed dystonic postures may require surgical correction.

**Ataxic CP (approximately 5%).** Impaired balance, coordination, and gait with normal or slightly decreased muscle tone; the pathology is cerebellar. Often associated with genetic or metabolic causes.

**Mixed CP (approximately 5%).** Combines features of two or more patterns — most commonly spastic with a dyskinetic component.

</div>
</details>


### GMFCS, MACS, CFCS, EDACS
<details id="gmfcs">
<summary>GMFCS, MACS, CFCS, EDACS</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**GMFCS (Gross Motor Function Classification System).** Introduced by Palisano, Rosenbaum, Walter and colleagues in Canada in 1997 (*Dev Med Child Neurol* 39:214–223) and expanded in 2007 (GMFCS – E&R, *Expanded and Revised*), the system has transformed orthopedic care for CP by providing a reproducible, prognostically useful five-level classification of motor function.

- *Level I* — walks without limitations; can run, jump, navigate stairs, and ambulate in the community without assistive devices.
- *Level II* — walks with limitations; can walk in most settings but with difficulty on stairs, uneven surfaces, and long distances.
- *Level III* — walks using a hand-held mobility device (cane, crutch, walker); uses wheeled mobility for long distances.
- *Level IV* — self-mobility with limitations; uses powered or pushed wheelchair for community mobility; may stand with support.
- *Level V* — transported in a manual wheelchair; no self-mobility; severe limitations in head and trunk control.

The level is remarkably stable with growth: a child at Level II at age 5 typically remains at Level II throughout life. This makes GMFCS the central instrument for planning hip surveillance, scoliosis monitoring, and surgical intervention.

**MACS (Manual Ability Classification System).** Eliasson and colleagues (*Dev Med Child Neurol* 2006;48:549–554) — a five-level classification of hand skills in manipulating objects, paralleling GMFCS. Levels I–V describe independence from full to fully dependent.

**CFCS (Communication Function Classification System).** Hidecker and colleagues (2011) — a five-level classification of communicative function, important in planning surgical intervention in quadriplegia.

**EDACS (Eating and Drinking Ability Classification System).** Sellers and colleagues (2014) — a five-level classification of the safety and efficiency of eating and drinking, prognostically linked to respiratory complications and cachexia.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The GMFCS level is considered stable from approximately age 5; movement up or down is unusual and warrants reassessment.</li>
    <li>The risk of paralytic hip dislocation correlates directly with GMFCS level: Level I — about 1–2%; Level V — 70–90%.</li>
    <li>Scoliosis develops in approximately 70% of patients at Level V and in significantly lower proportions at higher functional levels.</li>
    <li>GMFCS, MACS, CFCS, and EDACS are documented at each visit and form the standardized clinical signature of the patient.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Pathophysiology of orthopedic deformity
<details id="pathophysiology">
<summary>Pathophysiology of orthopedic deformity</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Bleck (*Orthopaedic Management in CP*, ch. 4) treats the orthopedic manifestations as the result of three simultaneously acting processes:

**Spasticity.** The velocity-dependent increase in muscle tone that produces sustained or rhythmic over-contraction of affected muscles, leading to phasic or continuous overactivation of the muscle spindles. Lance (1980) defined spasticity as one component of the upper motor neuron syndrome that also includes weakness, delayed response, loss of dexterity, and pathological co-activation of antagonists. The Modified Ashworth Scale of Bohannon–Smith (0–4) and the Tardieu scale (dynamic assessment with different velocities of stretch) are the standard clinical instruments.

**Muscle imbalance.** Spastic muscles overpower their normally toned antagonists, producing characteristic patterns of static posture and dynamic deformity. Classical examples: the spastic flexor-adductors of the hip overpower the extensors and abductors; the spastic gastrocnemius-soleus overpowers the dorsiflexors; the forearm pronators overpower the supinators. Detailed identification of these "winning" groups is the foundation of surgical planning.

**Bone growth.** The immature, growing bone progressively adapts to the persistent abnormal muscle forces, producing both *contractures* (soft-tissue shortening) and *bony deformity* (angular or rotational malalignment). The long tubular bones exhibit progressive anteversion (femur), internal or external torsion (tibia), valgus or varus deformity of the proximal and distal femur, and calcaneal pitch or planovalgus deformity.

**The classical example — the spastic hip.** The flexors and adductors are more spastic than the extensors and abductors; the hip assumes a sustained flexed, adducted position; over years the femoral head migrates laterally and superiorly; the acetabulum fails to develop normal coverage; the femoral neck remains in excessive anteversion and valgus; and the hip progresses through subluxation to dislocation. Analogous patterns of muscle imbalance produce equinus foot, crouch gait, scissor gait, and the other characteristic abnormalities.

### Three-dimensional gait analysis
<details id="gait-lab">
<summary>Three-dimensional gait analysis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Gage (*The Identification and Treatment of Gait Problems in CP*, Mac Keith Press 2009, chs. 4–8) systematizes contemporary computerized gait analysis (3D-CGA) as a mandatory preoperative instrument in the ambulatory CP child. The analysis combines four simultaneously recorded components:

**Kinematics.** Joint angles in all three planes (sagittal, frontal, transverse) through the gait cycle, recorded with markers tracked by infrared cameras. The output consists of characteristic curves for the hip, knee, ankle, and pelvis compared with normative data from typically developing children.

**Kinetics.** Ground-reaction forces (from force plates) and the calculated external and internal joint moments and powers. Kinetics identifies the source of dysfunction — spasticity, contracture, muscle weakness, or inappropriate antagonist activity.

**Dynamic electromyography.** The timing of muscle activation in the gait cycle (surface or fine-wire EMG). A hyperactive rectus femoris in swing — an indication for transfer; persistent tibialis posterior activity throughout stance — an indication for lengthening or transfer.

**Energy expenditure.** Oxygen cost per unit distance walked (the "energy index"), an objective measure of gait efficiency. Crouch gait typically doubles or triples the energy cost.

3D-CGA is used to plan single-event multilevel surgery, to assess the outcome of such operations, and for research. Gage emphasizes that without computerized analysis, orthopedic planning in the complex ambulatory CP child is guesswork based on imprecise clinical impressions.

</div>
</details>


### Rodda classification (diplegia)
<details id="rodda">
<summary>Rodda classification (diplegia)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Rodda and Graham (*Eur J Neurol* 2001;8 Suppl 5:98–108) distinguish five sagittal-plane patterns of gait in spastic diplegia:

- *Type I — Apparent equinus.* The ankle is in equinus from a tight gastrocnemius-soleus complex, with relatively preserved knee and hip kinematics.
- *Type II — True equinus.* Equinus combined with hyperextended knees and increased hip flexion.
- *Type III — Jump knee gait.* Equinus combined with flexed knees and flexed hips during stance, with the knee extending at terminal stance.
- *Type IV — Apparent crouch.* Equinus combined with flexed knees and flexed hips throughout stance.
- *Type V — Crouch.* Calcaneus ankle (typically iatrogenic from overly aggressive Achilles tendon lengthening), flexed knees, and flexed hips throughout stance.

Crouch gait is the most problematic pattern: it produces excessive energy expenditure, accelerates knee joint degeneration, and is poorly responsive to treatment once established.

</div>
</details>


### Winters–Gage–Hicks (hemiplegia)
<details id="wgh">
<summary>Winters–Gage–Hicks (hemiplegia)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Winters, Gage, and Hicks (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 1987;69:437–441) distinguish four types of hemiplegic gait by ascending complexity:

- *Type I.* Drop foot during swing from anterior tibialis weakness, with normal stance phase. Treatment: rigid or dynamic AFO.
- *Type II.* Persistent equinus in stance and swing from gastrocnemius-soleus spasticity. Type IIA — without knee recurvatum; Type IIB — with recurvatum.
- *Type III.* Type II features combined with limited knee flexion in swing from rectus femoris hyperactivity.
- *Type IV.* Type III features combined with hip flexion and adduction from spasticity of the flexor-adductors.

The classification provides a direct algorithmic link: a higher type implies more involved muscles and a broader surgical programme (typically within SEMLS).

</div>
</details>


### Amsterdam Gait Classification
<details id="amsterdam">
<summary>Amsterdam Gait Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Amsterdam Gait Classification (Becher and colleagues, 2005) distinguishes five types of hemiplegic gait by knee position at midstance and ground contact:

- *Type 1.* Foot-flat with neutral knee.
- *Type 2.* Toe contact with flexed knee.
- *Type 3.* Toe contact with hyperextended knee.
- *Type 4.* Total equinus with knee hyperextension through stance.
- *Type 5.* Crouch posture with extensor deficit at hip and knee.

The classification is widely used in northern Europe and coexists with the older Winters–Gage–Hicks model, which remains dominant in North American practice.

</div>
</details>

## Paralytic Hip Dislocation
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="paralytic-hip">
<details>
<summary>Paralytic Hip Dislocation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Pathogenesis and epidemiology
<details id="hip-pathogenesis">
<summary>Pathogenesis and epidemiology</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Paralytic hip dislocation is among the most consequential orthopedic complications of CP. It is *spastic* in origin — not congenital but acquired from the progressive action of spastic flexor-adductors on the immature growing proximal femur and acetabulum, with absence of normal axial loading in non-ambulatory patients.

**Epidemiology.** The risk correlates closely with GMFCS level, as shown by Soo and colleagues (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2006;88:121–129) in a cohort of 323 patients from the Australian surveillance program:

- GMFCS I: 0%
- GMFCS II: 15%
- GMFCS III: 41%
- GMFCS IV: 69%
- GMFCS V: 90%

**Pathogenetic cascade.** (1) Spastic flexors (iliopsoas) and adductors (adductor longus, brevis, gracilis) overpower the extensors and abductors; (2) the hip assumes a sustained flexed, adducted position; (3) absent normal loading of the femoral cone against the acetabulum, normal acetabular modeling does not occur — the acetabulum remains anteverted and flat at its posterolateral edge; (4) the proximal femur retains excessive anteversion and valgus, because muscle forces do not produce normal modeling; (5) the femoral head migrates laterally and superiorly under the pull of the spastic muscles; (6) subluxation progresses to complete dislocation, typically posterolateral.

**Silent evolution.** Progression is often silent — without pain or visible stiffness — and is detected only by routine radiographic surveillance. This is the central argument for systematic surveillance: symptoms only appear once the hip is already dislocated, when surgical options are sharply narrowed.

</div>
</details>


### Hip surveillance
<details id="hip-surveillance">
<summary>Hip surveillance</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Hip surveillance programs — systematic radiographic assessment of all children with CP on a schedule dictated by GMFCS — have transformed care for paralytic dislocation and have become standard in developed countries.

**Swedish CPUP (Cerebral Palsy Follow-Up Programme).** Hägglund and colleagues (Lund, begun 1994; *J Bone Joint Surg Br* 2005;87:95–101) demonstrate in a 20-year prospective cohort that systematic surveillance and timely intervention have eliminated new dislocations in the Scandinavian population. CPUP is a model for European programs and covers the hip, the spine, and the upper limb.

**Australian Hip Surveillance Program (AHSP).** Wynter, Gibson, Kentish, Love and colleagues (*Aust J Physiother* 2008) — a national protocol based on GMFCS with categorical intervals:

- GMFCS I: radiograph only if hip examination is abnormal (asymmetry of abduction, shortening).
- GMFCS II: initial radiograph at 12–24 months; repeat at 5 years (or on indication).
- GMFCS III: at 12–24 months, then annually to age 7; every 2 years to skeletal maturity.
- GMFCS IV: at 12–24 months, then every 6–12 months to age 7; annually to skeletal maturity.
- GMFCS V: at 12–24 months, then every 6 months to age 7; annually to skeletal maturity.

Examination by a pediatric orthopedist is at the same intervals, with clinical recording of passive abduction (norm > 45°), flexion contracture, shortening, scissoring, and visible asymmetry.

**Imaging standard.** AP pelvis radiograph with the hips in neutral position, parallel to the film plane; calibrated for measurement of distances and angles; standardized positioning, reproducible between visits.

</div>
</details>


### Reimers migration percentage
<details id="reimers">
<summary>Reimers migration percentage</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Migration Percentage (MP) of Henning Reimers (Denmark, *Acta Orthop Scand Suppl* 1980;184:1–100) is the standard radiographic measure for assessing femoral head coverage in CP and tracking progression of subluxation.

**Definition.** MP = (A/B) × 100%, where *A* is the distance from the Hilgenreiner-perpendicular line at the lateral edge of the acetabulum to the Hilgenreiner-perpendicular line at the lateral edge of the femoral head, and *B* is the transverse width of the femoral head. Numerically, this is the percentage of the femoral head not covered by the acetabular roof, measured on the AP pelvis radiograph.

**Thresholds and algorithm.**

- *< 33%* — acceptable. Continue surveillance on schedule.
- *33–40%* — pre-subluxation gray zone. Shorten the surveillance interval; consider adductor botulinum toxin for a dynamic component; consider soft-tissue surgery.
- *40–50%* — established subluxation. Indication for soft-tissue or early bony intervention.
- *> 50%* — significant subluxation. Bony reconstruction is standard.
- *> 70–80%* — near dislocation; usually with associated acetabular dysplasia.
- *100%* — complete dislocation.

**Rate of progression.** Progression of MP in CP is approximately linear: on average 7–10% per year in untreated GMFCS V, 3–5% per year in GMFCS III–IV. Rate of progression is an independent prognostic factor — rapidly progressive subluxation (>10% per year) calls for more aggressive intervention at a lower absolute MP.

**Other measurements.** Acetabular index (Hilgenreiner) — reflecting acetabular slope; Sharp angle — complementary in the older child; head-shaft angle (CCD) — increased in a valgus neck; femoral neck-shaft angle and anteversion of the proximal femur — assessed by CT for surgical planning of varus-derotation osteotomy.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>MP < 33% — acceptable; 33–40% — surveillance; 40–50% — soft-tissue surgery; > 50% — bony reconstruction.</li>
    <li>The rate of progression is an independent prognostic factor and sometimes mandates intervention at a lower absolute MP.</li>
    <li>Standardized positioning of the pelvis is critical for reproducibility — pelvic tilt can change MP by more than 5%.</li>
    <li>MP < 30% with asymmetry (> 10% difference between hips) warrants reassessment — asymmetry frequently precedes absolute progression.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Soft-tissue surgery
<details id="soft-tissue">
<summary>Soft-tissue surgery</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Soft-tissue surgery — targeted tenotomy and/or lengthening of the spastic flexor-adductors — is the first surgical line in early subluxation (MP 33–50%) in the younger child (typically under age 6–8 years). The goal is to release the spastic pull and allow the hip to return to a more centered position, on the assumption that bony anatomy — the acetabular roof and the proximal femur — is still capable of remodeling.

**Adductor tenotomy.** The classical procedure is percutaneous tenotomy of adductor longus and gracilis, with or without partial release of adductor brevis, through a simple medial incision below the inguinal crease. Care for the *anterior obturator nerve* — lying deep to adductor brevis and supplying adductor magnus. Selective obturator neurectomy (Stoffel) is rarely performed in modern practice — complete denervation of adductor magnus produces undesirable hip extensor weakness.

**Iliopsoas lengthening.** Iliopsoas lengthening over the pelvic brim (Bleck) — Z-plasty or intramuscular tenotomy of the tendon at the brim, sparing the motor function of iliacus (important for active hip flexion in the ambulatory child). The classical lengthening at the lesser trochanter (Banks–Green) is reserved for non-ambulatory patients in whom complete loss of active flexion is acceptable.

**Results.** When performed in time before age 5–6 with MP under 40%, soft-tissue surgery gives 60–80% long-term hip stabilization (Presedo and colleagues, *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2005;87:832–841). In the older child or with MP > 50%, isolated soft-tissue surgery is inadequate and is combined with bony reconstruction in a single stage.

</div>
</details>


### Bony reconstruction (VDRO + pelvic osteotomy)
<details id="bony">
<summary>Bony reconstruction (VDRO + pelvic osteotomy)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For established subluxation (MP > 40–50%) or failed soft-tissue surgery, bony reconstruction is performed, typically combining a varus-derotation femoral osteotomy with a pelvic osteotomy in a single stage.

**Varus-derotation osteotomy (VDRO).** An intertrochanteric femoral osteotomy with varization (typically 30–40° reduction of the neck-shaft angle from valgus 150–160° toward the normal 120–125°) and derotation (typically 20–40° reduction of excessive anteversion, measured by CT or by intraoperative clinical assessment). Fixation with a pediatric blade plate, modern locking plates (proximal humeral locking plate, adapted), or AO/DHS-type devices. The goal is to reorient the proximal femur so that the femoral head is centered within the encasing acetabular cavity and the loading forces are normalized.

**Pelvic osteotomies in CP.** Unlike congenital DDH, in CP the coverage deficit is typically *posterolateral* (not anterolateral), which dictates a specific choice of pelvic osteotomy:

- *Dega osteotomy* (Wieslaw Dega, Poznań, 1969). Incomplete supra-acetabular osteotomy entering the triradiate cartilage; coverage is redirected by plastic deformation of the acetabular roof. Provides simultaneous posterolateral and superior correction and is particularly suited to the paralytic hip.
- *San Diego modification* (Mubarak, Wenger) — incomplete circular osteotomy with multidirectional redirection of the coverage; universal correction for varied acetabular deficits.
- *Pemberton osteotomy* — original periacetabular osteotomy hinging around the posterior column; useful in the younger child with triradiate cartilage.
- *Salter osteotomy* — single iliac cut with redirection of the entire acetabular cavity; more useful for anterolateral deficit (DDH) than for the purely posterolateral deficit of CP.
- *Chiari osteotomy and shelf procedures* — salvage in the older child with a disproportionately large femoral head or established degenerative change unsuited to anatomical reconstruction.

**Results.** The combination of VDRO + Dega (or San Diego) with concurrent adductor and iliopsoas tenotomy gives durable reduction in 80–90% of cases in well-selected patients (Robb and colleagues, Mubarak and colleagues, *J Pediatr Orthop*). Long-term construct survivorship depends on the severity of the underlying disease — in GMFCS V with severe dyskinesia, recurrence is higher.

</div>
</details>


### Salvage procedures (Castle, Schanz, McHale)
<details id="salvage-hip">
<summary>Salvage procedures (Castle, Schanz, McHale)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In the older non-ambulatory patient (GMFCS IV–V) with established painful dislocation, arthritic change, and an unfeasible anatomical reconstruction, salvage procedures provide pain relief at the cost of function.

**Proximal femoral resection (Castle).** Mark E. Castle (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 1978;60:1051–1054) — resection of the proximal femur below the lesser trochanter with interposition of quadriceps or gluteus maximus to prevent heterotopic ossification and proximal migration. Provides good pain relief and easier perineal hygiene. Complication: heterotopic ossification (minimized by aggressive perioperative indomethacin or radiation prophylaxis).

**Valgus osteotomy (Schanz).** A high intertrochanteric valgus osteotomy that converts the femur into a hook-like structure supporting the pelvis, with or without resection of the femoral head. Klisic and colleagues described an expanded version. Pain relief is good, but ossification of the resected head sometimes produces secondary pain.

**Valgus osteotomy with femoral head resection (McHale).** McHale and colleagues (*J Pediatr Orthop* 1990;10:504–509) — combination of head resection with valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy that directs the upper end of the femur into the superoposterior acetabular region. A reliable salvage.

**Total hip arthroplasty (THA).** In selected ambulatory patients with spastic quadriplegia or diplegia and a painful dislocation, arthroplasty is feasible but technically demanding: severe acetabular dysplasia, excessive anteversion, asymmetric loading from spastic muscles, and high early dislocation risk. Outcomes in well-selected patients are acceptable, but complications are substantially more frequent than in primary arthroplasty in non-CP patients.

</div>
</details>

</details>

### Equinus
<details id="equinus">
<summary>Equinus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The equinus foot — plantarflexed ankle from spasticity or contracture of the gastrocnemius-soleus — is the commonest foot deformity in CP.

**Silfverskiöld test.** The Swedish orthopedist Nils Silfverskiöld (*Acta Chir Scand* 1923;56:315–330) described the clinical test that distinguishes isolated gastrocnemius contracture from combined triceps surae contracture: passive dorsiflexion of the ankle with the knee extended (gastrocnemius stretched) versus with the knee flexed to 90° (gastrocnemius relaxed). If dorsiflexion is limited only with knee extension, the contracture is isolated to gastrocnemius — an indication for recession. If dorsiflexion remains limited with the knee flexed, both gastrocnemius and soleus are contracted — a possible indication for formal Achilles tendon lengthening.

**Conservative management.** Botulinum toxin into the gastrocnemius (double injections of the two heads under ultrasound or electrostimulation guidance) gives 3–6 months of spasticity relief and facilitates stretching and AFO wear. Serial casting (Tardieu and colleagues) contributes to lengthening the dynamically shortened muscle. These measures apply to dynamic equinus (no fixed contracture).

**Surgical gastrocnemius lengthening.** The Strayer recession (Lloyd S. Strayer, *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 1950;32:671–676) — aponeurotomy of the gastrocnemius aponeurosis at the musculotendinous junction, allowing retraction of the muscle fibers and lengthening of functional length by 1–3 cm. Suitable when the Silfverskiöld test is positive. Vulpius (1913) and Baker — alternative modifications with inverted-V incision through the aponeurosis.

**Formal Achilles tendon lengthening.** Z-plasty or the Hoke percutaneous technique (three semi-sections through the tendon with rotation — Hoke, *J Bone Joint Surg* 1931;13:773–783). Reserved for cases with true triceps contracture (negative Silfverskiöld test) and for the older child with an established bony equinus deformity. Avoidance of over-lengthening is the single most important principle in CP foot surgery.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Over-lengthening of the Achilles tendon — the single most disabling iatrogenic complication in CP orthopedics. Produces calcaneus ankle and crouch gait with permanent loss of function.</li>
    <li>Strayer gastrocnemius recession is more conservative and the preferred first-line option for isolated gastrocnemius contracture; formal Achilles lengthening is reserved for combined triceps contracture.</li>
    <li>In the ambulatory child a small residual dynamic equinus (5–10°) is deliberately preserved as a safeguard against over-lengthening.</li>
    <li>Achilles lengthening in GMFCS IV–V is more forgiving — patients do not bear axial load and calcaneus deformity is a smaller concern.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Equinovarus
<details id="equinovarus">
<summary>Equinovarus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The equinovarus foot — plantarflexed and inverted — is the typical deformity in spastic hemiplegia. It originates from combined spasticity of the gastrocnemius, tibialis posterior and/or tibialis anterior with relative weakness of the dorsiflexors and peroneal muscles.

**Identifying the "guilty" muscle.** Dynamic EMG and the clinical Confirmation by Block test of tibialis posterior or tibialis anterior with lidocaine distinguish the two principal patterns. The classical Mooney test — lidocaine into tibialis anterior; if the varus resolves, tibialis anterior is "guilty" and amenable to a split or whole-tendon transfer.

**Tibialis posterior lengthening.** Z-plasty or intramuscular recession in the distal third of the leg. Preferred in tibialis-posterior-dominated equinovarus (continuous tibialis posterior activity during stance and swing).

**Split anterior tibialis tendon transfer (SPLATT).** Described by Mooney and Hoffer (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 1973): the lateral half of the tibialis anterior tendon is detached and transferred to a lateral cuboid or third-cuneiform insertion — redistributing the force from inversion toward eversion. Preferred in anterior-tib-dominated equinovarus with overactivity in swing.

**Complete tibialis posterior transfer through the interosseous membrane to the dorsum of the foot.** The classical procedure for drop foot from dorsiflexor weakness in polio — rarely indicated in CP.

**Bony correction.** With an established bony deformity, a dorsolateral cuboid closing-wedge osteotomy or a Dwyer medial calcaneal closing-wedge is added, typically as part of SEMLS.

</div>
</details>


### Equinoplanovalgus
<details id="planovalgus">
<summary>Equinoplanovalgus</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The equinoplanovalgus foot — plantarflexed at the ankle but pronated at the subtalar joint with hindfoot valgus and forefoot abduction — is common in spastic diplegia and quadriplegia. The deformity combines opposing components and requires multi-component correction.

**Pathoanatomy.** (1) Equinus from gastrocnemius-soleus contracture; (2) hindfoot valgus from overactive peroneus brevis and relative weakness of tibialis posterior; (3) abduction and plantarflexion of the talus with lateral displacement of the cuboid; (4) lateral displacement and dorsiflexion of the cuneiform and forefoot abduction.

**Surgical correction.** Multi-component:

- *Gastrocnemius recession* (Strayer) — for the equinus.
- *Calcaneal lengthening (Evans osteotomy)* (William Evans, *J Bone Joint Surg Br* 1975;57:270–278) — a wedge bone graft into the anterior process of the calcaneus, lengthening the lateral column and correcting forefoot abduction and the valgus of the Chopart joint. The standard surgical correction in the flexible planovalgus foot.
- *Mosca modification* — a modification of the Evans lengthening with a more posteriorly placed osteotomy.
- *Grice–Green subtalar arthrodesis* — for patients with severe progressive deformity and failure of lengthening; a joint-sparing arthrodesis using cancellous bone graft into the sinus tarsi.
- *Trans-lateral bone block (Karchinov)* — the Bulgarian modification for stabilization in the older child.
- *Triple arthrodesis* — salvage for rigid deformity in the adult.

</div>
</details>


### Calcaneus (crouch) foot
<details id="calcaneus">
<summary>Calcaneus (crouch) foot</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The calcaneus foot — calcaneus ankle with dorsiflexed calcaneus, typically from over-lengthened Achilles — is among the most disabling iatrogenic complications in CP orthopedics.

**Pathogenesis.** Over-lengthening of the triceps removes the principal extensor force for the knee in stance. Without active ankle plantarflexion in terminal stance, the body cannot advance over the planted foot; the knee cannot extend; and the patient compensates with sustained hip and knee flexion — crouch gait. Energy expenditure doubles or triples, and the knee develops progressive patellofemoral pain from sustained quadriceps overload.

**Treatment.** Predominantly preventive: careful dosing of Achilles lengthening (or preference for the Strayer recession), with deliberate preservation of 5–10° of residual dynamic equinus. With established crouch the treatment combines distal femoral extension osteotomy with patellar tendon advancement (Stout, Koop, Gage and colleagues; *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2008;90:2470–2484) and an appropriately designed ground-reaction AFO. Achilles lengthening cannot in principle be surgically reversed.

</div>
</details>

</div>
</details>

## Knee: crouch and stiff-knee gait
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="knee">
<details>
<summary>Knee: crouch and stiff-knee gait</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Crouch gait.** A gait pattern with sustained flexion at the hips and knees and neutral or calcaneus ankles. One of the most difficult problems in ambulatory CP. The mechanism combines: knee flexion contracture; hamstring spasticity or contracture (medial: semitendinosus, semimembranosus, gracilis; lateral: biceps femoris); iliopsoas spasticity or contracture; and weak quadriceps and hip extensors. The standard treatment is *distal femoral extension osteotomy* (DFEO) with *patellar tendon advancement* (PTA), combined with medial hamstring lengthening, psoas lengthening over the brim, and often patellar reconstruction in patella alta. Outcomes in well-selected patients (Stout, Gage and colleagues from Gillette — *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2008;90:2470–2484) show durable improvement in flexion contracture and function.

**Stiff-knee gait.** A gait pattern with limited knee flexion in swing (typically < 45° vs. norm 65°) from overactivity of the rectus femoris in early swing. The standard treatment is *rectus femoris transfer* (Gage, Perry) — the distal rectus femoris tendon is detached from the common quadriceps tendon and transferred to a knee flexor (sartorius, gracilis, semitendinosus, or iliotibial band), converting the force from an antagonist into a synergist of knee flexion in swing. Patient selection is by dynamic EMG — only patients with confirmed rectus femoris overactivity in swing are candidates.

**Hamstring lengthening.** The hamstrings are lengthened medially (Z-plasty of semitendinosus, aponeurotomy of semimembranosus, tenotomy of gracilis) or bilaterally (with biceps femoris) according to preoperative assessment. Over-lengthening leads to increased anterior pelvic tilt and a crouched posture — a new risk factor for crouch gait.

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Upper limb
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="upper-limb">
<details>
<summary>Upper limb</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Upper-limb involvement is most pronounced in hemiplegic patients but is present in many spastic diplegic and quadriplegic patients. The classical pattern: shoulder internally rotated and adducted; elbow flexed and forearm pronated; wrist flexed and ulnar-deviated; fingers flexed; "thumb-in-palm" deformity.

**Zancolli classification.** Eduardo Zancolli (Argentina; *Hand Clin* 2003;19:609–629) grades wrist and finger function by capacity for active extension:

- *Grade I.* Full finger extension at a neutral or slightly extended wrist.
- *Grade IIA.* Full finger extension only with the wrist in flexion (≥ 20°).
- *Grade IIB.* Incomplete finger extension even with the wrist flexed.
- *Grade III.* Inability to actively extend the fingers.

Algorithmic link: Grades I and IIA are most suited for functional surgical reconstruction (transfers, lengthenings); IIB and III are more suited for palliative correction of hygiene and cosmetic deformity.

**Surgical options.**

- *Flexor carpi ulnaris to extensor carpi radialis brevis transfer* (Green and colleagues) — the standard solution for an overactive FCU with wrist flexion-ulnar deviation.
- *Wrist tenodesis or arthrodesis* for severe deformity.
- *"Thumb-in-palm" correction* — release of adductor pollicis and flexor pollicis brevis, reconstruction of abductor pollicis longus or transfer of brachioradialis to extensor pollicis longus, with metacarpophalangeal-1 arthrodesis if required.
- *Finger flexor lengthening* (Z-plasties of flexor digitorum superficialis and/or profundus) or FDS-to-FDP transfer (Green) for severe flexion deformity.
- *Botulinum toxin* into overactive muscles — alternative and a preoperative "trial."

The functional goals are improvement of grasp, release, and hygiene; cosmetic improvement is secondary.

</details>

## Neuromuscular scoliosis
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="spine">
<details>
<summary>Neuromuscular scoliosis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Neuromuscular scoliosis in CP develops in approximately 70% of patients at GMFCS V and at substantially lower proportions at higher functional levels. Curves are typically long C-shaped with pelvic obliquity, producing asymmetry in sitting, a tilt of the pelvic girdle, and problems with hygiene and seating.

**Natural history.** Progression begins in early adolescence and continues after skeletal maturity — a characteristic of neuromuscular curves distinguishing them from idiopathic. The curve can reach 80–120° if progression is uncontrolled.

**Bracing.** A brace has a limited role — it may improve sitting balance and delay progression but does not reliably prevent the eventual disease. Used to defer surgery until skeletal maturity.

**Surgical management.** A long instrumented posterior spinal arthrodesis *T2 to pelvis* is the standard treatment when the curve produces functional sitting difficulty or is progressing rapidly. Pelvic fixation (S2-alar-iliac screws, or the Galveston technique with the intraosseous L-rosette of Allen and Ferguson) is critical for correction of the pelvic axis.

**Complications.** Complication rates are substantial — 10–20% for major complications, including deep infection, pulmonary decompensation (especially in GMFCS V with associated restrictive lung disease), bleeding (5–10% of total blood volume), pseudarthrosis, and instrumentation failure. Perioperative care requires a multidisciplinary team — anesthesiologist, pulmonologist, neonatologist/pediatric intensivist, nutritionist, and social worker.

</details>
</section>



## Principles of management
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="management">
<details>
<summary>Principles of management</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Botulinum toxin
<details id="botox">
<summary>Botulinum toxin</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Botulinum toxin type A (BTX-A; Botox, Dysport, Xeomin) injected into spastic muscle produces temporary chemodenervation by blocking presynaptic acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction. The action lasts 3–6 months and recovery is complete.

**Indications.** Dynamic spasticity (without fixed contracture) of gastrocnemius, tibialis posterior, iliopsoas, the adductors, the hamstrings, and dynamic upper-limb deformity. As a *trial intervention* — to assess the response to be expected from planned definitive surgical lengthening.

**Dosing.** Weight-based: BTX-A 6–10 IU/kg per muscle, with a maximum total dose of 400 IU (approximately 12 IU/kg) per session for children over 20 kg. Doses are adjusted for age and overall status. Ultrasound or electrostimulation guidance for the injection.

**Complications.** Transient local weakness (the intended effect, but in overdose it precipitates falls); systemic effects at high doses (rarely — dysphagia, generalized weakness); allergic reactions (rare). Repeated injection may produce immunologic resistance (antibodies).

</div>
</details>


### Selective dorsal rhizotomy
<details id="sdr">
<summary>Selective dorsal rhizotomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Selective dorsal rhizotomy (SDR) is a neurosurgical procedure in which a fraction (typically 30–60%) of the dorsal sensory rootlets of the lumbosacral spinal cord L2–S2 are selectively transected based on intraoperative electrophysiological identification of abnormal motor responses.

**Classical Peacock technique (Cape Town, 1980s).** A single-level laminectomy (L1) with access to the conus medullaris and cauda equina; identification of the dorsal rootlets and assessment of their EMG responses to stimulation. Rootlets with "abnormal" diffuse responses (activation of multiple non-adjacent muscle groups) are transected; those with "normal" localized responses are preserved.

**Classical indication.** An ambulatory diplegic patient at GMFCS II–III, aged 3–8 years, with isolated lower-extremity spasticity, good underlying strength (no residual weakness after spasticity is removed), no significant fixed contracture, and the cognitive capacity to participate in postoperative rehabilitation. Outside this narrow selection (older child, GMFCS IV–V, dyskinetic component, weak antagonists), the outcomes are disappointing.

**Results.** Long-term studies from St. Louis Children's Hospital (Park and colleagues) and from UK pediatric centers show sustained reduction of spasticity, improved gait, and reduced need for orthopedic intervention in the SDR cohort compared with controls. Postoperative rehabilitation is intensive (6–12 months) and critical for the outcome.

</div>
</details>


### Intrathecal baclofen
<details id="itb">
<summary>Intrathecal baclofen</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Intrathecal baclofen pump therapy (ITB) delivers baclofen — a GABA-B receptor agonist — directly to the spinal cord through an implanted reservoir (placed subcutaneously in the abdominal wall) and catheter (tunneled to a lumbar or thoracic intrathecal space).

**Indications.** Severe generalized spasticity or dystonia in non-ambulatory patients (GMFCS IV–V) in whom oral baclofen is ineffective or produces intolerable systemic side effects. ITB improves tone, facilitates positioning and care, reduces painful spasms, and improves comfort.

**Trial.** Before implantation a bolus trial of intrathecal baclofen (50 µg) is administered and the clinical response is assessed for 4–8 hours. A positive response justifies implantation.

**Technical parameters.** The pump requires refilling every 1–3 months (an ambulatory percutaneous procedure); the battery is replaced every 5–7 years; complications include catheter problems (kinking, displacement, infection), pump dysfunction, and intra-pump failure.

**Abrupt withdrawal.** Sudden cessation of intrathecal baclofen (pump failure, catheter leak) produces severe withdrawal — fever, muscular rigidity, autonomic instability with potentially fatal complications. It requires urgent management with oral baclofen and benzodiazepines and restoration of the intrathecal flow.

</div>
</details>


### Single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS)
<details id="semls">
<summary>Single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The principle of single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS) — addressing all the deformities of the lower extremity in a single operation rather than in serial interventions — was developed by Mercer Rang in Toronto in the 1970s as a response to the so-called "birthday syndrome": the practice of annual orthopedic operations through childhood that left the child in continuous rehabilitation and continuous functional instability.

**Principles.** (1) Comprehensive computerized gait analysis and clinical examination before surgery; (2) simultaneous execution of all indicated soft-tissue and bony procedures; (3) a single anesthetic and rehabilitation period; (4) intensive postoperative rehabilitation over 6–12 months.

**A typical SEMLS programme** in a diplegic child with crouch gait (Rodda IV–V):

- Bilateral adductor tenotomy for hip subluxation.
- Bilateral iliopsoas lengthening over the pelvic brim.
- Bilateral proximal femoral derotation osteotomy for excessive anteversion.
- Bilateral distal femoral extension osteotomy for knee flexion contracture.
- Bilateral patellar tendon advancement for patella alta.
- Bilateral medial hamstring lengthening.
- Bilateral rectus femoris transfer for stiff-knee gait.
- Supramalleolar rotational osteotomy for tibial torsion.
- Bilateral Evans calcaneal lengthening for equinoplanovalgus.
- Bilateral gastrocnemius recession for equinus.

**Age.** Typically 7–12 years — after the maturation of gait (about age 7), before significant bony remodeling, and before the durable functional deterioration of adolescence. The clinical judgment is demanding — too early an intervention carries the risk of recurrence; too late, of belated correction and durable secondary problems.

**Results.** Browne, Graham and colleagues from the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne (1990s) demonstrate substantial and durable gains in gait quality, energy expenditure, and function with SEMLS compared with conventional serial interventions. SEMLS has become the international standard for the operative care of the ambulatory CP child.

</div>
</details>


### Orthoses
<details id="orthoses">
<summary>Orthoses</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Ankle-foot orthoses (AFOs) are central to the orthotic management of CP gait:

- *Static AFOs* — hold the ankle at a fixed angle (typically 0–5° dorsiflexion), preventing equinus in stance and swing.
- *Ground-reaction (floor-reaction) AFOs* — rigid polypropylene constructions with a front panel above the ankle, converting the forefoot ground-reaction force into a knee extension moment. Particularly useful in crouch gait.
- *Articulated (hinged) AFOs* — permit dorsiflexion (for normalized progression in terminal stance) but limit plantarflexion (to prevent equinus in swing). Suitable for milder involvement.
- *Supramalleolar Orthosis (SMO)* — for milder hindfoot deformity.
- *Knee-Ankle-Foot Orthosis (KAFO)* and *Reciprocating Gait Orthosis (RGO)* — for more severe involvement, mainly for standing and limited walking in GMFCS IV.

The choice depends on gait pattern, contractures, and treatment goals. Coordination between orthopedist, physiotherapist, and orthotist is critical.

</div>
</details>

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Adult life with CP
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="adult">
<details>
<summary>Adult life with CP</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Adults with CP — particularly those at lower GMFCS levels — face a distinct set of orthopedic and medical problems. The experience of continually declining function in adulthood is the subject of growing clinical literature and advocacy.

**Progressive arthritis.** Particularly in the knees and hips in SEMLS-operated patients; in the spine from old neuromuscular scoliosis; in the upper limbs from sustained crutch or wheelchair use.

**Back pain.** From sagittal imbalance, degenerative spine disease, and compensatory hyperintensive muscle activity. Pain is often the first symptom that motivates an adult patient with CP to seek medical care.

**"Adult CP gait deterioration".** A worsening of gait pattern after the third decade — a well-recognized phenomenon reflecting the cumulative effects of contracture, weakness, and degenerative joint disease. It may require assistive devices the patient previously did not need, or a transition from GMFCS II to III, or III to IV.

**Pressure sores.** In non-ambulatory patients and those with severely restricted positional capacity — typically over the sacrum, the trochanters, the fibular heads, and the heels. They require multilayered prevention: a carefully chosen seat, dynamic positioning, skin care.

**The transition from pediatric to adult orthopedic care** is a recognized challenge — relatively few adult orthopedic surgeons are trained in the specific issues of adult CP, and specialized adult CP centers are emerging only slowly.

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="local">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Contributions to the orthopedic management of CP by continent and country; ordered alphabetically by country.

**Argentina <sup class="natflag">AR</sup>.** Eduardo Zancolli — the classification of wrist and finger function in spastic CP (Grades I, IIA, IIB, III), standard for surgical planning of the upper limb; reconstructive procedures for thumb-in-palm and for overactive flexor carpi ulnaris.

**Australia <sup class="natflag">AU</sup>.** Kerr Graham and colleagues from the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne are an international leader in contemporary CP management: the Australian Hip Surveillance Program (Wynter, Gibson, Kentish, Love; AHSP), standardized on GMFCS-based protocols and adopted as a national standard; the systematic development of single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS) with computerized gait analysis (Royal Children's Gait Laboratory); a rich scientific literature on botulinum toxin in pediatric spasticity (Graham, Boyd, Love); and the Functional Mobility Scale (FMS, Graham and colleagues, 2004) as a clinical instrument for measuring functional outcome over varied distances.

**Bulgaria <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Boycho Boychev (1908–1992), Yanko Holevich and Kaspar Karchinov — the Bulgarian orthopedic school left a specific contribution to the management of paralytic foot deformities. The *Karchinov procedure* — a trans-lateral bone block for stabilization of the planovalgus foot in the older child following failed Evans lengthening — is described in Karchinov K, *Acta Orthop Belg*, and in the Bulgarian operative literature. The *Boychev–Imamaliev Z-shortening* is used for correction of knee flexion contracture. Holevich, Dzherov and Vladimirov described lavsanoplasty grafts to reinforce the abductors in paralytic hip instability.

**Canada <sup class="natflag">CA</sup>.** Mercer Rang (Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, 1970s) — the conceptual introduction of single-event multilevel surgery and the critique of the "birthday syndrome." Robert B. Salter — the innovative pelvic osteotomy (1961), applicable to paralytic hip dysplasia with appropriate acetabular deficit. The GMFCS — developed at McMaster University (Palisano, Rosenbaum, Walter, 1997).

**Denmark <sup class="natflag">DK</sup>.** Henning Reimers (Aarhus, 1980) — the description of the migration percentage, the standard radiographic measure for paralytic hip subluxation. The classical thesis in *Acta Orthop Scand Suppl* 184 (1980) remains a model of clinical-radiographic study.

**Finland <sup class="natflag">FI</sup>.** Salo, Mäenpää, Sintonen and colleagues — a standardized approach to the management of spastic hemiplegia in the Scandinavian tradition; inclusion in the Helsinki Botulinum Toxin Working Group.

**Germany / Austria <sup class="natflag">DE</sup>.** Wieslaw Dega (1898–1989), Polish by origin and working chiefly in Poznań but trained in the German orthopedic tradition, described the supra-acetabular incomplete pelvic osteotomy (1969), a standard procedure for paralytic dysplasia of the acetabulum with posterolateral deficit. Strayer (US, trained under Steindler) described the gastrocnemius aponeurotomy (1950) — the standard approach for isolated gastrocnemius equinus. Vulpius (1913) — V-Y lengthening of the gastrocnemius-soleus. Tubby and Stoffel (Germany, early 20th century) — obturator neurectomy for paralytic hip contracture, historical.

**Italy <sup class="natflag">IT</sup>.** Alessandro Codivilla — combined procedures for paralytic equinus foot and for hip dislocation in the first decade of the 20th century, in the tradition of Risorgimento Italian orthopedics.

**Poland <sup class="natflag">PL</sup>.** Wieslaw Dega (see also DE) — founder of the Polish orthopedic school, originator of the supra-acetabular incomplete pelvic osteotomy, central contribution to paralytic hip reconstruction.

**Russia / USSR <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Tatyana Sergeevna Zatsepin (1885–1951) — obturator neurectomy for paralytic adductor contracture; early descriptions of tibialis posterior transfers. G. A. Ilizarov (Kurgan) — application of transosseous distraction osteosynthesis to complex knee flexion contracture and to paralytic foot deformity, particularly in rigid crouch posture unsuited to standard surgery. Vredensky and contemporaries — postwar development of pediatric orthopedic surgery in the Union research institutes. The Volkov–Oganesyan distractor — for elbow and knee contractures, used also in severe CP cases.

**South Africa <sup class="natflag">ZA</sup>.** Warwick Peacock (Cape Town) — the modern technique of selective dorsal rhizotomy (1980s), transferred to St. Louis, London, and other leading centers and established as a durably effective procedure in selected diplegic patients.

**Sweden <sup class="natflag">SE</sup>.** Nils Silfverskiöld (1923) — the test for distinguishing isolated gastrocnemius from combined triceps contracture, foundational for surgical planning. Gunnar Hägglund and colleagues (Lund) — the *Cerebral Palsy Follow-Up Programme* (CPUP, 1994), the model for hip surveillance programmes in Scandinavia and Europe; the Swedish demographic studies are foundational for the contemporary understanding of the natural history of paralytic dislocation.

**United States <sup class="natflag">US</sup>.** Eugene Bleck (Stanford) — *Orthopaedic Management in Cerebral Palsy* (1987; 2nd ed. 2008), the first comprehensive operative textbook grounded in gait analysis; the classical approach to the orthopedic management of CP. Freeman Miller (DuPont, Wilmington) — *Cerebral Palsy* (Springer 2005; 2nd ed. 2020), a second-generation operative textbook extended with MRI imaging and computerized analysis. James R. Gage (Gillette Children's, Saint Paul) — introduction of standardized computerized gait analysis, the kinematic-kinetic classification, and *The Identification and Treatment of Gait Problems in CP* (Mac Keith Press 2009). David Sutherland (San Diego) — pioneering work on the normal development of childhood gait, foundational for recognizing the deviations in CP. Mubarak and Wenger (San Diego) — San Diego modification of the Dega osteotomy. Mark Castle (1978) — proximal femoral resection as a salvage procedure. Sutherland, Olshen et al. — a graphical approach to presenting results of gait analysis.

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical line — the description of "Little's disease"</strong>
  <p>William John Little (London, 1810–1894) — the founder of the management of spastic diplegia in the modern sense. Himself disabled by childhood poliomyelitis, he studied under Stromeyer in Hanover (1834–35), underwent subcutaneous Achilles tenotomy, returned to London, and founded the Royal Orthopaedic Hospital. In 1862 he published the lecture *On the Influence of Abnormal Parturition, Difficult Labours, Premature Birth, and Asphyxia Neonatorum, on the Mental and Physical Condition of the Child, Especially in Relation to Deformities* (Trans Obstet Soc Lond 3:293–344) — the original description of spastic diplegia as the result of premature birth and perinatal complications. The condition bears his name in the older English literature.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Little 1862</strong> — *On the Influence of Abnormal Parturition...*, Trans Obstet Soc Lond 3:293–344. <a href="https://archive.org/details/transactionsobs00britgoog">Facsimile</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>Stromeyer 1838</strong> — *Beiträge zur operativen Orthopädik*, Hanover — subcutaneous tenotomy as a method.</li>
    <li><strong>Phelps 1891</strong> — *The prevention of deformities and the treatment of paralysis*, New York — an early systematic account of surgical options.</li>
    <li><strong>Nicoladoni 1882</strong> — Vienna — first description of a tendon transfer for paralytic foot.</li>
    <li><strong>Silfverskiöld 1923</strong> — *Acta Chir Scand* 56:315–330 — clinical test distinguishing gastrocnemius and soleus components.</li>
    <li><strong>Vulpius 1913</strong> — *Die operative Orthopädie der Gegenwart* — V-Y lengthening of gastrocnemius-soleus.</li>

  /* V4 ortho-15: open the target <details> when the page loads with a hash
     or when the reader clicks an in-page anchor. Native <details> isn't
     auto-expanded by :target alone, so we wire a tiny progressive enhancement. */
  (function () {
    function openByHash(hash) {
      if (!hash) return;
      var id = hash.replace(/^#/, "");
      if (!id) return;
      var el = document.getElementById(id);
      if (!el) return;
      if (el.tagName === "DETAILS") el.open = true;
      var inner = el.querySelector(":scope > details");
      if (inner) inner.open = true;
      var node = el.parentElement;
      while (node && node !== document.body) {
        if (node.tagName === "DETAILS") node.open = true;
        node = node.parentElement;
      }
      requestAnimationFrame(function () {
        el.scrollIntoView({ behavior: "smooth", block: "start" });
      });
    }
    function wire() {
      openByHash(window.location.hash);
      document.addEventListener("click", function (e) {
        var t = e.target;
        while (t && t !== document) {
          if (t.tagName === "A" && t.getAttribute && t.getAttribute("href") && t.getAttribute("href").charAt(0) === "#") {
            openByHash(t.getAttribute("href"));
            break;
          }
          t = t.parentNode;
        }
      });
      window.addEventListener("hashchange", function () { openByHash(window.location.hash); });
    }
    if (document.readyState === "loading") {
      document.addEventListener("DOMContentLoaded", wire);
    } else {
      wire();
    }
  })();
