## Introduction and scope

The systemic skeletal dysplasias (osteochondrodysplasiae) are a heterogeneous, genetically determined group of inherited disorders of bone and cartilage that disrupt normal skeletal growth, shape, mineralization, and integrity. They are distinguished from the dysostoses (developmental disorders of individual bones) by their generalized and progressive character: the defect affects the entire population of chondrocytes, osteoblasts, or osteoclasts, and so any skeletal segment may be involved. The International Nosology and Classification of Genetic Skeletal Disorders (most recent revision, 2023) recognizes more than 770 distinct entities grouped into 42 categories by molecular pathology, radiographic pattern, and clinical presentation. This is a substantial expansion from the 2019 revision (461 entities in 42 groups) and from the early classifications of the Maroteaux and Spranger era (about 50 entities in the 1970s), and reflects the explosive expansion of molecular-genetic dissection of these disorders over the last two decades.

Although individually rare, the skeletal dysplasias collectively affect approximately 1 in 5,000 live births and produce a substantial portion of pediatric and adult orthopedic practice. The orthopedic surgeon must be able to: (1) recognize the clinical features of the principal dysplasias and reach a preliminary working diagnosis from clinical examination and survey radiographs; (2) anticipate the characteristic complications of each entity — upper cervical instability, foramen magnum stenosis, spinal stenosis, progressive joint deformities, pathological fractures, scoliosis, and growth-plate problems; (3) coordinate care with the pediatrician, geneticist, neurosurgeon, spinal surgeon, anesthesiologist, endocrinologist, ophthalmologist, otolaryngologist, cardiologist, and physiotherapist; and (4) accept that every operative plan in these patients requires individualized weighing of benefits against the elevated anesthetic, instrumentation, and biological risks.

This chapter synthesizes content from Spranger, Brill, Superti-Furga and Nishimura *Bone Dysplasias: An Atlas of Genetic Disorders of Skeletal Development* (4th ed.), Taybi and Lachman *Radiology of Syndromes, Metabolic Disorders and Skeletal Dysplasias* (5th ed.), McKusick and Beighton *Heritable Disorders of Connective Tissue* (5th ed.), the latest revision of the International Nosology of Genetic Skeletal Disorders, Tachdjian's *Pediatric Orthopaedics* and Lovell and Winter's *Pediatric Orthopaedics* (8th ed.), addressing the principal categories of skeletal dysplasia and their orthopedic implications.

## Molecular pathology, classification, and nosology

Modern classification of the skeletal dysplasias rests on four intersecting axes: molecular (affected gene and protein), biochemical (metabolic or structural defect), radiographic (pattern of involvement along the limb and segment of long bone), and clinical (severity, timing, associated extra-skeletal features). The International Nosology organizes the dysplasias into groups that correspond principally to the affected molecular signaling pathways or structural proteins.

By molecular pathology the principal functional categories are: defects in extracellular matrix structural proteins (type I collagen — osteogenesis imperfecta; type II — achondrogenesis II, SED congenita, Kniest, Stickler; oligomeric matrix proteins — pseudoachondroplasia, MED); defects in signaling receptors and pathways (FGFR3 — achondroplasia, hypochondroplasia, thanatophoric dysplasia; PTH1R — Jansen); defects in matrix metabolism (SLC26A2 — diastrophic dysplasia, atelosteogenesis II; lysosomal hydrolases — mucopolysaccharidoses); defects in ossification and bone turnover (osteoclast function — osteopetroses; ALPL — hypophosphatasia; FGF23 — X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets); defects in transcription factors controlling skeletal development (RUNX2 — cleidocranial dysplasia; SOX9 — campomelic dysplasia); and ciliopathies (asphyxiating thoracic dystrophies, Ellis–van Creveld).

By radiographic pattern the dysplasias are characterized by the predominant site of involvement in the long bone: epiphyseal (MED), metaphyseal (Schmid, Jansen, McKusick), diaphyseal (Camurati-Engelmann), spondylo-epiphyseal, and spondylo-epi-metaphyseal. By pattern of limb shortening: rhizomelic (humerus and femur — achondroplasia, thanatophoric), mesomelic (forearms and lower legs — dyschondrosteosis, Léri-Weill), acromelic (hands and feet), and micromelic (all segments — achondrogenesis).

Antenatal diagnosis plays a growing role: third-trimester ultrasound detects severe and lethal forms, and molecular genetic testing allows confirmation and informed prenatal counseling.

## Orthopedic evaluation of the patient

Evaluation of a patient with suspected skeletal dysplasia begins with a detailed history (antenatal workup, birth weight and length, family history of short stature, consanguinity, developmental milestones, fractures, joint and neurological problems) and a complete physical examination focused on: body and limb proportions; analysis of segmental shortening (rhizo-, meso-, or acromelic); head (macrocephaly, frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia); eyes (blue sclerae, corneal clouding); oral cavity (cleft palate, dentinogenesis imperfecta); neck and chest; hands (trident, hitchhiker thumb, joint hypermobility); joint contractures; and full neurological examination.

The radiographic survey — the "skeletal survey" — contains a standardized set of projections of the skull, cervical spine, chest, pelvis, and all limbs. Interpretation applies the diagnostic guides of Spranger/Brill/Superti-Furga/Nishimura and Taybi-Lachman, organized by the leading finding: severe spinal changes with platyspondyly, epi- vs meta-/diaphyseal involvement, specific vertebral patterns ("rugger-jersey," "bone-in-bone"), and pelvic patterns (trident acetabulum).

## The FGFR3 chondrodysplasias

A series of related disorders is caused by activating mutations of various potency in the FGFR3 gene (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3), encoding a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor expressed in the resting, proliferative, and prehypertrophic zones of the cartilage growth plate. Constitutively activated FGFR3 inhibits chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation through STAT-1 and MAPK signaling and produces the characteristic rhizomelic shortening. Phenotype severity tracks with the activating potency of the individual mutation: mild (hypochondroplasia), moderate (achondroplasia), and severe (thanatophoric dysplasia, SADDAN — severe achondroplasia with developmental delay and acanthosis nigricans).

## Achondroplasia

Achondroplasia is the commonest non-lethal skeletal dysplasia of disproportionate short stature, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 25,000–40,000 live births. The condition is autosomal dominant: approximately 80% of cases represent new mutations (associated with advanced paternal age and explained by positive selection of mutation-carrying spermatogonial stem cells), and 20% are inherited from an affected parent. The homozygous state is lethal in infancy with a phenotype resembling thanatophoric dysplasia. The causative mutation is almost exclusively at codon 380 of FGFR3 (G380R), resulting from a G→A substitution at position 1138 of mRNA.

The classical clinical picture includes: rhizomelic shortening (the upper arms and thighs disproportionately shorter than the forearms and legs, so that in the upright position the hands reach only to the greater trochanter); average adult height ~131 cm in men and ~124 cm in women; macrocephaly with frontal bossing and depressed nasal bridge; midface hypoplasia; trident hands (with separation of the third and fourth fingers); thoracolumbar kyphosis in infancy that improves with weight-bearing and resolves in most cases; lumbar hyperlordosis in childhood and adulthood; short metacarpal hand with relatively long fingers; restricted elbow extension; and frequent genu varum (in approximately 30%). Milestones of sitting, walking, and speaking are mildly delayed, but intellectual development is normal, and life expectancy is near normal — except for the elevated infant risk of sudden death from cervicomedullary compression and the elevated adult cardiovascular risk.

The radiographic features are pathognomonic and allow diagnosis from birth in experienced hands: narrow lambdoid sutures, frontal bossing, small foramen magnum; square iliac wings with narrow sciatic notches; small cuboidal vertebral bodies with progressive narrowing of the interpedicular distance from L1 to L5 (the inverse of normal); rhizomelic shortening of the long bones with metaphyseal flaring ("chevron sign" of the distal femoral metaphysis); and short metacarpals.

## Foramen magnum stenosis in achondroplasia

Foramen magnum stenosis is the critical early orthopedic/neurosurgical complication of achondroplasia. The narrow foramen magnum (typically <30 mm² cross-section, compared with >100 mm² in age-matched controls) produces compression of the cervicomedullary junction with risks of: central sleep apnea and obstructive events, which may present as episodes of cyanosis, apnea, or sudden death in sleep; quadriparesis or hypotonia; delayed motor milestones; and in severe cases, sudden death. Screening with neurological examination, polysomnography, and MRI of the cervicomedullary junction is recommended practice in every infant with achondroplasia. Surgical decompression (suboccipital craniectomy with C1 cervical laminectomy, sometimes including C2) is required in approximately 5–10% of infants with objective symptomatology, demonstrable canal narrowing, and/or high-signal myelopathy on MRI.

## Spinal stenosis in achondroplasia

Spinal stenosis is the principal problem in adult patients with achondroplasia. It is produced by short pedicles and narrow interpedicular distances reversing the normal caudal widening from T12 to L5 (in achondroplasia the canal narrows progressively caudally); short laminae; and accelerated development of disc degeneration and spondylosis. Progressive neurogenic claudication, buttock and thigh pain on walking, paresthesias, and gradually developing sensorimotor deficits typically appear in the third to fifth decade. Surgical decompression — wide multilevel laminectomy with medial facetectomy, typically from L1 to S1, often extending cranially to T10 — is frequently necessary, often combined with fusion when degenerative spondylolisthesis, hyperlordosis, or segmental instability is present. Results are good in selected patients, but complications (dural tear, postoperative hematoma, protracted neurological recovery from chronic compression) are more common than in non-achondroplastic patients.

## Limb lengthening in achondroplasia

Limb-lengthening procedures, developed in the Soviet school by Ilizarov and refined by Catagni, Cattaneo, De Bastiani, Aldegheri, and many contemporary centers, are an established option for patients with achondroplasia who seek increased stature and improved function. A typical pediatric schedule includes bilateral lower-leg lengthening with Ilizarov apparatus (typical gain 8–10 cm), followed by bilateral femoral lengthening (typical gain 10–12 cm) and often bilateral humeral lengthening (typical gain 5–8 cm), with a total potential gain of 25–30 cm over a 4–6-year course. Modern motorized intramedullary devices (PRECICE and related) are an alternative in centers where they are available, reducing external fixator time at the cost of less axial control. The decision to undertake lengthening remains individualized, with full informed discussion of duration, pain, risks (nerve injury, joint contracture, regenerate failure), and the social and psychological dimensions, increasingly tied to multidisciplinary counseling.

## Vosoritide pharmacotherapy

Vosoritide, a bioengineered analog of C-type natriuretic peptide, downregulates FGFR3-induced MAPK signaling in the growth plate and in trials in children with achondroplasia increases linear growth velocity by approximately 1.5 cm/year over placebo. The agent is approved in many jurisdictions since 2021 as a daily subcutaneous injection in pediatric patients above a defined age. Long-term functional benefits (canal stenosis, sleep-disordered breathing, orthopedic complications, final adult stature) remain to be defined; safety data in adulthood and functional outcomes are still accumulating.

## Hypochondroplasia

Hypochondroplasia is caused by different FGFR3 mutations (typically at codon 540 — N540K — rather than 380) and produces a milder phenotype than achondroplasia — mild to moderate disproportionate short stature, relatively short hands and fingers, mild genu varum, lumbar hyperlordosis, sometimes macrocephaly. Facial features are less marked or absent. The condition is often not diagnosed until later in childhood, and in the mildest end of the spectrum may not present until adulthood or as an incidental radiographic finding. Orthopedic implications are similar to achondroplasia but generally milder and spinal stenosis is less frequent.

## Thanatophoric dysplasia

Thanatophoric dysplasia (Greek thanatos — death and phoros — bearing) is the severe, typically perinatally lethal form of the FGFR3 chondrodysplasias. Two radiographic types are distinguished: type I with curved long bones ("telephone-receiver" femurs) and a normal skull shape; and type II with straight long bones and a characteristic cloverleaf skull. The clinical picture at birth includes severe rhizomelic shortening, a narrow chest with short ribs, a large head with frontal bossing, and almost universal death in the first hours or days of life from respiratory failure due to thoracic narrowness. A very small population of patients survive with intensive ventilatory support and tracheostomy, raising complex ethical and medical issues.

## Pseudoachondroplasia

Pseudoachondroplasia is caused by mutations in the COMP gene (cartilage oligomeric matrix protein) and produces a phenotype superficially resembling achondroplasia but with several critical differences: normal facial features and normal head proportions at birth (so that patients are often not diagnosed until the end of the first or beginning of the second year of life when prominent short stature and waddling gait appear); coxa magna with progressive hip dysplasia and early coxarthrosis; elbow and knee instability with generalized joint hypermobility; and importantly — upper cervical instability from odontoid hypoplasia. Cervical assessment with dynamic lateral radiography (flexion/extension) in every patient is standard; contact sports and uncontrolled head/neck maneuvers are avoided, and prophylactic C1–C2 fusion is considered in patients with mechanical or neurological signs. Radiographic features include "anisospondyly" with anterior tongue-shaped vertebral bodies and severe epiphyseal changes.

## Diastrophic dysplasia

Diastrophic dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the SLC26A2 gene (DTDST — diastrophic dysplasia sulfate transporter) encoding a cell membrane sulfate transporter. The defect produces under-sulfation of proteoglycan GAG chains in cartilage and yields multi-system involvement of cartilaginous and cartilage-containing structures. The condition is endemic in Finland (where the carrier frequency reaches 1 in 70 and the incidence 1 in 30,000 — a founder effect) but is substantially rarer elsewhere. The same gene, in more severe mutations, causes atelosteogenesis type II (more severe, perinatally lethal) and achondrogenesis type 1B (most severe, early perinatal death), forming a disease spectrum.

The classical clinical features include: severe disproportionate short stature; "hitchhiker thumb" (proximally placed thumb with characteristic radial deviation — a pathognomonic sign); cauliflower ear from intrauterine inflammation of the auricular cartilage; cleft palate (in ~50%); bilateral severe rigid clubfeet, notoriously resistant to standard Ponseti management; cervical kyphosis with potential myelopathy (an important early orthopedic concern); severe progressive scoliosis; and joint contractures with multiple joint dysplasias. The hips are dysplastic with a tendency to early coxarthrosis and dislocation. The knees often have fixed flexion contractures and pathological patellar dislocation.

Orthopedic management is exceptionally challenging. Clubfoot management includes early aggressive casting in an attempt at flexibility, but definitive correction typically requires extensive surgery (posterior or combined posteromedial release, talectomy in selected cases). Cervical kyphosis requires strict surveillance with dynamic radiographs — progression may demand anterior-posterior stabilizing fusion. Scoliosis is often severe and progressive in childhood, tolerating bracing poorly and requiring early surgical fusion in selected cases. Hips often require osteotomy in childhood and early arthroplasty in adulthood. Finnish and Swedish collectives have accumulated the largest clinical experience and have defined many of the standards of care.

## Atelosteogenesis and achondrogenesis

Atelosteogenesis and achondrogenesis represent severe to lethal skeletal dysplasias. Achondrogenesis type IA (TRIP11), IB (SLC26A2), and II (COL2A1) present at birth with severe micromelic shortening, a small chest, and respiratory failure, typically with perinatal death. Atelosteogenesis I (FLNB), II (SLC26A2), and III (FLNB) are also severe, usually lethal in the neonatal or early infantile period, with severe rhizomelic shortening, dislocations, and characteristic "gumdrop" long bones with severe metaphyseal flaring.

## Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED)

Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED) is a group of autosomal dominant (often new-mutation) disorders caused by mutations in COMP, COL9A1, COL9A2, COL9A3, MATN3 (matrilin-3), and SLC26A2 (the recessive form). The condition affects principally the epiphyses of the long bones, producing characteristic irregular ossification with later development of premature degenerative arthritis of the hips, knees, shoulders, and ankles.

Classically the severe form of Fairbank (with multi-joint involvement and pronounced short stature) and the mild form of Ribbing (predominantly hip involvement and near-normal stature) are distinguished. Modern molecular data show that this division reflects the spectrum of phenotypic severity by affected gene and point mutation rather than separate entities.

Clinical presentation is typically in middle childhood (4–10 years) with intermittent joint pain, mild to moderate short stature, and waddling gait. Radiographs show small, fragmented, irregular epiphyses of the long bones — particularly the femoral heads (where MED often mimics bilateral Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease), the distal femoral and proximal tibial epiphyses (where it often produces genu valgum), and the talus. The vertebral bodies are nearly normal (distinguishing MED from SED), although mild changes are possible. The bilateral, symmetric, multi-joint pattern combined with family history distinguishes MED from Perthes.

Treatment is principally protective: limitation of high-impact activities (especially during the most active growth-plate period); weight management; adequate analgesia; and physical therapy to maintain range of motion. Surgical management is reserved for established complications: corrective osteotomy for varus or valgus deformity (especially around the knee); joint-preserving procedures for hip dysplasia (Bernese, Salter, or PAO in individual cases); and ultimately joint replacement for established arthritis, often in the fourth or fifth decade of life — decades earlier than in primary osteoarthritis. Hip and knee replacement results in MED are generally satisfactory but with a higher rate of instrumentation issues due to abnormal bone architecture.

## Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia (SED)

Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia exists in several principal forms, united by characteristic involvement of the vertebral bodies (platyspondyly) and epiphyses.

SED congenita is autosomal dominant, caused by mutations in COL2A1 (type II collagen), and presents at birth with severe disproportionate short stature (predominantly short trunk), flattened or flat face with hypertelorism, cleft palate in about one third of cases, progressive myopia with risk of retinal detachment, and odontoid hypoplasia with atlantoaxial instability. Radiographic features include flattened irregular vertebral bodies with anterior beaking in childhood and severely flattened "pancake" vertebrae (platyspondyly) in adulthood; delayed ossification of the pubic bones, proximal femoral epiphyses, and cuboids. Epiphyseal findings in the hips and knees parallel those of MED with early arthritis.

SED tarda presents in late childhood (typically 4–8 years) with relatively short stature (less severe than in congenita), mild facial features, and progressive joint problems. In many cases it is X-linked recessive (SEDL/TRAPPC2) and affects predominantly males; autosomal dominant forms are also described. Radiographic features are predominantly in the spine with characteristic "hump-back" or "dome-shaped" superior vertebral bodies and matrix accumulation along the posterior and superior corners.

Atlantoaxial instability from odontoid hypoplasia is the critical orthopedic concern in SED congenita, requiring careful assessment with dynamic radiographs and often CT or MRI of the cranio-vertebral junction. Prophylactic posterior C1–C2 fusion (typically transarticular screw fixation or Goel-Harms fixation) is advocated in many patients, especially with mechanical signs (atlanto-dental interval >5 mm) or neurological symptoms. Scoliosis is common and often progressive, requiring bracing and selected surgical fusion. Hip and knee problems require progressive joint-preserving and ultimately joint-replacing treatment.

Retinal detachment is a recognized severe complication of type II collagen mutations (seen in both SED congenita and Stickler syndrome) and requires lifelong ophthalmologic surveillance.

## Metatropic dysplasia and Kniest syndrome

Metatropic dysplasia (a TRPV4-pathy) is characterized by vertebral platyspondyly, metaphyseal flaring, and progressive severe scoliosis, due to which body proportions change with growth (newborns appear with relatively long trunk, adults with short trunk). Cervical and thoracic kyphosis, early hip arthritis, and scoliosis are the main orthopedic problems.

Kniest syndrome is a COL2A1-pathy with severe myopia and retinal detachment, conductive and sensorineural deafness, cleft palate, severe progressive joint problems, and characteristic metaphyseal flaring of the femurs resembling "candelabrum stems."

## Metaphyseal chondrodysplasias

The metaphyseal chondrodysplasias are a group of conditions characterized by abnormal metaphyseal ossification with sparing of the epiphyses. The principal forms are:

Schmid form (autosomal dominant, COL10A1 — type X collagen mutation): the commonest form; presents in late childhood with mild to moderate short stature, coxa vara, genu varum, and waddling gait. Radiographic features are distal metaphyseal flaring and irregularity of the femoral and tibial metaphyses and the proximal humerus. Orthopedic management focuses on coxa vara (valgus osteotomies) and varus deformities of the knee (corrective osteotomies or guided growth).

Jansen form (autosomal dominant, activating mutation in PTH1R — the receptor for PTH and PTHrP): severe short stature with constitutive PTH-receptor activation producing persistent hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, and often nephrocalcinosis. Severe metaphyseal expansion produces a dramatic radiographic picture with "cup-shaped" metaphyseal patterns.

McKusick form (cartilage-hair hypoplasia) (autosomal recessive, RMRP gene encoding the RNase MRP enzyme): short stature, fine sparse hair, immunodeficiency (T-cell dysfunction with elevated risk of severe infections and malignancy), Hirschsprung-like megacolon, and characteristic metaphyseal abnormalities. The condition is enriched in the Old Order Amish population (where it was originally described by McKusick in the Lancaster, Pennsylvania kindreds) and in other isolated populations.

Shwachman-Diamond form (SBDS gene mutation): metaphyseal dysplasia combined with exocrine pancreatic insufficiency and bone-marrow failure (neutropenia, risk of myelodysplasia and leukemia). Multidisciplinary management is essential.

## Osteogenesis imperfecta

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) — "brittle bone disease" — is a group of inherited disorders characterized by bone fragility and a wide spectrum of extra-skeletal manifestations. Overall incidence is approximately 1 in 15,000–20,000 live births. Classically OI is regarded as a disorder of type I collagen (the principal structural protein of bone, encoded by COL1A1 on 17q21 and COL1A2 on 7q22), but more recent molecular dissection has revealed a growing number of non-collagen forms due to defects in post-translational processing, secretion, and mineralization of collagen.

## Sillence classification of OI

The classical Sillence classification (1979) recognizes four principal types on clinical and radiographic grounds:

Type I (mild): Autosomal dominant, caused by a quantitative reduction of type I collagen (typically from a nonsense mutation in COL1A1 producing haploinsufficiency). Mild to moderate bone fragility, usually blue sclerae, normal or near-normal stature, dentinogenesis imperfecta in approximately 50% (type IB), normal intellect, and relatively normal life expectancy. Multiple long-bone fractures in childhood, decreasing in frequency at puberty.

Type II (lethal): Autosomal dominant new mutations (rarely autosomal recessive) producing severe qualitative collagen abnormalities. Multiple intrauterine fractures, severe deformity at birth, dark blue sclerae, a soft, unossified or poorly ossified skull, "beaded" rib cage, perinatal death from respiratory failure in most cases (>90%) in the first days or weeks of life.

Type III (severe, progressively deforming): Autosomal dominant new mutations (sometimes autosomal recessive). Severe bone fragility with progressive deformity of long bones and skull, severely short stature, multiple fractures, severe scoliosis, often with a triangular face ("tri-pointed facial profile") and blue sclerae in childhood that lighten in adulthood. Many patients are wheelchair-dependent. Dentinogenesis imperfecta is common (type IIIB).

Type IV (moderate): Autosomal dominant. Moderate bone fragility, short stature (less severe than type III), deformity, normal or near-normal sclerae in adulthood (typically blue in infancy that lightens). Dentinogenesis imperfecta in about half (type IVB).

Modern extensions of the classification include at least 21 molecularly distinct forms, including IFITM5-linked type V with hyperplastic callus formation and ossification of the forearm interosseous membrane; CRTAP- and P3H1-linked types VII and VIII with severe deformity; SERPINF1-linked type VI with mineralization defects; WNT1-linked severe recessive forms; and others. Molecular classification is gradually replacing the Sillence classification in specialized centers but Sillence remains a useful practical reference for phenotypic severity.

## Clinical features and diagnosis of OI

The cardinal feature is bone fragility with multiple fractures from minor trauma. Other characteristic features include: short stature (variable by type); bone deformity (typical bowing of long bones from healed fractures and ongoing remodeling); blue sclerae (from a thin sclera allowing the underlying choroid to show through — most marked in types I and II); dentinogenesis imperfecta (bluish-gray brittle teeth with crowns worn nearly to the gum line, in approximately 50% of cases); hearing impairment (typically conductive in childhood, sensorineural in adulthood, from involvement of the middle-ear ossicles and cochlea); joint hypermobility; easy bruising; and additional features — malignant hyperthermia susceptibility, accelerated bone turnover, valvular heart disease in severe forms. The diagnosis is principally clinical, supported in many cases by molecular genetic testing, which also assists counseling and prenatal diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis in infancy includes non-accidental injury — a critical distinction requiring careful integration of clinical, radiological, laboratory, and social data. Laboratory features (typically normal Ca, phosphorus, and ALP) and molecular testing support discrimination; OI often shows characteristic features (triangular face, blue sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta, wormian bones in the skull on lateral view); and child abuse often shows typical fracture patterns at specific sites (metaphyseal corner fractures, posterior ribs, scapulae) with associated bruising and a negative OI molecular workup. Cooperation with a pediatric expert in child abuse is standard in every suspected case.

## Bisphosphonate therapy in OI

Bisphosphonate therapy — typically intravenous pamidronate (the Glorieux protocol: 1 mg/kg/day IV for 3 days every 4 months under 2 years, and every 4–6 months above 2 years) or zoledronate, with oral alendronate as an alternative — reduces fracture rates, increases bone density, reduces bone pain, and improves functional outcomes in OI. Optimal duration, dosing, and cessation are debated; many centers run repeated courses through childhood and adolescence, with continuation into adulthood depending on response. Known adverse reactions in children: transient acute-phase reaction with first infusion (fever, myalgia, nausea); hypocalcemia; and a theoretical but rarely described risk of jaw osteonecrosis or atypical fractures at high cumulative dose. Denosumab (anti-RANKL antibody) and new anabolic agents (sclerostin inhibitors) are in study.

## Surgical management of OI

Surgical management of OI has been transformed by the concept of growing telescoping intramedullary rods. Original Bailey-Dubow rods (two telescoping components) and modern Fassier-Duval rods (threaded ends fixing into both epiphyses) provide protective fixation that grows with the child. The principle involves multiple osteotomies along the length of a bowed long bone to straighten it, insertion of the rod, and intramedullary stabilization in a straight position. Sublocked rods, Rush rods, and Kirschner wires remain alternatives in selected situations.

Acute fracture management in OI follows the principles of routine care: appropriate reduction, immobilization for the shortest required period (because of the risk of escalating osteoporosis from prolonged immobilization), and early rehabilitation. Multiple fractures occurring around previous fixation devices require revision with longer rods.

Scoliosis in OI is complex because of poor bone quality. Bracing is poorly tolerated and largely ineffective. Surgical fusion for severe progressive curves (>50°) requires multi-anchor segmental fixation and often additional methylmethacrylate augmentation.

Basilar invagination and cranial settling — upward translation of the cervical spine into the cranium due to soft cranial bone — is a recognized late complication of severe OI. Management requires neurosurgical and skull-base input with anterior decompression, posterior stabilizing occipitocervical fusion, or combinations.

## Osteopetrosis

Osteopetrosis — "marble bone disease" — is a group of inherited disorders of osteoclast function producing dense, brittle bone from failure of normal bone resorption. The principal forms are:

Autosomal recessive (infantile, malignant) osteopetrosis: severe form presenting in infancy. The causative mutation is most often in TCIRG1 (about 50% of cases), CLCN7, OSTM1, or others. Clinical features include: bone-marrow failure (from obliteration of the marrow spaces by dense bone, with severe pancytopenia requiring transfusions and predisposing to infection); recurrent infections; hepatosplenomegaly from extramedullary hematopoiesis; cranial nerve compression from foraminal narrowing (threatening blindness in the second year of life); severe bone fragility; and untreated death in infancy and early childhood. Treatment is allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation in selected cases, providing new osteoclasts from a donor lineage — the only definitive cure, with increasingly good long-term survival in contemporary centers.

Autosomal dominant (Albers-Schönberg) osteopetrosis: typically presenting in adolescence or adulthood with multiple fractures, characteristic radiographic features (dense bones, "bone-within-bone" appearance of vertebrae, "rugger-jersey" spine with alternating bands of sclerotic and lucent vertebral endplates), and modest clinical impact. The condition is caused by mutations in CLCN7 (type II) or LRP5 (type I) and proceeds with relatively normal life expectancy. Orthopedic management focuses on fracture care.

Surgical fracture management in osteopetrosis is particularly challenging: the dense bone resists drilling and damages instruments; nonunion risk is increased due to limited vascularity of dense bone; implant complications are common; and anesthetic risks are increased due to hypercalcemia. Preoperative CT planning, specialized instruments (diamond burrs, fluoroscopic guidance), and prolonged postoperative monitoring are recommended.

## Pycnodysostosis and Camurati-Engelmann

Pycnodysostosis (CTSK mutations in cathepsin K) produces increased bone density with paradoxical fragility, short stature, acro-osteolysis of the distal phalanges, hypoplastic clavicles, and a characteristic facial phenotype; it is also known as "Toulouse-Lautrec disease."

Camurati-Engelmann disease (TGFB1 mutations; progressive diaphyseal dysplasia) causes progressive symmetric cortical thickening of the diaphyses with skull-base involvement in severe cases. Corticosteroid therapy (low-dose prednisone) is the standard and offers substantial symptom relief.

## Fibrous dysplasia and McCune-Albright

Fibrous dysplasia (a somatic GNAS1 mutation) replaces medullary bone with fibro-osteoid tissue with a characteristic "ground-glass" radiographic appearance. The monostotic form affects one bone; the polyostotic form affects many. The combination with café-au-lait pigmentation with "coast of Maine" jagged borders and endocrine hyperfunction is McCune-Albright syndrome.

Orthopedic management depends on the affected bone: the proximal femur often requires valgus-flexion osteotomy with long intramedullary fixation for the "shepherd's-crook" deformity. Bisphosphonate therapy offers pain relief.

## Mucopolysaccharidoses

The mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) are a group of lysosomal storage disorders caused by deficiency of enzymes involved in glycosaminoglycan (GAG) degradation. Accumulation of partially degraded GAGs (dermatan, heparan, keratan, and chondroitin sulfate, and hyaluronan) in lysosomes throughout the body produces multi-organ disease with characteristic skeletal manifestations grouped under the term "dysostosis multiplex."

Dysostosis multiplex is the radiographic presentation of MPS, with paddle-like ribs, J-shaped sella turcica, bullet-shaped phalanges, short broad metacarpals with proximal pointing, trident acetabula, coxa valga, vertebral beaking at the gibbus level (T12-L2), and epiphyseal changes with late degenerative changes.

The principal types are:

MPS I (Hurler — severe; Hurler-Scheie — intermediate; Scheie — mild; all from αL-iduronidase deficiency, IDUA gene). Hurler is a severe form with progressive intellectual decline, corneal clouding, severe dysostosis multiplex with gibbus, cervical instability, hip dysplasia, carpal tunnel syndrome in early childhood, and "claw hand." Modern treatment: hematopoietic stem cell transplantation before age 2 and enzyme replacement therapy (laronidase).

MPS II (Hunter): X-linked recessive, iduronate sulfatase deficiency. Similar skeletal features without corneal clouding. Treatment: idursulfase.

MPS III (Sanfilippo): four subtypes; neuropsychiatric symptomatology dominates, skeletal features are mild.

MPS IV (Morquio): severe skeletal involvement with characteristic spondylo-epi-metaphyseal dysplasia and preserved intellect. Severe short stature with short trunk. Atlantoaxial instability and spinal stenosis are the critical orthopedic concerns, requiring lifelong surveillance and frequently prophylactic posterior C1–C2 fusion in early childhood. Enzyme replacement therapy: elosulfase alfa.

MPS VI (Maroteaux-Lamy): arylsulfatase B deficiency, similar features to MPS I without intellectual involvement. Treatment: galsulfase.

MPS VII (Sly) and MPS IX: rare, with milder skeletal features.

Orthopedic management of MPS includes: prophylactic cervical fusion in selected cases; gibbus management with bracing and surgical correction for progression; spinal canal decompression; early carpal tunnel release; corrective femoral osteotomy for coxa valga; and joint replacement in surviving adults. Anesthetic risks are significant — difficult airway, risk of cervical luxation during positioning, and breathing problems from rigid chest cages.

## Mucolipidoses and Gaucher disease

The mucolipidoses (particularly type II/I-cell disease and type III/pseudo-Hurler polydystrophy from GNPTAB mutations) present with features resembling MPS due to impaired transport of multiple lysosomal enzymes.

Gaucher disease (glucocerebrosidase deficiency) presents with hepatosplenomegaly and skeletal features including femoral head avascular necrosis, "Erlenmeyer flask" appearance of the distal femoral metaphysis, pathological fractures, and bone pain. Enzyme replacement (imiglucerase) and substrate reduction (miglustat, eliglustat) have transformed the prognosis.

## Hypophosphatasia

Hypophosphatasia is an autosomal recessive (in mild forms autosomal dominant) disorder of mineralization caused by mutations in ALPL encoding tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP). Severity ranges from a perinatal lethal form, through a severe infantile form with kyphosis, fractures, and muscle weakness, to an adult form with fractures, bone pain, and premature tooth loss. Biochemically it is characterized by low alkaline phosphatase and accumulation of its substrates (pyridoxal-5'-phosphate, phosphoethanolamine, inorganic pyrophosphate). Enzyme replacement (asfotase alfa, recombinant bone-targeted TNSALP) has transformed the prognosis in severe forms with marked skeletal and neurological improvements.

## X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets

X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets (XLH) is the commonest inherited form of rickets and osteomalacia. It is caused by mutations in PHEX, raising circulating FGF23 (a phosphaturic hormone) which suppresses renal phosphate reabsorption and inhibits 1-α-hydroxylase. The result is renal hypophosphatemia with normal or slightly reduced 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D and normal calcemia. Clinical features include short stature, often with relatively normal proportions; varus or valgus deformities of the lower limbs (predominantly varus in early childhood, varus or valgus later); bone pain; and dental problems. Traditional treatment with oral phosphate and calcitriol (requiring many daily doses and often poorly tolerated) is now joined since 2018 by burosumab, an anti-FGF23 monoclonal antibody that markedly reduces clinical severity and requires only monthly or bimonthly injection. Orthopedic management of residual deformities with guided growth or corrective osteotomy remains indicated for significant mechanical problems.

## Cleidocranial dysplasia

Cleidocranial dysplasia, caused by mutations in RUNX2 (CBFA1) — the principal osteoblast-differentiation transcription factor — produces: aplasia or hypoplasia of the clavicles (allowing the patient to bring the shoulders together anteriorly — a pathognomonic feature); delayed closure of cranial fontanelles and sutures (often lifelong wide sutures) with "second" lambdoid widening; supernumerary and unerupted teeth (often requiring orthodontic and surgical uncovering); short stature; wide pubic symphysis often progressing to hip dysplasia and dislocation. Orthopedic management is largely supportive; the shoulder and clavicles rarely require surgical intervention; hip dysplasia may require specific treatment in childhood and adolescence.

## Larsen, Stickler, and Trevor

Larsen syndrome (FLNB mutations) is characterized by multiple congenital dislocations (hips, knees, elbows), a flat face with depressed nasal bridge, and severe cervical kyphoses with myelopathy risk. Knee dislocations require early casting; hip dislocations are particularly resistant to treatment.

Stickler syndrome (mutations in collagen types II, IX, or XI) produces severe myopia with retinal detachment, deafness, cleft palate, and progressive joint problems with epiphyseal dysplasia features; ophthalmologic surveillance is critical.

Trevor disease (dysplasia epiphysealis hemimelica) is cartilaginous overgrowth from one side of an epiphysis with hemimelic mass; it affects predominantly the medial tibial epiphysis or talus in boys in early childhood. Surgical excision is the treatment of choice.

## Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva

Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP) is an exceptionally rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by an activating mutation of ACVR1 (activin A receptor) that leads to progressive ossification of soft tissue — muscle, fascia, tendon, ligament — in response to trauma, biopsy, or even venipuncture. Beginning in early childhood with back and upper-limb ossification, mobility is progressively restricted to the point that patients become locked in a "second skeleton." Surgical intervention (including removal of heterotopic ossifications) is contraindicated, as it provokes a cascade of new ossification. Management is directed at trauma avoidance and care of respiratory and nutritional function. Palovarotene, a selective retinoid γ-agonist, has demonstrated slowing of new heterotopic ossification in clinical trials and has recently been approved in several jurisdictions.

## Principles of orthopedic management

The management of patients with skeletal dysplasia follows several unifying principles.

First, accurate diagnosis is essential because of the wide variation in natural history and complications. Diagnosis combines clinical features, radiographic features (a standardized skeletal survey), family history, and increasingly molecular genetic testing through targeted panels or whole-exome sequencing, often within a multidisciplinary consilium.

Second, the orthopedic surgeon must recognize systemic features and coordinate care with the pediatrician, geneticist, neurosurgeon, spinal surgeon, cardiologist, endocrinologist, ophthalmologist, and physiotherapist; many dysplasias have multi-system manifestations requiring lifelong coordination.

Third, surveillance for specific complications is critical: atlantoaxial instability (pseudoachondroplasia, SED, Morquio, OI); foramen magnum stenosis (achondroplasia); basilar invagination (OI); spinal stenosis in adulthood (achondroplasia, SED); obstructive sleep apnea (achondroplasia, diastrophic, Morquio); early arthritis (MED, SED).

Fourth, surgical risks in these patients require attention to bone quality (reduced density in OI and MPS, dense and brittle bone in osteopetrosis), anesthetic considerations (difficult airway, cervical luxation risk during positioning, malignant hyperthermia in OI), and overall systemic complexity.

Fifth, disease-modifying therapies — bisphosphonates in OI, vosoritide in achondroplasia, enzyme replacement in MPS, asfotase alfa in hypophosphatasia, burosumab in XLH, palovarotene in FOP — have altered the natural history of many of these conditions.

## Local contributions

Skeletal-dysplasia research is an international academic field to which research groups from many countries have contributed across molecular dissection, radiographic classification, and orthopedic management. The text below follows alphabetical order of country.

Australia. David Sillence in Sydney introduced in 1979 the foundational clinical classification of osteogenesis imperfecta (the Sillence four-type classification), which remains the practical reference for OI severity. Sydney and Adelaide remain active centers in the expanding molecular dissection of OI.

Belgium. Ghent and Leuven maintain active centers for molecular diagnosis of skeletal dysplasias and for the management of OI.

Bulgaria. The Bulgarian orthopedic school in the person of Boycho Boychev (1902–1971) — founder of the Department of Orthopedics and Traumatology at the Higher Medical Institute, Sofia — laid the basis for postwar pediatric orthopedic care for children with dysplasias in the country, with systematic accounts of the management of hip dysplasia and many other pediatric orthopedic problems in his textbook *Surgical Orthopedics*, which remains a principal Bulgarian pediatric-orthopedic reference. Contemporary clinical care for patients with OI, achondroplasia, and MPS is delivered at University Hospital "Aleksandrovska", University Hospital "Lozenets", and UMHAT "Sv. Ivan Rilski" in Sofia, at the Medical University of Plovdiv, and at the National Center for Rare Diseases.

Canada. Francis Glorieux in Montreal developed the standard pamidronate protocol in OI (the "Glorieux protocol") and has contributed substantially to modern OI management. The Shriners Hospital in Montreal remains an international reference for the care of children with OI.

Finland. The Finnish population, with a pronounced founder effect for diastrophic dysplasia (DTDST), has enabled Finnish collectives (Hästbacka, Kaitila, Peltonen and colleagues) to map and clone the SLC26A2 (DTDST) gene in 1994. Helsinki and Turku remain international references for diastrophic dysplasia management.

France. Pierre Maroteaux in Paris is another of the founders of skeletal dysplasia nosology, with significant contributions to the description of many dysplasias (including MPS VI of Maroteaux-Lamy, the dyschondrosteosis of Léri-Weill, and others). The Paris school maintains active clinical and research cohorts.

Germany. Jürgen Spranger in Mainz is one of the leading names in international systematization of skeletal dysplasias and co-author of the reference atlas *Bone Dysplasias* (4th ed., 2019). German groups in Berlin, Heidelberg, and Mainz are active in the nosology committees and in molecular-genetics research. Camurati-Engelmann disease (Engelmann in Vienna, Austria, 1929; Camurati in Bologna, Italy, 1922) illustrates the European integrated approach to describing these rare states.

Italy. Mario Camurati in Bologna described progressive diaphyseal dysplasia (1922), which now carries his and Engelmann's names. The Italian school in Rome and Genoa maintains serious OI clinical cohorts and an active research program in bisphosphonate therapy.

Japan. Gen Nishimura in Tokyo is co-author of the reference Spranger-Brill-Superti-Furga-Nishimura atlas and a leading figure in Asian skeletal-dysplasia nosology. The Japanese school has contributed to the description of many entities and to contemporary management of achondroplasia with vosoritide (Japan being among the first jurisdictions to approve).

South Africa. Peter Beighton in Cape Town, who edited the 5th edition of *Heritable Disorders of Connective Tissue* (1993), contributed to the standardization of joint-hypermobility assessment (the Beighton score, used also in dysplasias with significant hypermobility) and to the description of rare dysplasias in the South African population.

Sweden. Swedish and Finnish collectives have close cooperation in the management of diastrophic dysplasia and OI; Karolinska Institute in Stockholm is a leading European center for molecular diagnosis.

Switzerland. Andrea Superti-Furga in Lausanne is co-author of the principal *Bone Dysplasias* atlas and a leading member of the International Nosology Committee. Lausanne, Zurich, and Basel are active centers for molecular diagnosis and care.

United Kingdom. Christine Hall and Hooshang Taybi (born in Iran, worked in the US, with close cooperation with British colleagues), and the contemporary Great Ormond Street Hospital (GOSH) group in London have contributed substantially to the radiographic classification and clinical management of skeletal dysplasias in children. The British orthopedic school has been particularly influential in OI management and spinal deformity care.

United States. Victor McKusick in Baltimore laid the foundations of medical genetics as a discipline and described many skeletal dysplasias in his landmark *Heritable Disorders of Connective Tissue* (5th edition edited by Peter Beighton). Hooshang Taybi in Los Angeles and Ralph Lachman in California created another principal reference atlas (*Radiology of Syndromes, Metabolic Disorders, and Skeletal Dysplasias*, 5th ed.). Cleveland Clinic, Mayo Clinic, Shriners-Twin Cities, and the International Skeletal Dysplasia Registry in Los Angeles form a network of reference centers.

## Summary and take-home points

The systemic skeletal dysplasias are a heterogeneous group of inherited disorders producing characteristic patterns of skeletal abnormality with associated systemic features. Molecular dissection over the last two decades has expanded the classification to more than 770 entities and enabled precise diagnosis and targeted treatment in a growing number of conditions.

Achondroplasia — the commonest disproportionate-short-stature dysplasia — arises from FGFR3 mutations and produces rhizomelic short stature with foramen magnum stenosis in infancy and progressive spinal stenosis in adulthood; modern treatment with vosoritide is transforming pediatric therapy. Multiple and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasias produce epiphyseal abnormalities with early arthritis, with SED carrying the critical concern of atlantoaxial instability from odontoid hypoplasia. Diastrophic dysplasia — endemic in Finland — presents with characteristic hitchhiker thumb, severe clubfeet, and scoliosis. Osteogenesis imperfecta, the principal disorder of bone fragility, ranges from lethal Type II to mild Type I and has been transformed by modern bisphosphonate therapy and telescoping intramedullary rod techniques. Osteopetrosis produces dense but brittle bone with characteristic radiographic features, requiring hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation in the severe recessive form. The mucopolysaccharidoses, and particularly Morquio syndrome, produce severe skeletal involvement with critical atlantoaxial instability, requiring lifelong management; enzyme replacement therapies have transformed the prognosis of MPS I, II, IVA, VI, and VII. X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets and hypophosphatasia are now managed with targeted biological therapy (burosumab, asfotase alfa).

Across all of these conditions: accurate diagnosis (including molecular); surveillance for specific complications; coordinated multidisciplinary care; and orthopedic intervention tailored to the underlying disorder, bone quality, and systemic risks, determine successful outcomes.
