## Introduction

This chapter addresses two classical focal developmental disorders of physeal growth that are habitually grouped together in board-style curricula and in academic discussion because of their shared pathology: asymmetrical physeal growth producing a progressive angular limb deformity. Blount disease (tibia vara) is a disorder of the medial portion of the proximal tibial physis, producing varus, internal tibial torsion, and procurvatum of the proximal tibia. Madelung deformity is a disorder of the ulnar-volar portion of the distal radial physis, producing a characteristic wrist deformity with volar and ulnar tilt of the distal radial articular surface, dorsal subluxation of the distal ulna, and a pyramidal (inverted-V) arrangement of the carpus with the lunate at the apex. Both conditions require recognition of asymmetric physeal growth as the underlying mechanism, careful imaging of the specific anatomy, and stage- and age-appropriate treatment that combines release of tethering structures, correction of the established deformity, and where appropriate, management of residual growth. The material is synthesized from Paley's *Principles of Deformity Correction*, Sabharwal's *Pediatric Lower Limb Deformities*, Tachdjian's *Procedures in Pediatric Orthopaedics* (Herring), Lovell & Winter's *Pediatric Orthopaedics* (8th edition), Green's *Operative Hand Surgery* (6th edition), and selected primary reports cited below.

## Anatomy and Vascular Supply Relevant to Both Deformities

The proximal tibial physis is one of the most heavily loaded growth centres in the skeleton and shows particular vulnerability of its medial portion. The physis is divided into five horizontal zones — reserve, proliferative, hypertrophic, mineralization, and ossification — with the hypertrophic zone the most mechanically vulnerable. The blood supply of the proximal tibial epiphysis derives from the peripheral perichondrial branches of the genicular recurrent arteries (anterior and posterior), entering peripherally through the perichondrium; the physis itself is avascular and receives nutrients by diffusion from the epiphyseal and metaphyseal sides. The medial perichondrium lies directly beneath the pes anserinus and the medial collateral ligament. With significant varus, the Hueter-Volkmann principle predicts that increased compression on the medial portion reduces growth in the proliferative and hypertrophic zones, while the unloaded lateral portion continues normally — the pathogenetic link of Blount disease.

The distal radial physis is the analogous key growth centre for the upper limb, providing approximately 75% of the longitudinal growth of the radius and approximately 40% of the growth of the upper limb. It has a more complex geometry: the articular surface of the distal radius is inclined volarly (normal 11–15°) and ulnarly (normal 22–23° relative to the long axis of the radius). The blood supply derives from the palmar carpal branch of the ulnar artery, the dorsal carpal branches, and the palmar branch of the palmar metacarpal network. The volar radiocarpal ligamentous complex (radioscaphocapitate, long radiolunate, and short radiolunate) attaches to the volar margin of the distal radius; the abnormal structure described by Vickers and named the "Vickers ligament" originates from the volar-ulnar margin of the radius to the lunate and, in Madelung deformity, acts as a tether on the ulnar-volar portion of the physis.

The distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) is critical for forearm rotation. Normal supination-pronation requires the distal radius to rotate around the relatively fixed distal ulna and an intact triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC). In Madelung deformity, progressive dorsal subluxation of the ulna, increased volar tilt of the radius, and chronic disruption of the TFCC limit supination often more than pronation. This explains the "bayonet wrist" clinical sign and the functional difficulty with activities such as opening a door or shaking hands.

## Blount Disease: Definition and Epidemiology

Blount disease — proximal tibia vara — is a developmental disorder of the medial portion of the proximal tibial physis, producing progressive varus, internal tibial torsion, and procurvatum of the proximal tibia. Walter Putnam Blount of Milwaukee described the entity in 1937 and distinguished it from physiological bowing by its progressive course. Two forms are classically recognized with distinct demographic and clinical features: infantile (onset before 4 years of age; the most common) and adolescent (onset between 10 years and skeletal maturity; less common). Some authors recognize an intermediate juvenile form (4–10 years) with features of both, but most contemporary textbooks (Sabharwal, Herring) maintain the binary classification.

The infantile form is closely associated with early walking (the typical patient is an overweight child who walked before 12 months of age), obesity, African ancestry (substantially higher prevalence in children of African descent in North American and Scandinavian cohorts), and a family history of tibia vara or varus deformity. Bilateral involvement is common in 60–80% of infantile and approximately 50% of adolescent cases, often asymmetrically. The adolescent form is almost invariably associated with obesity (BMI >95th percentile in ≥85% of cases in contemporary series); patients are often taller and prematurely advanced in weight. Additional associated factors described in the literature include vitamin D deficiency, metabolic syndrome, and certain dietary patterns.

## Pathophysiology

The fundamental pathological process is focal retardation of growth in the medial portion of the proximal tibial physis, with involvement of the medial portions of the epiphysis and metaphysis as well, while the lateral portion continues normally. Proposed mechanisms include mechanical (the Hueter-Volkmann principle — increased compression on the medial physis from the varus axis and overweight inhibits growth), biological (intrinsic developmental defects of the medial physis in predisposed individuals), and vascular (compromised perichondrial blood supply from chronic compression). Sabharwal and colleagues synthesize a multifactorial model in which early walking provides premature loading of an immature physis, obesity increases the magnitude of the load, and genetic predisposition (probably affecting chondrocyte sensitivity to mechanical stress and to hypoxia) determines the threshold of response.

Disease progression reflects a positive feedback loop: the varus axis shifts the weight-bearing line medially, increasing medial compression, further inhibiting medial growth, and worsening the varus. Without intervention, severe deformity may develop with associated disruption of the physeal histoarchitecture, fragmentation of the medial epiphysis, and ultimately formation of a bony bridge crossing the physis (physeal bar) — an irreversible localized physeal obstruction. CORA (Center of Rotation of Angulation) analysis as articulated by Paley, applied to the proximal tibia, localizes the apex of the angular deformity near or just below the level of the physis, confirming the physeal pathology as source and guiding the level of osteotomy.

## Langenskiöld Classification and Quantitative Assessment

The Langenskiöld classification (1952), based on the radiographic evolution of the medial proximal tibial epiphysis and metaphysis, recognizes six stages of increasing severity:

Stage I: Medial metaphyseal "beaking" without involvement of the epiphysis.

Stage II: Wedging of the medial epiphysis with a more pronounced metaphyseal "beak."

Stage III: Step-off along the medial border of the epiphysis with fragmentation.

Stage IV: Filling of the medial epiphyseal step-off by the metaphyseal "beak"; the epiphysis appears "folded" against the metaphysis.

Stage V: Cleft in the medial epiphysis with a separate medial fragment.

Stage VI: Bony bridge crossing the medial physis — established physeal bar.

The classification carries prognostic value (stages V and VI rarely improve without surgical intervention; stages I and II may resolve with conservative management), but the inter-observer reliability is only moderate; repeat assessments by different radiologists agree in only approximately 60–70% of cases. The classification should therefore not be the sole criterion for treatment decisions and must be interpreted alongside clinical parameters and quantitative angular measurements.

The metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle of Drennan is the quantitative measurement used to distinguish infantile Blount disease from physiological bowing and to monitor progression. The angle is measured between (1) a line drawn through the most prominent points of the proximal tibial metaphysis and (2) a line perpendicular to the long axis of the tibial shaft. Angles below 11° suggest physiological bowing; angles above 16° suggest Blount disease; intermediate angles (11–16°) are indeterminate and require careful follow-up. Additional parameters used in contemporary cohorts and in the adolescent form include:

- Femoral-tibial angle (FTA): normally 5–7° valgus after 4 years of age; in severe Blount the FTA may be ≥10° varus.
- Mechanical axis (MAD, mechanical axis deviation): deviation of the weight-bearing line from the centre of the knee; medial deviation >15 mm is significant.
- Medial proximal tibial angle (mMPTA): normally 87° (85–90°); in Blount it is decreased (typically <80°).
- Lateral distal femoral angle (mLDFA): helps exclude a concurrent femoral deformity (normally 87–88°).
- CORA analysis per Paley: localizes the apex of the angular deformity — critical for planning an osteotomy with minimal axis of translation.

In the adolescent form, a femoral contribution (distal femoral varus) is often present; planning must include full assessment of the mechanical axis with long-cassette standing radiographs.

## Clinical Features

Infantile Blount disease typically presents in the young child (2–4 years) with bow-legs that have not resolved with normal walking development. The varus is more marked than expected for age and is often combined with internal tibial torsion, producing the characteristic in-toeing gait. Examination shows that the apex of the bowing is at the level of the proximal tibial metaphysis, not diffusely along the limb (in contrast to physiological bowing, which is more diffuse). The lateral thrust during stance phase of gait is characteristic and reflects the progressive medial physeal insufficiency; the thrust may be subtle in early disease and obvious in advanced. When observed in a child who walked before 14 months, with BMI >85th percentile and unilateral or asymmetric varus deformity, it is strongly suggestive of Blount disease and warrants radiographic confirmation.

Adolescent Blount disease presents with progressive genu varum in the adolescent or young adult — typically in a markedly obese patient. A unilateral or asymmetric pattern, medial knee pain with weight-bearing, often accompanying pes planus, and a femoral anteversion contribution are characteristic. With a significant femoral component the full assessment must include evaluation of the distal femoral varus with long-cassette standing radiographs. A distal femoral varus component is often present and must be taken into account in treatment planning — failure to address it leads to undercorrection and recurrence.

## Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of the infantile form is principally with physiological bowing, which is common in small children, typically bilateral and symmetric, peaks at 12–18 months of age, and resolves spontaneously by 2–3 years. The metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle of Drennan, a unilateral or asymmetric pattern, the lack of improvement with growth, the lateral thrust on examination, and the radiographic findings of medial metaphyseal "beaking" distinguish Blount disease.

Other causes of genu varum to be considered include:

- Rickets (vitamin D deficiency, vitamin D-resistant, X-linked hypophosphatemic): the radiographic findings of cupping and fraying of the metaphyses, the biochemical findings (low 25-OH vitamin D, elevated PTH, or hypophosphatemia), the wrists and knees showing "softness" signs, and the characteristic systemic (rather than focal) pattern of involvement establish the diagnosis. Metabolic conditions must be excluded or treated before elective surgery.
- Skeletal dysplasias: Schmid metaphyseal chondrodysplasia produces varus with characteristic metaphyseal findings mimicking rickets but with normal biochemistry; spondyloepimetaphyseal and other dysplasias should be considered with short stature or a family history. SHOX-related dysplasias may include both mesomelic short stature and tibia vara as well as Madelung.
- Focal fibrocartilaginous dysplasia: a localized fibrocartilaginous lesion in the medial proximal tibial metaphysis producing localized varus; typically presents earlier (8–24 months), often resolves spontaneously, and has a characteristic localized metaphyseal indentation.
- Post-traumatic deformity: after prior physeal injury, osteomyelitis, or tumour procedure; the history and focal location of the pathology guide the diagnosis.
- Generalized bone dysplasias (achondroplasia, pseudo-achondroplasia): systemic findings — short stature, rhizomelic or mesomelic reduction, frontal bossing, trident hand — guide the diagnosis.

## Treatment of Blount Disease

Treatment is staged according to the Langenskiöld stage, patient age, severity of quantitative deformity, and the presence of a femoral component.

### Conservative Treatment (Bracing)

Stages I–II in a child under 3 years of age: A knee-ankle-foot orthosis (KAFO) is the standard initial treatment. The brace is laterally hinged with medial support and must be worn continuously (>23 hours per day) for at least one year; compliance is challenging. Reported success rates are 50–80% in early stages. Failure of bracing or progression to stage III or higher is an indication for surgery. Sabharwal and colleagues caution against extending bracing indications to children over 3 years of age or to stages III–VI, in which success is unlikely.

### Surgical Treatment

Proximal tibial osteotomy (stages III–IV or failure of bracing): The osteotomy is performed at a level just below the tibial tuberosity. Options include:

- *Closing wedge*: removal of a lateral wedge with full correction in a single stage; stable, with low vascular risk, but requires bone resection and often shortening.
- *Opening wedge*: creation of a medial gap and filling with bone graft; preserves length but with increased risk of vascular or nerve injury with acute correction.
- *Dome*: a semicircular osteotomy with rotation; versatile and flexible for multiplanar correction.
- *Gradual correction with external fixation* (Ilizarov or Taylor Spatial Frame): particularly suited to severe deformities, multi-apical deformities, older age, the need for lengthening, or residual deformities.

The principle, emphasized by Paley in *Principles of Deformity Correction*, is mild overcorrection of varus to 5–10° valgus, because the residual tendency toward varus from continuing physeal asymmetry may return the bone to neutral over time. Derotation to address the internal tibial torsion and concurrent fibular osteotomy to avoid limiting correction are routinely included. The peroneal nerve is at risk with the fibular osteotomy and with acute correction and must be protected — a proximal fibular osteotomy or prophylactic fasciotomy of the lateral compartment is recommended with acute large corrections.

Hemiepiphysiodesis / "guided growth" with a lateral tension-band plate: Stevens (Salt Lake City) popularized the tension-band plate (Stevens 8-plate) with screw fixation on either side of the physis as a minimally invasive, reversible alternative to osteotomy. The plate is placed along the lateral proximal tibial physis and slows the lateral growth, allowing the medial side to "catch up." Advantages are short post-operative recovery, minimal invasiveness, and reversibility (the plate is removed when correction is achieved). Limitations are: it requires sufficient residual growth (usually at least 2 years until physeal closure), does not work in established medial physeal bar (stages V–VI), and does not correct a pre-existing rotational deformity. With combined femoral deformity, a distal femoral lateral hemiepiphysiodesis may be added. The "guided growth" approach is particularly attractive in the older child and in the adolescent form with adequate residual growth.

Physeal bar resection (stages V–VI): With established physeal bar, varus worsens even after corrective osteotomy, because continuing asymmetric growth returns the deformity. Bar resection (per Langenskiöld, later refined by Peterson and others) with interposition of fat, methyl methacrylate, or silicone may restore continued growth if the bar occupies <50% of the physeal surface. Bar resection is combined with concurrent or delayed osteotomy for correction of the established deformity. With larger bars or repeated failure, completion of physeal closure with contralateral epiphysiodesis (to avoid limb-length discrepancy) and osteotomy is the alternative.

### Adolescent Blount Disease

Treatment is primarily surgical, since bracing is rarely effective at this age. Options include:

- Lateral hemiepiphysiodesis with adequate residual growth (minimum 18–24 months to physeal closure). The Stevens tension-band plate is the standard; with a significant femoral component a distal femoral lateral hemiepiphysiodesis may be added. Correction is monitored by serial mechanical axis assessment, and when neutral or mild valgus is reached the plate is removed to avoid overcorrection.
- Proximal tibial osteotomy (acute correction with internal fixation in moderate deformities, or gradual correction with external fixation — Taylor Spatial Frame or Ilizarov — in severe or multiplanar).
- Combined procedures with distal femoral correction with a significant femoral component: full correction of the mechanical axis requires concurrent addressing of both components.
- With severe obesity — a multidisciplinary approach with nutritionist, psychologist, and in some cases bariatric assessment before elective surgery to optimize outcomes and reduce the risk of recurrence.

## Complications and Long-Term Outcomes

The complications of Blount disease and its treatment include:

- Incomplete correction or recurrence of varus: relatively common, particularly with conservative treatment of advanced disease, with inadequate overcorrection at osteotomy, or with failure to address a femoral component.
- Growth arrest of the proximal tibial physis: may produce limb-length discrepancy (LLD) requiring contralateral epiphysiodesis or lengthening.
- Intra-articular deformity and early degenerative arthritis of the medial compartment of the knee: the principal long-term problem; early arthritis in adults often necessitates total knee arthroplasty by the fourth or fifth decade, with a higher revision rate than primary arthritis.
- Limb-length discrepancy: from unilateral involvement or from post-operative physeal arrest.
- Compartment syndrome after osteotomy: particularly with acute corrections; the standard is prophylactic fasciotomy of the lateral compartment or pre-emptive assessment of compartment pressures.
- Peroneal nerve injury: the nerve is at risk with proximal tibial osteotomy and must be protected, particularly with concurrent fibular osteotomy.
- Valgus overcorrection: with overly aggressive correction or with concurrent lateral hemiepiphysiodesis continuing after correction is achieved.
- External fixation-related complications: pin-site infections, delayed consolidation, joint stiffness.

The long-term outcomes of infantile disease diagnosed and treated at an early stage are generally good. With delayed diagnosis, advanced disease, or established physeal bar, outcomes are less reliable, with a higher rate of recurrence, early osteoarthritis, and need for salvage procedures.

## Madelung Deformity: Definition and Epidemiology

Madelung deformity is a developmental disorder of the distal radial physis, characterized by premature partial closure of its ulnar-volar portion, producing a progressively shortened radius, increased volar and ulnar tilt of the distal radial articular surface, dorsal subluxation of the distal ulna, and a pyramidal (inverted-V) arrangement of the carpal bones with the lunate at the apex. Otto Madelung of Bonn described the deformity in 1878 in *Über die Spontanluxation der Hand nach vorne*, converting observations by earlier authors (Dupuytren, Malgaigne) into a defined clinical entity.

The condition is approximately four times more common in women. Presentation is typically in late childhood or early adolescence (8–14 years) with progressive deformity, pain, and cosmetic concerns, though mild cases may be discovered incidentally or only in adulthood. Bilateral involvement is common (50–70%), though often asymmetric. The condition may be sporadic ("true" Madelung), familial (often autosomal dominant), or a manifestation of Léri-Weill dyschondrosteosis (LWD) — an autosomal dominant syndrome with mesomelic short stature caused by haploinsufficiency of the SHOX gene in the pseudoautosomal region PAR1 of Xp22/Yp11. Homozygous SHOX mutation produces the severe Langer mesomelic dysplasia. "Pseudo-Madelung deformity" may also result from trauma to the distal radial physis (post-traumatic Madelung-like deformity), infection, tumour, or as part of multiple hereditary exostoses.

## Pathophysiology

The underlying pathology is premature partial closure of the ulnar-volar quadrant of the distal radial physis, producing an asymmetric pattern of growth in which the radial-dorsal portion of the physis continues to grow normally while the ulnar-volar portion does not. The resultant deformity is a complex 3D problem and cannot be reduced solely to an angular issue — there is a component of shortening, of angular deformity, and of rotation.

The Vickers ligament — an abnormal thickened volar radiocarpal ligament extending from the volar-ulnar margin of the distal radius to the lunate — is a characteristic finding, described by Donald Vickers and Nielsen in 1992. Their original hypothesis is that the ligament is the primary tethering structure that creates focal compression on the ulnar-volar portion of the physis and thus causes its premature closure; the alternative hypothesis regards the ligament as a secondary consequence of an underlying physeal defect caused by SHOX haploinsufficiency. SHOX (Short stature HOmeobOX-containing gene) is a critical regulator of chondrocyte proliferation in the proliferative and hypertrophic zones and is particularly active in the distal radial and proximal tibial physes — an explanation for the associated short stature and mesomelic reduction in LWD. Whatever the initial generator, the operation of release of the Vickers ligament combined with physiolysis produces clinical improvement, particularly in early forms, which supports at least a partial primary pathogenetic role.

The consequence of asymmetric growth is the constellation of findings:

- Relative shortening of the radius compared with the ulna (positive ulnar variance).
- Increased volar and ulnar tilt of the distal radial articular surface.
- Dorsal subluxation of the ulnar head ("bayonet wrist").
- Pyramidal "wedging" of the carpus with the lunate at the apex.
- Widening and potential diastasis of the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ).
- Triangular shape of the distal radial epiphysis.
- Restriction of forearm rotation, especially supination, and of wrist dorsiflexion.

The Cook-Crisco (Vanderbilt) classification quantitatively grades severity based on ulnar tilt (normal <30°), volar tilt (normal 11–15° volar), lunate-radius distance, and ulnar variance.

## Clinical Features

Presentation is typically with progressive wrist deformity in late childhood or adolescence, pain (particularly with loading), restricted supination, weakness of grip, and cosmetic concerns. Examination reveals the characteristic deformity with an apparent ulnar prominence (from the dorsally subluxed ulnar head — "bayonet wrist"), restricted supination, and restricted wrist motion. Pain is often ulnar-sided, exacerbated by pronation-supination, and may be associated with TFCC pathology.

The deformity is sometimes confused with a healed distal radius fracture or with rheumatoid arthritis, both of which can produce similar superficial appearances. In sporadic or familial cases with late detection (an adult patient with mild deformity and no significant functional complaints), the approach is observation. In syndromic Léri-Weill dyschondrosteosis, the examination should include stature, body proportions, assessment of the lower limbs (mesomelic shortening, possible tibia vara), and a spinal examination.

## Imaging

Standard imaging is bilateral PA and lateral wrist radiographs, taken with the forearm neutral and the elbow flexed to 90°. The classical findings include:

- Increased ulnar tilt (increased slope of the distal radial articular surface): typically >30°.
- Increased volar tilt of the distal radius: typically >25° (normal 11–15°).
- Dorsal subluxation of the distal ulna: visible in the lateral view.
- Pyramidal carpus: the carpal bones are arranged in an inverted-V configuration with the lunate at the apex.
- Triangular distal radial epiphysis: a characteristic shape from the asymmetric ossification.
- Positive ulnar variance: from the relative shortening of the radius.
- Increased radius-ulna distance at the DRUJ with possible diastasis.
- Localized volar-ulnar cyst-like change of the radius (occasionally).

MRI is useful for demonstrating the Vickers ligament and assessing the cartilaginous components of the deformity, particularly in younger patients; ultrasonography may visualize the ligament in experienced hands. CT with 3D reconstruction is valuable in complex cases for surgical planning — particularly for multiplanar osteotomy and assessment of the DRUJ.

When a syndromic form is suspected, SHOX gene testing is recommended where available, along with multidisciplinary evaluation (geneticist, endocrinologist for assessment of short stature, orthopaedist for assessment of tibial deformity in LWD).

## Treatment of Madelung Deformity

Treatment is tailored to severity, age, skeletal maturity, and the presence of pain or functional complaints.

### Observation

Asymptomatic mild deformity in the skeletally mature patient or mild cases without functional complaints: periodic clinical and radiographic follow-up suffices. Pain-directed physiotherapy, ergonomic adjustment, and activity modification may defer the surgical decision.

### Surgical Treatment in the Skeletally Immature Patient

In symptomatic deformity in the skeletally immature patient with adequate residual growth (usually >2 years to physeal closure):

- Vickers ligament release combined with Langenskiöld-Vickers physiolysis: excision of the thickened ligament and resection of the bar between the physis and metaphysis with interposition (fat) is the procedure of choice in patients with mild-to-moderate disease and significant residual growth. Described by Vickers and Nielsen (1992), and subsequently refined by Murphy, Wolfe, Carter, and others. The principle is release of the tether from the ligament and addressing the asymmetric physis, permitting remodelling of the deformity with continued growth.
- Dome osteotomy of the distal radius: in moderately advanced deformity, may be combined with Vickers ligament release for correction of the existing tilt and restoration of more normal articular orientation.
- With a significant femoral or tibial component in the context of LWD — concurrent or staged correction of the lower limbs.

### Surgical Treatment Near or At Skeletal Maturity

Symptomatic deformity at or near skeletal maturity: corrective osteotomy of the distal radius (with or without ulnar shortening) is the standard surgical treatment.

- Radial osteotomy: typically a closing-wedge dorsoradial or opening-wedge from the volar ulnar side, designed to correct the volar and ulnar tilt and restore more normal orientation of the distal radial articular surface. Opening-wedge osteotomies require a bone graft. Multiplanar dome osteotomy allows simultaneous correction of tilt and rotation.
- Ulnar shortening osteotomy: addresses the relative ulnar overgrowth contributing to ulnar-sided wrist pain and to TFCC pathology. May be performed concurrently with radial correction or as a stand-alone procedure in mild cases with predominantly ulnar-sided pain.
- Distal ulnar resection (Darrach procedure) or hemiresection arthroplasty (Bowers procedure): reserved for cases with severe DRUJ derangement. The Darrach procedure removes the distal ulna (approximately 2–3 cm), which eases motion but potentially destabilizes the ulnar stiloid and carries a risk of ulnar carpal translocation over time.
- Sauve-Kapandji procedure: arthrodesis of the DRUJ with formation of a proximal pseudarthrosis of the ulnar diaphyseal segment. It preserves an existing "ulnar head," restoring forearm rotation, and has been used successfully in selected cases with severe DRUJ derangement.

In severe multiplanar deformities, Buck-Gramcko (Hamburg) described combined dome osteotomies and radial lengthening with external fixation in patients with significant shortening — an approach particularly applicable in the LWD context with mesomelic deficit. Gradual correction with external fixation (mini-Ilizarov or TSF) also has application in complex scenarios with DRUJ incongruity and the need for lengthening.

## Outcomes

Outcomes of surgical correction of Madelung deformity are generally good, with substantial improvement of pain, function, and cosmesis in most patients. Long-term issues include:

- Progressive DRUJ degeneration: may necessitate later salvage procedures (Darrach, Sauve-Kapandji, DRUJ implant arthroplasty).
- Recurrence of the deformity: rare when the underlying physeal arrest has been addressed; more common when only an osteotomy is performed without physiolysis in the immature patient.
- Carpal arthritis: from persistent abnormal joint biomechanics.
- Loss of correction: with technically suboptimal osteotomy or early loading.
- Graft-related complications: with opening-wedge procedures — donor site morbidity, delayed consolidation.
- External fixation-related complications: in complex cases with lengthening.

## Associated Conditions

### Léri-Weill Dyschondrosteosis (LWD)

LWD is an autosomal dominant disease, described by André Léri and Jean Weill in 1929 (Paris) and caused by haploinsufficiency of the SHOX gene. The disease produces mesomelic dwarfism (short forearms and lower legs with relatively preserved length of the upper arms and thighs), Madelung deformity, and mild generalized short stature. Women are more severely affected than men because of X-inactivation and the location of SHOX in the pseudoautosomal region. Orthopedic care addresses the Madelung deformity by the principles described above, and the associated mesomelic shortening of the lower limbs, which may necessitate limb lengthening procedures in selected cases. Growth hormone in pre-pubertal age may affect final stature in specialized centres.

### Langer Mesomelic Dysplasia

Described by Léonard O. Langer (Minnesota, 1967), Langer mesomelic dysplasia is the homozygous form of SHOX mutation, producing severe mesomelic short stature with hypoplastic ulnae and fibulae, severe Madelung deformity, and associated features. The condition is rare and requires individualized multidisciplinary management with orthopaedist, geneticist, endocrinologist, and physiotherapist.

### Multiple Hereditary Exostoses (MHE)

MHE may produce a Madelung-like wrist deformity from involvement of the distal radius and ulna by exostoses, with disruption of growth and characteristic shortening and bowing of the forearm (HMO-induced Madelung-like). Treatment combines excision of the exostoses with corrective osteotomies and, in severe cases, ulnar lengthening (Masada classification of the formulated types).

### Turner Syndrome

The 45,X genotype includes monosomy for SHOX, and in approximately 5–10% of cases is associated with a Madelung-like deformity, usually milder than LWD because of the residual SHOX from the second X (when mosaicism is present).

## Local Contributions by Country

This section presents selected historical and contemporary contributions in alphabetical order by country, focusing on clearly attributable named features or techniques that are established in the international literature.

### Australia

Donald Vickers (Sydney) and Nielsen described in 1992 the thickened volar radiolunate ligament now called the "Vickers ligament" — a structure established as a central pathogenetic and surgical target in the management of Madelung deformity. Their operative technique (ligament release plus physiolysis) remains a reference procedure for the skeletally immature patient.

### Bulgaria

Boycho Boychev (1902–1971) and his *Surgical Orthopaedics* present a systematic description of varus deformities of the knee in childhood in the Bulgarian literature, with a practical description of bracing and proximal tibial osteotomy in the conditions of post-war orthopaedic practice. The contemporary Bulgarian school applies internationally accepted algorithms (Sabharwal/Paley).

### France

André Léri (Paris) and Jean Weill in 1929 described the autosomal dominant dyschondrosteosis with mesomelic dwarfism and Madelung deformity — a syndrome later molecularly clarified as SHOX haploinsufficiency. The French literature continues to expand understanding of the syndromic form and its orthopaedic management.

### Germany

Otto Madelung (Bonn, 1878) described the spontaneous anterior dislocation of the hand, thus establishing the clinical entity bearing his name, in *Verhandlungen der Deutschen Gesellschaft für Chirurgie*. Dieter Buck-Gramcko (Hamburg) subsequently contributed substantially to the surgical management of complex Madelung deformities, particularly in the LWD context, describing combined dome osteotomies and techniques for radial lengthening. Karlsruhe/Augsburg groups (Hülsemann et al.) systematized the use of physiolysis in young patients.

### Italy

The Italian school of paediatric orthopaedics (Rizzoli Institute, Bologna) has contributed to the use of gradual correction with external fixation (Ilizarov method, applied by Italian surgeons from the 1980s) in severe tibial deformities, including adolescent Blount disease.

### Russia/USSR

Gavriil A. Ilizarov (Kurgan) with his development of circular external fixation and the principles of distraction osteogenesis in the 1950s–1960s, provided a universal platform for the gradual correction of complex angular deformities of the limbs. The principles are applied today in severe and multiplanar deformities of Blount disease, particularly in adolescent and adult forms with the need for concurrent lengthening.

### Scandinavia (Finland and Sweden)

Anders Langenskiöld (Helsinki) in 1952 published the radiographic classification of Blount disease in six stages — a foundational work that remains a reference for staging and for treatment decisions. The physeal bar resection ("Langenskiöld procedure") was originally described by him.

### United Kingdom

The British school of paediatric orthopaedics (Robert Jones, Naughton Dunn, John Charnley in the broader context) developed the principles of elective tibial osteotomy and laid the foundation for contemporary understanding of varus deformities; the specific contribution to Madelung is through the work of British hand surgeons at Stanmore and Sheffield on physiolysis.

### United States

- Walter Putnam Blount (Milwaukee) in 1937 described the clinical entity bearing his name in the *Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery*, and distinguished the infantile from the adolescent form.
- J. Christopher Drennan (Albuquerque) described the metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle as a quantitative criterion for distinguishing physiological bowing.
- Peter M. Stevens (Salt Lake City) in 2007 published the tension-band plate for guided growth — the contemporary standard for minimally invasive correction in patients with adequate residual growth.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

Blount disease and Madelung deformity, although affecting different parts of the skeleton (proximal tibia and distal radius respectively), share the unifying theme of asymmetric physeal growth producing progressive angular deformity, and are joined in academic programmes precisely because of the shared pathogenetic principles.

Blount disease is a disorder of the medial proximal tibial physis with progression by the six stages of Langenskiöld. The classic associations (obesity, early walking, African ancestry) characterize the typical infantile form; the adolescent form is dominated by obesity and often includes a femoral component. Treatment is staged: bracing in early disease in the young child; proximal tibial osteotomy or hemiepiphysiodesis in established disease; physeal bar resection with osteotomy in severe disease with physeal bridge; and for the adolescent form — guided growth with tension-band plates or gradual correction with external fixation (TSF, Ilizarov) in complex cases. Careful planning with CORA analysis, mild overcorrection to valgus, and concurrent addressing of the rotational and femoral component are key principles.

Madelung deformity is a disorder of the ulnar-volar distal radial physis with a characteristic 3D deformity (volar and ulnar tilt, dorsal ulnar subluxation, pyramidal carpus). SHOX haploinsufficiency in Léri-Weill dyschondrosteosis is the molecular basis of many syndromic cases; the Vickers ligament is a central pathogenetic and surgical target. Treatment combines Vickers ligament release and physiolysis in the immature patient and corrective osteotomy (with or without ulnar shortening) in the mature, with attention to the DRUJ and associated ulnar variance.

The unifying principles of recognition of asymmetric physeal growth, careful imaging of the specific anatomy, multidisciplinary evaluation in syndromic forms (especially LWD with SHOX genetic confirmation), and treatment tailored to age, severity, and underlying disease, ensure successful outcomes in both disorders.
