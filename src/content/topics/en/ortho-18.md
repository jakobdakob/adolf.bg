## Introduction

Coxa vara is a deformity of the proximal femur in which the neck–shaft angle (NSA) is reduced below the age-appropriate norm. The term was introduced in the late nineteenth century by German orthopaedists and has since come to designate a clinically, etiologically, and pathologically heterogeneous group of conditions — from the rare isolated developmental form with its characteristic inferomedial defect of the femoral neck to the systemic manifestations seen in skeletal dysplasias, fibrous dysplasia, osteogenesis imperfecta, and the acquired variants that follow infection, trauma, or Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease. What unifies these conditions is not a common etiology but a common biomechanical consequence: the reduced NSA shifts the line of body weight medially relative to the center of the femoral head, increases load on the cortical surface of the femoral neck, converts the normally compressive loading of the proximal physis into shear, and ultimately disrupts the abductor mechanics of the hip.

The chapter synthesizes content from six anchor references: Friedrich Pauwels — *Biomechanics of the Normal and Diseased Hip* (Springer, 1976; German originals 1935 and 1973), which sets out the conceptual framework for varus and valgus deformities of the proximal femur; Dietrich Tönnis — *Congenital Dysplasia and Dislocation of the Hip in Children and Adults* (Springer, 1987), in which the modern principles of varus and valgus osteotomy of the proximal femur are formulated; John A. Herring — *Tachdjian's Pediatric Orthopaedics* (Elsevier, latest edition), still the standard for the description of developmental coxa vara, the "Fairbank triangle," and the Hilgenreiner–epiphyseal angle; Dror Paley — *Principles of Deformity Correction* (Springer, 2002), which provides the modern geometric framework for osteotomies of coxa vara within the CORA analysis; Sanjeev Sabharwal — *Pediatric Lower Limb Deformities* (Springer, 2016), with practical algorithms and a comparison of techniques; and the relevant chapters of *Lovell and Winter's Pediatric Orthopaedics* (Wolters Kluwer, 8th edition, 2020). For the Pauwels biomechanics section, the corresponding chapters of Berry & Lieberman — *Surgery of the Hip* — are also drawn upon.

The clinical importance of recognizing coxa vara is threefold: it is a common cause of painless limp with Trendelenburg sign in early childhood and must be distinguished from congenital hip dislocation; untreated or incompletely corrected coxa vara predictably leads to progressive deformity, limb-length discrepancy, and accelerated degenerative arthritis of the hip in adulthood; and, not least, it represents one of the original clinical problems in which biomechanical analysis of the proximal femur allows the choice of surgical correction to be grounded not just empirically but in quantitative calculation.

## Definitions and Normal Anatomy of the Proximal Femur

The neck–shaft angle (NSA) is the angle between the long axis of the femoral diaphysis and the long axis of the femoral neck, measured in the frontal plane on an AP radiograph of the hip in neutral rotation. At birth the normal NSA is about 150°, falling to about 145° in early childhood, to 135° by age 5–6 years, and reaching 125–135° in adulthood. The decrease of NSA with growth is physiological and reflects the adaptation of the proximal femur to the loading of upright gait; accelerated or excessive reduction is the criterion for coxa vara.

The age-specific threshold values for coxa vara are: in the adult < 120°; in late adolescence < 120°; in early adolescence < 125°; in middle childhood (5–10 years) < 125°; in early childhood (under 5 years) < 130°. These thresholds, formalized in the work of Tönnis and Tachdjian, are more useful than any fixed single number because they take account of the physiological decline of NSA through development. It is important to remember that NSA on AP radiograph is the true NSA only when the femoral neck is in a purely frontal plane; with increased anteversion (as in Perthes disease or after SCFE), the rotational component distorts the measured NSA. Precise measurement requires either careful standardized limb positioning or CT with reformats along the plane of the neck.

Femoral neck anteversion — the angle between the plane of the femoral neck and the frontal plane — is the other major geometric parameter of the proximal femur and is often abnormal in conjunction with coxa vara. Normal anteversion is about 30–40° at birth, falling to 10–15° in adulthood. Loss of the normal decline of anteversion through childhood (persistent increased anteversion) is common in coxa vara of various etiologies and contributes to abductor dysfunction, restricted external rotation, and increased internal foot progression in gait.

The articulo-trochanteric distance (ATD) — the distance from the tip of the greater trochanter to the top of the femoral head, measured along a line perpendicular to the long axis of the femoral shaft — is a third key geometric measurement. The normal ATD is about 5 mm at birth, 15–25 mm in childhood, and 25–35 mm in adulthood. A reduced or negative ATD means that the greater trochanter has overgrown the femoral head, which occurs both in true trochanteric overgrowth (after premature closure of the proximal physis) and in severe varus deformity without trochanteric overgrowth, and is a quantitative measure of abductor dysfunction.

The blood supply of the proximal femur after fusion of the physeal rings is predominantly through the medial femoral circumflex artery via its posterior (superior) and inferior retinacular branches running along the femoral neck. Disruption of these vessels — by dislocation, femoral neck fracture, or aggressive surgery in the intertrochanteric region — is the critical anatomical consideration for any osteotomy of the proximal femur and the reason that a lateral approach with meticulous preservation of the capsular attachments to the neck is the standard for corrective osteotomy techniques in childhood.

## Classification

Coxa vara can be classified along two independent axes — etiologically and pathogenetically/biomechanically — and modern practice uses both at once.

### Etiological classification (by origin of the deformity)

Developmental (infantile, congenital) coxa vara: a primary, more often unilateral, rare (1 in 25,000 live births) disorder of the proximal femoral physis in which a triangular fragment of bone separates from the inferomedial corner of the neck along an oblique line. Presentation is in early childhood (2–6 years) with painless limp. This is the "classical" coxa vara of the orthopaedic literature.

Dysplastic coxa vara: a manifestation of a skeletal dysplasia — cleidocranial dysplasia, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia (SED congenita and SED tarda), spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, metaphyseal chondrodysplasia (Schmid type and Jansen type), multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, diaphyseal aclasis, the mucopolysaccharidoses, campomelic dysplasia, and others.

Metabolic coxa vara: in severe fluorosis, in vitamin D–resistant rickets (hypophosphatemic), in vitamin D–dependent rickets (pseudo-deficiency), and in hyperphosphatasia.

Coxa vara in fibrous dysplasia: monostotic and polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, McCune–Albright syndrome, and Mazabraud syndrome — the pathognomonic "shepherd's crook" of the proximal femur.

Coxa vara in osteogenesis imperfecta (particularly types III and IV): the result of multiple stress fractures and plastic deformation of the weakened bone.

Coxa vara in multiple hereditary exostoses: involvement of the proximal femoral metaphysis by osteochondromas reduces NSA.

Post-infectious coxa vara: following neonatal or early infantile septic arthritis of the hip with destruction of the femoral head and proximal physis ("Tom Smith arthritis").

Post-traumatic coxa vara: after femoral neck fracture with malunion in varus, after traumatic epiphysiolysis with premature closure of the physis, or after avascular necrosis with secondary deformity.

Coxa vara as a sequela of Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease: typically coxa magna and coxa breva with relative trochanteric overgrowth; true varus is uncommon but possible.

Coxa vara following SCFE: typically retroversion rather than true varus, but in cases of severe slip with a real NSA deficit, corrective valgus osteotomy is an option.

Idiopathic coxa vara in adolescence: a rare entity; the differential diagnosis includes occult SCFE and metabolic bone disease.

### Pathogenetic/biomechanical classification

The Pauwels analysis of the 1930s and 1950s describes coxa vara in terms of the orientation of the proximal physis relative to the resultant joint reaction force at the hip (R). In a normal hip, the angle between R and the physis is such that the loading across the physis is predominantly compressive — the compressive component of R outweighs the shear component. As the angle between R and the physis increases (typically with varus deformity of the neck), the shear component rises and the compressive component falls, until at a critical physeal angle of around 35–40° above the horizontal the shear loading begins to predominate. Because physeal bone formation is stimulated by compression and inhibited by shear, the steeply inclined physis is a self-sustaining vice: varus increases shear, shear disturbs physeal growth, disturbed growth deepens varus. This is the conceptual explanation for why even moderate coxa vara tends to progress, and for why bringing the physis into a more horizontal orientation — exactly what valgus osteotomy achieves — restores the normal pattern of loading.

This pathogenetic framework also explains the one numerical criterion that has proven prognostic value in developmental coxa vara: the Hilgenreiner–epiphyseal angle (HEA). HEA essentially measures the residual physeal tilt relative to horizontal (Hilgenreiner's line) and is therefore a direct surrogate for the shear/compression ratio of loading across the physis. HEA below 45° is compatible with a stable or improving state; HEA above 60° practically always progresses; the 45–60° range is a "watch zone" with an indication for correction in the face of documented progression.

## Pathology of Developmental Coxa Vara

Developmental coxa vara — also called infantile coxa vara and, in older literature, "idiopathic coxa vara of childhood" — is a primary disorder of the inferomedial corner of the proximal femoral metaphysis. The condition is rare (approximately 1 in 25,000 live births), affects both sexes equally, and is bilateral in 30–50% of cases. A familial pattern is established in a small minority of cases and is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance; the exceptions — association with cleidocranial dysplasia, spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, and the Schmid type of metaphyseal chondrodysplasia — are described as "syndromic developmental coxa vara" and should prompt a systemic workup.

Pathologically the condition is characterized by a triangular fragment of bone that separates from the inferomedial corner of the femoral neck along an oblique line extending from the inferomedial aspect of the metaphysis downward and laterally toward the calcar. This fragment — first described by the British orthopaedist Sir Harold Fairbank in 1928 and since named the "Fairbank triangle" — represents a preserved island of metaphyseal bone, separated from the surrounding regions by a defect of physeal expansion and abnormal bone architecture. Tönnis, in his classic 1987 monograph, described a "wandering" of the fragment upward and laterally with growth that progressively worsens the deformity: as the child grows, the bone defect widens, the neck shortens and tapers, NSA falls, and the physis becomes more vertical. In severe cases of untreated progressive disease, the physis may undergo complete functional failure with the development of "femoral neck pseudarthrosis" — a false joint at the site of the physeal defect that often requires aggressive surgical reconstruction.

Histologically the metaphyseal bone in the defect zone shows disordered endochondral ossification with islands of incompletely ossified cartilage, fibrous tissue, widened lacunae, and avascular zones — a pattern that distantly recalls that of the physeal disorders of rickets and of metaphyseal chondrodysplasia. This histological appearance and the association with skeletal dysplasias have led to the hypothesis that developmental coxa vara is an expression of a primary disorder of endochondral ossification in the proximal femoral metaphysis, with or without an associated generalized skeletal disorder.

The etiology of isolated developmental coxa vara remains unknown. Hypotheses include: (a) a primary disorder of endochondral ossification — as above; (b) a vascular disturbance of the inferomedial metaphysis in the setting of critical blood supply from the inferior retinacular vessels; (c) the mechanical hypothesis of Carroll, in which abnormal load distribution in early childhood sets up the vicious cycle of shear physeal loading and progressive deformity; and (d) a genetic hypothesis supported by the familial cases and the associations with skeletal dysplasias. The condition is most likely multifactorial, with a primary genetic or vascular predisposition and a secondary mechanical amplification.

## Clinical Features

Developmental coxa vara typically presents between 2 and 6 years of age with a painless limp. Parents often notice that the child "limps" or "waddles" without complaint of pain and that the movements of the affected hip are restricted, especially abduction and internal rotation. With bilateral involvement the gait is "waddling" without obvious limb-length discrepancy; with unilateral involvement there is a Trendelenburg gait (drop of the pelvis toward the sound side during single-leg stance on the affected side), compensatory trunk lean toward the affected side (combined Trendelenburg–Duchenne gait), and visible limb-length discrepancy. The child has no systemic symptoms and extra-skeletal growth is normal; in syndromic developmental coxa vara, by contrast, the characteristic systemic features of the underlying condition are present.

Examination reveals reduced abduction (typically 30° or less), reduced internal rotation, and a tendency of the limb to assume a position of external rotation and adduction at rest. The Galeazzi test — asymmetry of the level of the knees with the child supine and the hips and knees flexed — is positive in unilateral disease and reflects shortening of the proximal femoral segment. The Trendelenburg test (pelvic drop on the contralateral side with single-leg stance on the affected side) is positive and quantitatively reflects abductor weakness. Hip abductor strength is reduced but not absent — in contrast to certain neuromuscular conditions in which the abductors are truly paralyzed.

Pain is typically absent in early childhood but may emerge in late childhood and adolescence as a result of the progressive deformity, joint dysfunction, or early degenerative changes. Pain is localized to the groin or the anterolateral hip and often refers down the inner aspect of the thigh to the medial knee — the classic pattern of referred hip pain. The emergence of pain in childhood is a sign of progressive deformity and is often an additional argument for surgical intervention.

Limb-length discrepancy in unilateral developmental coxa vara is usual and varies from 1 to 3 cm, depending on the severity of the deformity and the age of the child. With severe progressive coxa vara the shortening may reach 4–5 cm by skeletal maturity and may require a combined approach with valgus osteotomy plus contralateral epiphysiodesis or, in selected cases, lengthening of the affected limb.

## Imaging

An AP radiograph of the pelvis centered over the pubic symphysis with the hips in neutral rotation is the first and usually sufficient study for the diagnosis. The characteristic radiographic findings are:

* Reduced neck–shaft angle (NSA): measured between the long axis of the diaphysis and the long axis of the neck; typically < 110° in established childhood developmental coxa vara, compared with the normal 140–150° for the age.
* Fairbank triangle: a triangular bone island in the inferomedial corner of the metaphysis, separated from the rest of the bone by an irregular widened strip of physeal disorganization.
* Vertical physeal defect: a widened, irregular, partly radiolucent strip oriented vertically or near-vertically in the medial half of the physis, representing the region of disordered endochondral ossification. In severe cases the defect may span the entire width of the neck and herald pseudarthrosis.
* Shortened, wide femoral neck: with relative overgrowth of the greater trochanter above the level of the femoral head.
* Relatively low ATD (articulo-trochanteric distance): an indirect measure of abductor dysfunction.
* In later stages: secondary acetabular changes with increased acetabular index, lateralization of the femoral head, and sometimes early degenerative changes.

### The Hilgenreiner–Epiphyseal Angle (HEA)

The Hilgenreiner–epiphyseal angle, introduced by Weinstein and colleagues in 1984 and validated in numerous subsequent series, is the principal quantitative measurement in developmental coxa vara and the only one with proven prognostic value. HEA is measured between Hilgenreiner's line (a horizontal line drawn through the triradiate cartilages of both acetabula) and a line along the proximal femoral physis. The normal HEA in a child without coxa vara is below 25°. The values are interpreted as follows:

* HEA < 45°: stable or improving state; observation with serial radiographs every 6–12 months is indicated.
* HEA 45–60°: intermediate zone; the decision for surgical correction is taken according to documented progression, clinical symptoms, and the age of the child.
* HEA > 60°: practically always progresses; surgical correction is indicated in the near term, unless the child is too young for safe intervention.

HEA is a more reliable measure than NSA because it directly reflects the shear-to-compression ratio of loading across the physis, while NSA reflects only the geometry of the neck relative to the diaphysis. HEA is also less sensitive to rotational errors of limb positioning. For these reasons modern treatment algorithms are based on HEA, with NSA used as an additional characteristic and for the planning of correction.

### CT and MRI

CT with three-dimensional reconstruction is useful in complex cases and for the planning of osteotomies — especially in severe deformities with a rotational component (increased anteversion), in syndromic developmental coxa vara with additional acetabular deformities, and in revision cases. MRI is used to assess the physis in borderline cases, when avascular necrosis is suspected as part of the differential diagnosis, and to assess the cartilaginous morphology of the femoral head and acetabulum before reconstructive surgery.

## Treatment of Developmental Coxa Vara

Treatment of developmental coxa vara depends on the HEA, the rate of progression, the symptomatology, the age of the child, and the presence of associated conditions. The goals of treatment are: (a) restoration of a normal NSA in order to normalize abductor mechanics and proximal physeal loading; (b) prevention of progression of the deformity and of femoral neck pseudarthrosis; (c) minimization of limb-length discrepancy; and (d) preservation of physeal growth when possible.

### Observation

Children with HEA below 45° and no symptoms are observed with serial AP radiographs every 6–12 months. In a small minority of these cases HEA decreases spontaneously with growth and no surgical intervention is needed; in the majority, HEA remains stable or rises slowly and the decision for surgery is deferred until the 60° threshold is reached or the clinical picture justifies early intervention.

The recommended physical restrictions during observation are modest: avoidance of intense impact loading (competitive running and jumping sports) without routine restriction of everyday activity or of school physical education. The attempts of older authors to actively counteract progression with abduction braces have not shown efficacy and are not used today.

### Surgical correction: valgus osteotomy of the proximal femur

The standard operative treatment of established developmental coxa vara with HEA above 45–60° (depending on the rate of progression and the clinical picture) is valgus osteotomy of the proximal femur. This is a complex procedure that requires careful preoperative planning, an appropriate implant, and meticulous surgical execution. The principal technical variants are:

Subtrochanteric valgus osteotomy (closing-wedge): the classical and most commonly used technique. Through a lateral incision under the greater trochanter, a transverse osteotomy of the femur is performed just distal to the lesser trochanter, with removal of a lateral wedge of bone whose apex is medial. Closing the osteotomy brings the distal fragment into abduction relative to the proximal fragment and so effectively increases NSA. The degree of correction is planned preoperatively by a trigonometric or geometric method: for a 30° NSA correction (a typical planned correction from 100° to 130°) a wedge is calculated with a width approximately equal to the width of the femur at the level of the osteotomy, multiplied by tan(30/2). Fixation is by a pediatric blade plate (Coventry, Wagner, AO) with an appropriate angle (90° when the osteotomy is close to perpendicular to the blade) or by a pediatric hip screw device.

Intertrochanteric valgus osteotomy (Pauwels Y-osteotomy): the technique described by Friedrich Pauwels in the 1950s is the historical standard. A Y-shaped wedge of bone is removed above the lesser trochanter, so that the proximal fragment is rotated about a horizontal axis and the physis is "brought" into a more horizontal orientation. Pauwels emphasized the simultaneous adduction component of the osteotomy, intended to reduce the effective moment arm of body weight around the center of the femoral head and so to reduce R. This technique, despite its technical complexity, remains the preferred approach for severe varus deformities in older children and adolescents, where the conceptual advantage of simultaneous correction of tilt and rotation outweighs the simplicity of the subtrochanteric wedge.

Opening-wedge osteotomy with bone graft: an alternative in which medial closure is avoided by inserting a structural bone graft (autograft from the iliac crest or allograft) into the open wedge. The advantage is that no bone is removed and limb length is preserved or slightly increased; the disadvantages are the additional donor-site morbidity and a theoretically less stable construct.

The standards of execution, formulated by Wagner and continued by Tönnis, include: (a) slight overcorrection — the target NSA is 140–150° (sometimes up to 160° in very young children), since some loss of correction with growth is the rule; (b) an adduction component to the osteotomy in older and adolescent children, consistent with Pauwels' principles; (c) rigid fixation that allows early postoperative motion of the hip; (d) avoidance of unnecessary periosteal dissection in the zone of the osteotomy to preserve blood supply and to favor rapid healing; and (e) attention to anteversion in syndromic coxa vara, where a simultaneous rotational correction may be required.

### Timing of surgical correction

The recommended age for surgical correction of established developmental coxa vara is between 3 and 6 years. Earlier surgery (under 3 years) is technically demanding because of the small size of the proximal femur and the limited platform for fixation; later surgery (after 8 years) increases technical complexity and makes adequate overcorrection harder to achieve.

In bilateral cases the standard is staged treatment — the more affected side first, then the contralateral side after complete healing and physiotherapy recovery (usually 4–6 months later). Simultaneous bilateral treatment is possible in specialized centers but prolongs immobilization and the risk of complications.

The postoperative protocol varies between centers: Wagner's classical approach required hip-knee spica immobilization for 6 weeks and prohibition of full weight-bearing for 12 weeks; modern practice with rigid internal fixation allows only soft protection (no spica), partial weight-bearing from 6 weeks, and full weight-bearing from 12 weeks. In all cases active hip flexion and abduction exercises are begun early (within the first 2–4 weeks) to prevent stiffness.

## Outcomes and Complications

Outcomes of valgus osteotomy for developmental coxa vara are generally good: in large series (Carroll, Weinstein, Borden, Coleman, Tönnis), in properly selected patients with adequate overcorrection, the physis is "brought" into normal or near-normal orientation, physeal bone formation is restored, the bone defect fills with new bone, NSA remains within the normal range to the end of growth, and abductor function recovers. The degree of functional recovery depends on: (a) the severity of the initial deformity; (b) the child's age at surgery — younger children have better remodeling potential; (c) the adequacy of overcorrection; (d) simultaneous correction of anteversion when indicated; and (e) in bilateral cases, the symmetry of bilateral treatment.

Complications of surgical treatment are divided into early, intermediate, and late:

### Early complications

Infection: rare (under 1%) in modern practice but always catastrophic. Prophylaxis is standard pediatric-dose antibiotics, meticulous sterility, and minimization of operative time.

Loss of fixation: the risk is higher in very young children with a limited bone platform for the implant, in osteoporosis in the underlying condition (e.g., osteogenesis imperfecta), and with inadequate postoperative protection. Prophylaxis is pediatric-specific implants and an adequate postoperative regimen.

Neurological injury: most commonly to the sciatic nerve in subtrochanteric osteotomy with medial lateralization of the distal fragment, or by retraction mechanisms. Prophylaxis is meticulous technique and avoidance of aggressive manipulation near the nerve.

Malunion and delayed union: the risk is about 5–10% with the classical subtrochanteric wedge and depends on the quality of the bone contact and the stability of the fixation. Treatment is early re-fixation with additional bone grafting.

### Intermediate and late complications

Loss of correction with growth: typically 5–10° and predictably allowed for in the preoperative plan by slight overcorrection. Greater loss is a sign of inadequate overcorrection or of continuation of the underlying disease.

Premature closure of the proximal femoral physis: the most common late complication and the cause of the second most common complication — relative overgrowth of the greater trochanter. Premature physeal closure occurs in a substantial proportion of cases after valgus osteotomy (various series — 30–50%) and is the result of: (a) operative violation of the physis; (b) the pre-existing physeal dysfunction underlying the disease; (c) altered loading after the osteotomy. The clinical expression is a small ATD and a persistent Trendelenburg sign.

Greater trochanteric overgrowth: as the central physis closes prematurely while the apophysis of the greater trochanter continues to grow, the relative height of the trochanter rises above the femoral head, the abductor moment arm is shortened, and the Trendelenburg sign returns. This complication appears in middle childhood and adolescence (typically 8–14 years). Treatment is an additional surgical procedure — greater trochanteric advancement: osteotomy of the trochanteric apophysis with lateral and distal transfer and refixation with screws or with a transverse wire. Outcomes of this secondary procedure are good in the great majority of cases and restore the effective abductor moment arm.

Alternatively (and to forestall the later second procedure) in very young children with severely progressive coxa vara, preventive apophysiodesis of the greater trochanter at the time of the first operation may be considered; this strategy, advocated by Borden and by Sabharwal, remains a matter of ongoing discussion and is not universally accepted.

Limb-length discrepancy: in unilateral developmental coxa vara the discrepancy often continues to progress to the end of growth despite successful correction of NSA, because premature closure of the proximal physis removes the principal source of proximal femoral growth. Management is traditional — serial measurement with scanograms, prediction of the final discrepancy, and planning of contralateral epiphysiodesis if a discrepancy of more than 2 cm is anticipated at maturity. With a predicted large discrepancy (> 4–5 cm), lengthening of the affected limb by the Ilizarov method or intramedullary by Paley is considered.

Recurrence of varus: rare with adequate overcorrection and no underlying disease, but a realistic possibility in syndromic developmental coxa vara, particularly in metaphyseal chondrodysplasia and in severe fibrous dysplasia. Treatment is reoperation with further valgus osteotomy.

Femoral neck pseudarthrosis: a rare but catastrophic complication of severely progressive untreated developmental coxa vara or of failed treatment. Progression of the physeal defect to complete interruption of the neck requires aggressive reconstruction with intertrochanteric valgus osteotomy combined with cancellous bone grafting of the defect or, in the most severe cases, with vascularized fibular grafting.

Degenerative arthritis in adulthood: the long-term fate of the hip after treated developmental coxa vara depends on the quality of correction, on preservation of the sphericity of the femoral head, and on secondary acetabular deformities. In the majority of cases function remains good to the fourth or sixth decade, but progressive degeneration is a real possibility and warrants follow-up with periodic radiographs in adult life. Total hip arthroplasty, when it becomes necessary, is technically more demanding than primary because of residual bone deformity and often requires specialized implants.

## Coxa Vara in Skeletal Dysplasias

Coxa vara is a frequent component of the phenotype of many skeletal dysplasias. Recognition of the underlying dysplasia is essential, because treatment of dysplastic coxa vara — though following the same principles as developmental coxa vara — differs substantially in the detail: the bone of a child with a dysplasia is more vulnerable, the physis is primarily disordered, physeal closure occurs earlier and more predictably, the risk of recurrence is higher, and additional deformities are usually present that require a complex plan.

### Cleidocranial dysplasia

Cleidocranial dysplasia (CCD) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the RUNX2 (CBFA1) gene on chromosome 6p21, encoding a key transcription factor for osteoblast differentiation. The classical triad is aplasia or hypoplasia of the clavicles with compensatory shoulder droop, delayed closure of the cranial sutures with a large anterior fontanelle and extensive Wormian bones, and dental anomalies with delayed dentition and supernumerary permanent teeth. Coxa vara is one of the common skeletal manifestations, appearing in early childhood and often progressive. The particular feature is that in CCD the physis is primarily dysplastic and physeal bone formation is slowed, so that both surgical healing and postoperative growth-related correction are slower than expected. Treatment follows the general algorithm of valgus osteotomy but with emphasis on fixation (the implant must provide longer stabilization because of the slow healing) and on the timing of the correction — postponement to later childhood when possible.

### Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia (SED)

Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia is a group of autosomal dominant (SED congenita) and autosomal recessive (SED tarda) disorders of type II collagen, caused by mutations in the COL2A1 gene. Clinically they are characterized by disproportionate short-trunk dwarfism with relatively normal limbs, progressive deformities of the spine (kyphoscoliosis), and systemic joint deformities. Coxa vara is almost universal in SED congenita and often progresses rapidly in the first 5 years of life. In addition to varus, femoral head dysplasia is common with a small, irregularly ossified head and a wide, flat acetabulum. For these reasons, in SED the treatment of coxa vara is often part of a broader reconstructive strategy that includes acetabular reconstruction (Salter, Pemberton, or Dega), and the valgus femoral osteotomy is planned to improve not only NSA but also coverage of the femoral head. Outcomes are less predictable than in isolated developmental coxa vara and total hip arthroplasty in early adulthood is not uncommon.

### Metaphyseal chondrodysplasia (Schmid type and Jansen type)

Metaphyseal chondrodysplasia is a group of disorders of the metaphysis with disordered endochondral ossification. The Schmid type — autosomal dominant, caused by mutations in the COL10A1 gene for type X collagen — is relatively common and is characterized by mild to moderate disproportionate short-trunk dwarfism, varus deformity of the lower limbs, and characteristic metaphyseal changes on radiograph. Coxa vara is common and usually moderate; treatment follows the principles for developmental coxa vara but with emphasis on the systemic metaphyseal disturbance and the risk of recurrence. The Jansen type — a rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by activating mutations of the PTHR1 gene for the PTH/PTHrP receptor — is significantly more severe, with marked hypercalcemia, severe metaphyseal disturbance, and more severe coxa vara; treatment is highly specialized and often multidisciplinary.

### Spondylometaphyseal dysplasia

Spondylometaphyseal dysplasia (SMD) groups together disorders with vertebral and metaphyseal involvement. The Kozlowski type is the best known and is characterized by severe metaphyseal disturbance, progressive scoliosis, and coxa vara presenting in early childhood. Treatment of coxa vara in this context follows the general algorithm with emphasis on the overall skeletal fragility and the need for simultaneous spinal assessment.

### Campomelic dysplasia

Campomelic dysplasia is a severe autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the SOX9 gene for a cartilage transcription factor. The classical manifestation is bowing of the long bones (from the Greek *kampe* — bend, and *melos* — limb; literally "bent limb"), especially of the tibia, with a characteristic anterolateral curve; coxa vara is a frequently associated finding. Multiple systemic manifestations (respiratory failure from laryngeal and tracheal hypoplasia, sex-chromosome anomalies with XY sex reversal) often make the prognosis poor in the neonatal period.

### Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia

Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED) is a group of autosomal dominant (COMP, COL9A1/2/3, MATN3) and autosomal recessive (SLC26A2/DTDST) disorders that affect epiphyseal osteochondral formation at multiple joints. The clinical presentation is delayed ossification and incomplete development of the epiphyses of the long bones, in the limbs and in the spine, with mild disproportionate short stature and early symptomatic osteoarthritis. Coxa vara is frequently associated with the dysplastic, delayed-ossifying femoral head; the differential diagnosis from bilateral Perthes disease can be extraordinarily difficult in early childhood, and correct distinction has significant treatment implications.

## Coxa Vara in Fibrous Dysplasia

Fibrous dysplasia is a benign medullary fibro-osseous lesion in which normal bone is replaced by dysplastic fibrous tissue containing scattered, poorly mineralized trabeculae of woven bone. Since the 1990s the lesion has been understood as a developmental disorder caused by a post-zygotic activating mutation in the GNAS1 gene, which encodes the α-subunit of the stimulatory G protein. Somatic mosaicism accounts for the highly variable phenotype — from a single asymptomatic monostotic lesion to widespread polyostotic disease with severe skeletal deformity, and to McCune–Albright syndrome with its classical triad of polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait pigmentation with "Coast-of-Maine" jagged borders, and endocrine hyperfunction (precocious puberty, hyperthyroidism, GH-secreting adenomas).

When polyostotic involvement of the proximal femur is present, fibrous dysplasia produces the pathognomonic "shepherd's-crook" deformity: progressive varus of the proximal femur, caused by repeated stress fractures through the dysplastic-weakened bone with secondary plastic deformation in a varus position. The deformity is often severe, progresses with growth, and in the adult is one of the classical indications for reconstructive surgery in orthopaedics. Radiographically there is a characteristic intramedullary "ground-glass" density reflecting the mineralization of woven trabeculae in the dysplastic marrow, together with expansion of the bone, thinning of the cortex, and a sclerotic rim.

Treatment of severe "shepherd's-crook" deformity in polyostotic fibrous dysplasia is subtrochanteric or intertrochanteric valgus osteotomy with intramedullary fixation. The principles are: (a) an intramedullary implant (Russell–Taylor or a specialized reconstruction nail with proximal fixation) is preferred over plate osteosynthesis because the diseased cortex is poor for screw purchase and because the entire length of the bone may be involved; (b) the wedge correction must be large — often 40–50° — because of the severe baseline deformity; (c) curettage and bone grafting of the lesion at the osteotomy site can improve the quality of the bone contact but must be balanced against the risk of secondary fracture from removal of structural bone; (d) bisphosphonates (intravenous pamidronate or zoledronate) systemically reduce bone pain and biochemical markers of bone turnover, and are sometimes used perioperatively; (e) radiation therapy is absolutely contraindicated because of the risk of inducing sarcomatous transformation.

The results of this reconstructive surgery are generally satisfactory, but the risk of disease progression, of repeated deformity, and of stress fracture in other segments of the affected bone remains, and several interventions over the patient's lifetime are not uncommon.

## Coxa Vara in Osteogenesis Imperfecta

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a group of inherited disorders of type I collagen causing mild to severe bone fragility with multiple fractures. The Sillence classification of 1979 distinguishes four principal types — type I (mild, autosomal dominant, COL1A1 haploinsufficient), type II (the lethal perinatal form), type III (the severe progressive deforming form), and type IV (moderate with disproportionate short stature) — but modern genetic classification expands the spectrum to 19+ types with various molecular mechanisms. Coxa vara is common in types III and IV and is the result of multiple stress fractures of the proximal femur with plastic deformation in varus, analogous to the "shepherd's crook" of fibrous dysplasia but with different consequences for surgical treatment.

When severe coxa vara is established in OI, the standard treatment is reconstructive intramedullary fixation with telescoping rods (Fassier–Duval or Bailey–Dubow type). The principles of the procedure are: (a) straightening of multiple femoral deformities by multiple osteotomies (proximal, midshaft, distal — according to the localization of the deformities) and alignment over a guiding rod; (b) the telescoping rod elongates with the growth of the child, reducing the need for repeat changes; (c) simultaneously with the rod, a valgus osteotomy of the proximal femur is performed to correct NSA; (d) bisphosphonates (cyclic intravenous therapy with pamidronate or zoledronate) are central to modern treatment and significantly reduce fracture frequency and slow the progression of deformities.

Long-term outcomes in OI depend substantially on the severity of the underlying disease: in moderate forms effective treatment allows preservation of ambulation into adulthood; in severe forms a wheelchair is often required despite optimal treatment.

## Coxa Vara in Multiple Hereditary Exostoses

Multiple hereditary exostoses (MHE), also called multiple osteochondromas or diaphyseal aclasis, is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the EXT1 or EXT2 genes (with a third, rarer locus EXT3) for glucosyltransferases of the exostosin family. The disorder is characterized by the formation of multiple osteochondromas (bony-cartilaginous outgrowths) around the physes of the long bones and the flat bones from childhood; the prevalence is approximately 1 in 50,000.

In MHE the proximal femoral metaphysis is often involved by one or more osteochondromas, which distort the growth of the physis and produce coxa vara, often combined with shortening of the femoral neck and relative overgrowth of the greater trochanter. Treatment follows the principles for developmental coxa vara — valgus osteotomy with slight overcorrection — often combined with resection of symptomatic osteochondromas if they cause mechanical compression or pain. Long-term care requires surveillance for the two principal risks of MHE: malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma (lifetime risk about 1–3%, increased with involvement of the pelvic girdle and the proximal long bones — so progressive pain or growth of a larger lesion in adulthood requires biopsy) and the development of coxa valga in other patients, since the effect of osteochondromas on the proximal physis is not universally varus.

## Post-Infectious Coxa Vara ("Tom Smith Arthritis")

Post-infectious coxa vara — also known as "Tom Smith arthritis" after the British surgeon who described the condition in the nineteenth century — is the sequela of neonatal or early infantile septic arthritis of the hip with destruction of the proximal physis and femoral head. Progressive necrosis and subsequent irregular healing lead to severe deformity with shortening of the femoral neck, severe varus, secondary acetabular changes, and ultimately early and severe degenerative arthritis.

The Hunka & Choi classification of 1982 distinguishes five types of residual changes after infantile septic arthritis: type I — normal femoral head and neck; type II — deformed head with relatively preserved neck; type III — femoral neck pseudarthrosis; type IV — severe destruction of the head and neck; and type V — severe destruction with dislocation. Treatment of types III–V is particularly complex and requires an individualized approach: in type III a valgus osteotomy with bone grafting of the pseudarthrosis can restore continuity; in types IV–V a Schanz osteotomy (an angulation osteotomy of the proximal femur directing the distal fragment into a position that articulates with the acetabulum as a pseudoarticulation — a palliative reconstruction in adulthood) may be required, or aggressive reconstruction with total hip arthroplasty in adulthood, often requiring specialized components.

## Post-Traumatic Coxa Vara

Post-traumatic coxa vara may occur in three principal scenarios: after femoral neck fracture with malunion in varus (particularly in pediatric fractures of Delbet types I–II with initially preserved blood supply); after traumatic epiphysiolysis of the proximal femoral physis with premature closure and subsequent coxa breva and relative varus; and after avascular necrosis of the femoral head with secondary head deformity and relative neck shortening. Treatment follows the principles for developmental coxa vara — valgus osteotomy at HEA above 45–60° and/or at NSA below 110° — with additional attention to avascular necrosis and its sequelae if present.

## Coxa Vara in Legg–Calvé–Perthes Disease and after SCFE

In Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease the classical late consequence is coxa magna (an enlarged, non-spherical femoral head) and coxa breva (a shortened femoral neck with relative trochanteric overgrowth) rather than true coxa vara. In a small subset of severe cases, especially with non-classical involvement of the medial column of the femoral head and neck, a real varus deformity of NSA may develop that requires the same approach of valgus osteotomy (combined sometimes with trochanteric apophysiodesis or with trochanteric advancement).

After SCFE retroversion (posterior slip of the epiphysis relative to the metaphysis) is more common than true varus, but in severe slips or after failed in-situ pinning with progression, a real coxa vara may develop and require corrective osteotomy — typically the intertrochanteric Imhäuser–Southwick technique with three-dimensional correction (flexion, valgus, internal rotation) of the distal fragment.

## Coxa Vara in Adolescence

Coxa vara presenting for the first time in adolescence without a prior history of developmental coxa vara or of skeletal dysplasia is rare and demands a thorough differential diagnosis. The possibilities include: occult or mildly presenting SCFE with a predominantly varus component; unilateral late-presenting Perthes disease; idiopathic adolescent coxa vara (rare); metabolic disease (hypophosphatemic rickets, severe fluorosis); a tumor of the proximal femur (osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma, aneurysmal bone cyst of the femoral neck); and post-infectious sequelae of incompletely treated or unrecognized early infantile infection.

Treatment follows the general principles — valgus osteotomy for significant NSA deficit — with emphasis on remaining growth and its implications for surgical planning. Hemiepiphysiodesis of the trochanteric apophysis has been used to control progressive trochanteric overgrowth in selected cases, especially in unilateral disease with anticipated limb-length discrepancy.

## Pauwels Biomechanics

The Pauwels biomechanical analysis of the proximal femur — published in two classical monographs (*Der Schenkelhalsbruch ein mechanisches Problem*, 1935, and *Atlas zur Biomechanik der gesunden und kranken Hüfte*, 1973; and in English translation as *Biomechanics of the Normal and Diseased Hip*, Springer, 1976) — provides the conceptual framework not only for the surgical treatment of coxa vara but for all of modern reconstructive hip surgery. The principal conceptual contributions of Pauwels are the following:

### The hip joint reaction force (R)

In the single-leg (unipedal) phase of gait the hip joint is subject to substantial loading, not only from the body weight (W) of the longitudinally placed mass above the hip joint (approximately 5/6 of the total body mass when the supported limb is taken into account) but also from the tension of the hip abductors (M) needed to maintain a horizontal pelvis during stance on that leg. Pauwels formulated an equilibrium equation around the center of the femoral head: M × m = W × w, where m is the abductor moment arm (about 5 cm in normal anatomy) and w is the moment arm of body weight (about 12–14 cm, depending on the position of the body center of gravity). It follows that M = W × (w/m), which for typical values gives M ≈ 2.5–3 × W. The resultant of the two forces (R = M + W) is directed obliquely medial and downward and reaches 3.5–4 × W for ordinary gait and up to 7–8 × W for stair climbing and running.

In coxa vara the abductor moment arm (m) is reduced (because the tip of the trochanter is closer to the center of the femoral head), which requires a larger M for the same W. This increases R and imposes greater loading on the proximal femoral neck and so on the physis. The converse — in coxa valga the moment arm is enlarged, M is reduced, and R is smaller.

### Geometry of loading across the physis

The resultant R crosses the proximal physis at a particular angle that determines whether the component of R perpendicular to the physis (compressive) or the component parallel to the physis (shear) predominates. In a normal hip the angle between R and the physis is such that the compressive component is much larger than the shear; in coxa vara the physis is more vertical and the shear component grows at the expense of the compressive. At a critical physeal angle of about 35–40° above the horizontal, shear loading begins to predominate. Because physeal bone formation is stimulated by compression and inhibited by shear, the coxa-vara physis is subject to a self-sustaining vicious circle: varus reduces compression and increases shear, shear disturbs growth, disturbed growth deepens varus.

This concept is the direct conceptual justification for the Pauwels intertrochanteric Y-osteotomy: rotating the proximal fragment so that the physis takes a more horizontal orientation relative to R restores the compressive pattern of loading and permits physeal healing. The same logic explains why simple increase of NSA (without account of the orientation of R) is inadequate in severe cases and why the combination of valgus with an adduction component — as in the classical Pauwels Y-osteotomy — is conceptually superior to a simple wedge.

### Trigonometry of the planned correction

Pauwels derived trigonometric equations for calculating the necessary wedge for a planned valgus correction. The simple subtrochanteric closing-wedge technique requires a wedge with a apex angle equal to the planned NSA correction (e.g., 30° correction → 30° wedge), but the wedge size (base) is equal to the width of the femur at the level of the osteotomy multiplied by tan(planned correction/2). For a typical 7-year-old with a femoral width of 25 mm at the level of the osteotomy and a planned correction of 30°, the base of the wedge is 25 × tan(15°) = 25 × 0.27 = 6.7 mm. The more complex intertrochanteric Y-osteotomy with simultaneous correction of tilt and rotation requires more complex geometry, usually illustrated by Pauwels with geometric diagrams.

The Pauwels analysis remains current in modern practice. Paley in *Principles of Deformity Correction* (Springer, 2002) summarizes and extends the Pauwels concepts in the more general framework of CORA (center of rotation of angulation) and LACS (limb anatomical and mechanical axis) analysis, while Sabharwal in *Pediatric Lower Limb Deformities* (Springer, 2016) translates these principles into practical algorithms for the choice of osteotomy in childhood.

## Surgical Techniques — Detailed Overview

### Subtrochanteric valgus osteotomy with pediatric blade plate

The classical technique, described by Coventry (1953) and developed by Wagner (1970s) and Tönnis (1987), is as follows: patient position — supine on a radiolucent table with a small roll under the affected side. Skin incision — lateral, from under the greater trochanter distally for about 10–15 cm. The approach passes through the fascia of the thigh and the iliotibial tract in line with the incision; the vastus lateralis is detached from the intermuscular septum and elevated forward, exposing the lateral cortex of the femur in the intertrochanteric and subtrochanteric region. Under image intensifier control a transverse osteotomy of the femur is made just distal to the lesser trochanter; the wedge is removed with apex medial and base lateral. The size and geometry of the wedge are planned preoperatively as described in the biomechanics section. The implant — a pediatric blade plate (Coventry, Wagner, or AO) with an appropriate angle — is inserted through the proximal fragment along a pre-planned trajectory so that after closure of the osteotomy the plate seats on the lateral surface of the distal fragment. Screws in the distal fragment stabilize the construct.

The advantages of this technique are its relative simplicity, well-established efficacy, and the availability of suitable pediatric implants. The disadvantages are: the vertical correction is pure (without an adduction component), which may leave the physis still in a relatively vertical orientation in severe cases; implant placement in the proximal fragment can be technically demanding in very young children with a limited bone platform; and, in the absence of simultaneous rotational correction, rotational problems remain unresolved.

### Pauwels intertrochanteric Y-osteotomy

The classical Pauwels technique (1950s) is conceptually superior in severe coxa vara in older children and adolescents. The skin approach is similar to that for subtrochanteric osteotomy. A Y-shaped wedge is removed in the intertrochanteric region: the upper limb of the Y runs obliquely from lateral-cranial to medial-caudal; the lower limb runs obliquely from medial-cranial to lateral-caudal; and the central wedge fragment is "inscribed" between the two limbs. After removal of the wedge and manipulation of the proximal fragment into simultaneous valgus and adduction, the fragments are apposed and fixed with a pediatric blade plate or a pediatric hip screw device. The advantages are simultaneous correction of physeal tilt and an adduction component to reduce R; the disadvantages are significant technical complexity and a larger dissection zone in the intertrochanteric region with potentially increased risk to the blood supply of the head.

### Opening-wedge osteotomy with bone graft

An alternative technique in which medial closure is avoided by opening-wedge osteotomy and insertion of a structural bone graft (autograft from the iliac crest or allograft from a bone bank). The advantage is that no bone is removed and limb length is preserved or slightly increased, which is useful in unilateral disease with anticipated significant limb-length discrepancy; the disadvantages are the additional donor-site morbidity (with autograft), a theoretically less stable construct, and a higher risk of loss of correction.

### External fixation and gradual correction (Ilizarov/Paley)

For severe multiplanar deformities with additional rotational and lengthening components and for syndromic developmental coxa vara in older children with limb-length discrepancy over 4–5 cm, external fixation by the Ilizarov method or with a hexapod (Taylor Spatial Frame) allows gradual correction of the three axes of the deformity plus lengthening in a single procedure. This technique, developed by the Soviet school of Ilizarov in the 1960s and popularized in the West by Paley and colleagues in the 1990s, remains indicated in complex cases and in selected centers.

### Greater trochanteric advancement

The additional or secondary procedure of greater trochanteric advancement is indicated for established relative overgrowth of the trochanter with abductor dysfunction after earlier valgus osteotomy. The technique is performed through a lateral approach: osteotomy of the trochanteric apophysis at the plane of the base of the trochanter; the apophysis with the abductor muscle attachments is detached and transferred laterally and distally into the plane of the physiological abductor moment arm (m in the Pauwels analysis); refixation is by screws or by a transverse wire. The outcomes of this procedure are good in the great majority of cases and restore the effective abductor moment arm.

## Complications, Differential Diagnosis, and Long-Term Outcomes

The differential diagnosis of pediatric coxa vara covers a wide spectrum of conditions that may mimic the picture of classical developmental coxa vara and must be systematically distinguished:

### Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)

DDH with late treatment or late presentation can produce a varus appearance of the proximal femur with shortening of the neck, particularly in patients with prior acetabular remodeling or with residual dysplasia. The history of DDH in early childhood, the absence of the Fairbank triangle on radiographs, the dysplastic acetabulum (with increased acetabular index), and the often associated external signs (Ortolani and Barlow tests in the neonatal period, asymmetry of the thigh skin creases) distinguish DDH.

### Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease

Perthes produces relative trochanteric overgrowth that resembles coxa vara on examination, but the radiographic features — fragmentation of the femoral head, metaphyseal cysts, widening of the medial joint space — and the typical age and presentation distinguish Perthes from isolated developmental coxa vara. In severe cases of Perthes a real varus component may also develop, requiring valgus osteotomy in combination with the measures usual for Perthes.

### Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)

SCFE produces an apparent varus from the posterior slip of the head, but the older age (adolescence), the characteristic radiographic features — Klein's line not transecting the epiphysis, physeal widening, metaphyseal blanch — and the underlying pathogenesis distinguish SCFE from developmental coxa vara. Chronic stable SCFE may require similar valgus reconstructive osteotomy (Imhäuser–Southwick).

### Tumors of the proximal femur

Cysts, osteochondromas, osteoid osteoma, and aneurysmal bone cyst in the proximal femoral metaphysis or neck can cause local weakness and deformity that resembles coxa vara. Systematic radiographic assessment of the lesion matrix, the presence of cortical reaction, and, if needed, biopsy distinguish the tumor conditions.

### Metabolic disease

Hypophosphatemic rickets, vitamin D–dependent rickets, and severe fluorosis can produce varus deformity of the proximal femur in conjunction with a systemic metabolic disturbance and a characteristic radiographic pattern (physeal widening, poor calcium front, generalized osteomalacia). Biochemical workup (serum Ca, P, ALP, 25-OH vitamin D, PTH, fluoride excretion) is required in any suspicious coxa vara to exclude metabolic disease.

Long-term outcomes of treated and untreated coxa vara are relatively well documented in several large series. The natural history of untreated progressive developmental coxa vara is toward severe deformity, abductor dysfunction, limb-length discrepancy, formation of a femoral neck pseudarthrosis in the most severe cases, and accelerated degenerative arthritis of the hip in adulthood. Adequate surgical correction by valgus osteotomy restores hip mechanics in the great majority of cases and prevents the progression of these complications. Long-term outcomes depend substantially on the underlying etiology: developmental coxa vara without other anomalies has the best prognosis, with more than 80% satisfactory function in adulthood; coxa vara in the setting of skeletal dysplasia, post-infectious or post-traumatic changes is more variable and total hip arthroplasty in early adulthood is not uncommon.

## Local Contributions (by Country)

The world literature on coxa vara bears the imprint of particular national traditions in pediatric orthopaedics. This section summarizes the principal contributions by country, listed alphabetically by the English name of the country.

### Austria

Adolf Lorenz (Vienna, 1854–1946) and his school were among the first to address pediatric hip deformities systematically, including infantile coxa vara, in the context of the practice of the time for DDH and other congenital anomalies. Later, Reinhard Graf (Stolzalpe) developed the ultrasound assessment of the infant hip, which today is the standard for early assessment and for suspected developmental coxa vara.

### Bulgaria

The Bulgarian orthopaedic school treats coxa vara in the traditional textbooks of general and pediatric orthopaedics (Boycho Boychev and colleagues, *Surgical Orthopaedics*, 1969 and subsequent editions). In national practice the standard valgus osteotomies of Coventry/Wagner with pediatric blade plates are applied in specialized pediatric orthopaedic centers (UMHATEM "N. I. Pirogov" and SBALOZ) and in the university clinics in Sofia, Plovdiv, and Varna. The Bulgarian literature is consolidated in the modern textbooks of pediatric orthopaedics by the staff of the Medical University of Sofia.

### Canada

Mercer Rang (Toronto, 1933–2003) and Dennis Wenger summarized North American practice in *The Art and Practice of Children's Orthopaedics* (1993), with a practical emphasis on individualization of treatment in developmental and dysplastic coxa vara. The Canadian contribution to intertrochanteric osteotomy technique — especially in the context of SCFE and its sequelae — continues through the work of Robert Salter and his successors at SickKids.

### Germany

The German contribution to the understanding and treatment of coxa vara is incontestably the most significant in the world literature. Friedrich Pauwels (Aachen, 1885–1980) established the biomechanical framework with his classical monograph *Der Schenkelhalsbruch ein mechanisches Problem* (Stuttgart, 1935) and the later *Atlas zur Biomechanik der gesunden und kranken Hüfte* (Springer, 1973); the Pauwels intertrochanteric Y-osteotomy remains the historical surgical benchmark. Heinrich Hilgenreiner (Prague/Vienna, 1870–1954) provided the line that bears his name, which serves as the basis of the Hilgenreiner–epiphyseal angle. Dietrich Tönnis (Dortmund) consolidated the modern principles of varus and valgus osteotomy in his monograph *Congenital Dysplasia and Dislocation of the Hip in Children and Adults* (Springer, 1987), in which he also describes the "wandering" of the triangular fragment with growth. Heinrich Wagner (Rheinfelden) developed the pediatric blade plate that bears his name and refined intertrochanteric osteotomy technique in the late 1960s and early 1970s. The more recent contribution of Reinhold Ganz (born in Switzerland but for much of his career in Bern and in Germany) to the understanding of the pericapital and periacetabular anatomy of the hip has left its mark on the differential diagnosis of coxa vara from early adulthood femoroacetabular impingement.

### Italy

Renato Bombelli (Milan) developed the osteotomy principles for reconstruction of the varus dysplastic hip in adults in his monograph *Osteoarthritis of the Hip — Classification and Pathogenesis* (Springer, 1976; 2nd edition 1983) and emphasized the role of valgus-extension intertrochanteric osteotomy. The Italian tradition in pediatric orthopaedics (Rizzoli Institute, Bologna) continues to contribute to reconstructive strategies for complex pediatric hip deformities.

### Japan

Takaaki Sugioka (Kurume, 1932–2018) developed the transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy, originally directed at joint-preserving surgery for avascular necrosis of the femoral head, but applicable also in complex deformities including sequelae of SCFE with a true varus component. The Japanese school in pediatric orthopaedics continues to contribute to reconstructive strategies for severe conditions.

### Russia

Gavriil A. Ilizarov (Kurgan, 1921–1992) developed the principles of gradual bone correction by external fixation, which in modern orthopaedics are applied to severe multiplanar deformities of the proximal femur, including severe coxa vara in osteogenesis imperfecta, in multiple hereditary exostoses, and in post-infectious deformities. The Ilizarov school of Kurgan continues to contribute to reconstructive surgery of complex pediatric limb deformities.

### Switzerland

The AO school of Davos — Maurice Müller (Bern), Martin Allgöwer (Basel), and colleagues — standardized pediatric blade plates and the principles of rigid bone fixation that are even today the basis of coxa vara surgery in Europe and a substantial part of the world. Reinhold Ganz (Bern) developed the periacetabular osteotomy, which in dysplastic coxa vara combined with acetabular dysplasia is used for reconstructive therapy in late adolescence and early adulthood.

### Turkey

The Turkish school of pediatric orthopaedics, centered in Ankara (Hacettepe University) and Istanbul, contributes to modern series on the outcomes of valgus osteotomy and to techniques for reconstruction of severe post-infectious deformities.

### United Kingdom

Sir Harold Fairbank (London, 1876–1961) first described the triangular bone fragment in the inferomedial corner of the femoral neck in developmental coxa vara in his monograph *Atlas of General Affections of the Skeleton* (1951); since then the fragment has borne his name. The British tradition in pediatric orthopaedics — represented by A. Graham Apley in *Apley's System of Orthopaedics and Fractures* — continues to define the standard of clinical assessment and operative indications.

### United States

The North American tradition of pediatric orthopaedics includes Mihran Tachdjian, John Herring (Texas Scottish Rite Hospital, Dallas) — whose *Tachdjian's Pediatric Orthopaedics* remains the world standard; Walter Borden and Robert Coleman — with a series of classical works on developmental coxa vara and on greater trochanteric advancement; Stuart Weinstein (University of Iowa) — who validated the Hilgenreiner–epiphyseal angle as a prognostic measure in 1984; Sanjeev Sabharwal — author of *Pediatric Lower Limb Deformities* (Springer, 2016) with practical algorithms for the choice of osteotomy; and Dror Paley — author of *Principles of Deformity Correction* (Springer, 2002), the conceptual basis of modern CORA analysis and osteotomy geometry.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

Coxa vara in children and adolescents is a heterogeneous group of conditions unified by the common biomechanical consequence of a reduced femoral neck–shaft angle. Developmental coxa vara — rare, idiopathic, presenting in early childhood with painless limp and the characteristic Fairbank triangle on the AP pelvis radiograph — is quantified by the Hilgenreiner–epiphyseal angle, the only measure with proven prognostic value: HEA above 60° practically always progresses and is an indication for valgus osteotomy, HEA 45–60° is an intermediate zone, and HEA below 45° permits observation. Dysplastic coxa vara — a component of many skeletal dysplasias — requires an individualized plan that takes account of the systemic features of the underlying disease and is often combined with acetabular reconstruction. Coxa vara in fibrous dysplasia ("shepherd's crook") and in osteogenesis imperfecta requires intramedullary fixation with appropriate implants. Post-infectious and post-traumatic coxa vara are technically more difficult and often require reconstructive surgery with less predictable long-term outcomes.

The Pauwels biomechanical analysis provides the conceptual basis for surgical correction: conversion of the high-angle physeal orientation to a more horizontal one that redistributes loading from shear to compression and so permits physeal healing. The standard operative treatment is valgus osteotomy of the proximal femur — subtrochanteric, intertrochanteric Pauwels, or opening-wedge — with slight overcorrection to 140–150° NSA, rigid fixation with a pediatric blade plate or a pediatric hip screw device, and meticulous postoperative recovery. The principal long-term complications are premature closure of the proximal physis with relative trochanteric overgrowth (requiring greater trochanteric advancement in adolescence), limb-length discrepancy (requiring contralateral epiphysiodesis or lengthening of the affected limb), and degenerative arthritis in adulthood. Across all etiologies, accurate diagnosis, quantitative radiographic assessment by HEA, timely surgical correction in progressive cases, and attention to the late sequelae of trochanteric overgrowth and limb-length discrepancy determine successful outcomes.
