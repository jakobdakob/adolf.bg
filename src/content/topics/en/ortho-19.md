## Introduction and Definitions

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) is a disorder of the adolescent hip in which the proximal femoral physis fails mechanically, allowing the metaphysis to translate and rotate relative to the epiphysis. Although the displacement is conventionally described as the epiphysis "slipping" off the metaphysis, the actual mechanical event is the reverse: the epiphysis remains held in the acetabulum by the ligamentum capitis femoris and the joint capsule, while the femoral neck and shaft displace anteriorly and externally rotate beneath it. The result is the characteristic posterior and medial position of the femoral head relative to the metaphysis — a "posterior retroversion" of the proximal femur that forms the substrate for later cam-type femoroacetabular impingement.

SCFE remains one of the most consequential diagnoses in adolescent orthopedics for three reasons. First, delayed diagnosis is common — a classic teaching point is that SCFE is missed in nearly half of patients on first presentation because of knee pain. Second, the consequences of missed or delayed diagnosis include severe deformity and avascular necrosis with lifelong disability. Third, surgical treatment is among the most consequential decisions in pediatric orthopedic practice: technical error or delay measured in hours, not days, can determine the difference between an excellent long-term outcome and established avascular necrosis.

This chapter synthesizes content from Tachdjian's Pediatric Orthopaedics (Herring, 6e), Lovell & Winter's Pediatric Orthopaedics (Flynn, Weinstein, Crawford, 8e), Callaghan's The Adult Hip (3e, particularly the chapters on modified Dunn and Ganz surgical dislocation), Nho/Leunig/Kelly's Hip Arthroscopy and Hip Joint Preservation Surgery, Apley & Solomon's System of Orthopaedics and Trauma, and the landmark papers by Loder (1993), Aronsson and Carney, Ganz, Leunig, and Sankar.

## Anatomy and Vascular Supply

An understanding of the vascular anatomy of the proximal femur is indispensable for the rational treatment of SCFE and for assessment of the risk of avascular necrosis (AVN). The principal blood supply to the adolescent femoral head arises from the medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA), whose deep branch ascends along the lower border of obturator externus, passes between gemellus inferior and obturator externus, perforates the capsule along the posterosuperior margin of the femoral neck, and continues as a series of retinacular (subsynovial) vessels of Weitbrecht to the articular cartilage of the head. The lateral epiphyseal branches are the most important functional vessels for the weight-bearing zone of the head.

The lateral femoral circumflex artery (LFCA) contributes minimally to the femoral head after closure of the proximal femoral physis but in the adolescent provides a limited medial-anterior supply. The artery of the ligamentum teres (a branch of the obturator or medial femoral circumflex artery) contributes less than 20% of the blood supply to the adolescent head and cannot compensate for loss of the retinacular vessels.

The two critically vulnerable points in this vascular arrangement are: (1) the posterosuperior capsule of the femoral neck, where the retinacular branches are most densely grouped and may be disrupted by the sharp anterior metaphyseal edge during the slip or by aggressive reduction; and (2) the intracapsular course of the deep branch of the MFCA, which may be occluded by tamponade from an intra-articular hematoma in acute unstable SCFE. The landmark work of Gautier, Ganz, and colleagues (2000) on the vascular anatomy of the femoral head, confirmed by angiographic and laser-Doppler studies, provides the anatomic basis for the surgical hip dislocation approach and for the modified Dunn — the two procedures that allow anatomic reduction with preservation of the MFCA.

The classic angiographic and injection studies of Trueta (1957) and Chung (1976) divided the blood supply of the adolescent femoral head into four groups: lateral epiphyseal vessels (the dominant group, from the deep branch of the MFCA, entering posterosuperiorly), superior metaphyseal vessels, inferior metaphyseal vessels (from the LFCA), and vessels of the ligamentum teres. The lateral epiphyseal vessels supply 75–80% of the blood to the adolescent femoral head and are almost entirely responsible for the weight-bearing posterosuperior surface, which collapses in classical post-SCFE AVN.

## Epidemiology

The incidence of SCFE is approximately 10–11 per 100,000 in the United States, with substantial variation by sex, ethnicity, and geographic region. Boys are affected approximately 1.5 to 2 times more often than girls. The peak age of onset is 13 years in boys and 11 years in girls, reflecting the timing of pubertal growth and physeal maturation. Bilateral SCFE occurs in 20–40% of cases in the classical series and in close to 60% in the more recent prospective MRI-based series by Sankar and colleagues, who demonstrated that the rate of "silent" contralateral disease had been underestimated. The contralateral slip typically develops within 18–24 months of the initial slip.

The condition is significantly more common in African American, Hispanic, and Polynesian populations than in white European-descended populations. Epidemiologic studies from Hawaii and New Zealand report incidences exceeding 50 per 100,000 among Polynesian children — one of the highest recorded ratios for the disease. The classical risk factor profile is the obese adolescent male in late puberty (so-called "Andy Gump" body habitus of relative short stature for weight, with delayed sexual maturation and a substantial proportion of patients having concurrent hypogonadal or hypothyroid biochemistry).

The epidemiologic rise in childhood obesity in the last three decades has been paralleled by a documented rise in SCFE incidence in multiple populations, with a clear gradient: risk roughly doubles for each quartile increase in BMI above the 95th percentile for age.

## Etiology and Pathogenesis

The fundamental defect in SCFE is mechanical failure of the proximal femoral physis under physiological load. Several factors contribute to the increased risk during the adolescent growth spurt:

(1) the physis is most active during pubertal growth, and rapid metaphyseal expansion produces a thicker but mechanically weaker physeal cartilage with a widened hypertrophic zone — the layer through which the slip propagates;

(2) the orientation of the physis becomes more vertical during the adolescent growth spurt, shifting from the more horizontal childhood position toward a relatively vertical position, increasing the shear forces across it under load;

(3) retroversion of the femoral neck, which normally diminishes in adolescence, is preserved or even exaggerated in patients at risk for SCFE — often cited as a predisposing anatomic factor;

(4) excess body weight in the obese patient increases the loading on the physis in approximately a squared relationship;

(5) hormonal factors — particularly the imbalance between sex steroids and growth hormone — affect the strength of the physeal cartilage. The classical hypothesis is that estrogen and testosterone strengthen the physis while growth hormone widens it; in patients with relative deficiency of sex steroids and normal growth hormone activity (hypogonadism, delayed puberty, obesity with lowered peripheral estrogen), the physis is simultaneously thicker and weaker.

The endocrine risk factors deserve specific mention because they have important clinical implications. SCFE is associated with hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency (including induced by recombinant growth hormone replacement — a well-documented complication), panhypopituitarism, hypogonadism, renal osteodystrophy (where SCFE is bilateral and often associated with severe deformity and resistant to conventional treatment), and radiation exposure of the pelvis.

The classical teaching is that any patient with SCFE outside the typical age range (younger than 10 or older than 16) should undergo endocrine evaluation; many practitioners now recommend endocrine screening (TSH, IGF-1, electrolytes including calcium and phosphate, renal function) for any SCFE patient given the prevalence of subclinical endocrine disease.

The mechanism is thought to be twofold: the underlying predilection from the endocrinopathy plus the accelerated physeal widening from the therapy. A low threshold for radiographic examination is recommended for knee or hip pain in a child on growth hormone therapy, particularly during the first 6–12 months of treatment.

## Classification

## Temporal Classification

The classical temporal classification recognizes three types based on the duration of symptoms. It remains clinically useful but has been surpassed prognostically by the Loder stability classification.

Acute SCFE: symptoms present for less than 3 weeks. The patient typically presents with sudden onset of severe hip or knee pain, often with a precipitating event (a fall, a trip, a sporting injury) and is unable to bear weight. The physeal injury is recent and the physis is still potentially capable of healing in a corrected position.

Chronic SCFE: symptoms present for more than 3 weeks. The patient typically reports a gradual onset of hip or knee pain over weeks to months, sometimes with antalgic gait and external rotation of the affected limb. The physis has been progressively displacing over time and may have begun to remodel.

Acute-on-chronic SCFE: a history of chronic symptoms of weeks to months, with a sudden worsening associated with an acute mechanical event. This combination is particularly common and represents the chronic remodeling of the physis with superimposed acute additional displacement.

## Stability Classification (Loder)

The Loder classification (1993), based on ability to weight-bear, has become the most important practical classification because it predicts the risk of avascular necrosis:

Stable SCFE: the patient can bear weight on the affected limb, with or without crutches. The risk of avascular necrosis (AVN) in Loder's original series and in subsequent meta-analyses is approximately 0–10%.

Unstable SCFE: the patient cannot bear weight on the affected limb even with crutches. The risk of avascular necrosis in the original series was 47%; in the newer meta-analyses by Zaltz and colleagues, it is 20–58%. This tenfold increase over the stable form makes stability the most consequential single clinical finding for prognosis.

The Loder classification has two important limitations the clinician must recognize. First, it is subjective — a patient with severe pain may refuse weight-bearing without the epiphysis being truly mechanically unstable. Second, a large prospective series by Sankar and Millis demonstrated that a fraction of "unstable" patients in fact have a stable physis at direct intraoperative assessment, and the converse. Objective intraoperative assessment of stability by gentle manipulation under fluoroscopy or direct vision is therefore increasingly sought.

## Radiographic Severity Classification

The Southwick classification (modified) grades the severity of the slip on the lateral radiograph by the head-shaft angle:

Mild: head-shaft angle less than 30° greater than the contralateral side.

Moderate: head-shaft angle 30–50° greater than the contralateral side.

Severe: head-shaft angle greater than 50° greater than the contralateral side.

The Wilson classification provides a similar grading based on the percentage of displacement of the epiphysis relative to the metaphysis: mild (<33%), moderate (33–50%), severe (>50%).

## Clinical Features

The classical presentation is of an obese adolescent with a limp and pain in the hip, groin, anterior thigh, or — characteristically — the knee. The phenomenon of referred knee pain from a hip lesion is one of the great teaching points of pediatric orthopedics: the obturator nerve innervates both the hip joint capsule and the medial knee, and pathology of the hip can produce isolated knee pain that completely conceals the underlying hip diagnosis.

The orthopedic teaching is therefore: "in any adolescent with knee pain, examine the hip; in any adolescent with hip pain, examine the knee." Failure to examine the hip in an adolescent with knee pain has been the cause of countless missed and delayed SCFE diagnoses, with the consequences of more severe slip, increased difficulty of treatment, and worse outcomes. The mean diagnostic delays of 4–5 months between first health-care visit and diagnosis, catalogued in the classical series by Kocher and Bishop, illustrate the scale of this problem.

The acute unstable SCFE presents with severe pain, inability to bear weight, and the limb held in obvious external rotation with shortening. The chronic stable SCFE presents with gradual onset of mild to moderate hip or knee pain, antalgic limp, decreased internal rotation of the hip, and the limb in slight external rotation at rest.

Physical examination reveals: decreased internal rotation of the hip (the most consistent and reliable finding); the classical Drehmann sign (when the affected hip is flexed, it goes into obligatory external rotation rather than the normal slight internal rotation — a pathognomonic finding when compared with the contralateral side); the limb in mild to severe external rotation at rest; apparent shortening of the limb; antalgic gait or, in unstable disease, inability to bear weight. The contralateral hip should be examined carefully, since bilateral involvement is common and contralateral SCFE may be present even when asymptomatic.

## Imaging

Plain radiographs are the standard initial imaging study, and the indispensable views are the AP pelvis and the frog-lateral (or true lateral) view of both hips. The AP pelvis alone is insufficient because the typical posterior slip of the epiphysis is best seen on the lateral view; many missed SCFEs reflect inadequate imaging rather than misinterpretation.

The classical AP-pelvis findings are subtle in early SCFE: a widened, irregular, "fuzzy" appearance of the proximal femoral physis (the so-called "fuzzy" physis of Steel); the Klein line (drawn along the superior border of the femoral neck) failing to intersect the lateral aspect of the epiphysis (which it should normally cross); the "metaphyseal blanch sign" of Steel (a crescent-shaped area of increased density at the metaphyseal-physeal junction, reflecting superimposition of the displaced epiphysis on distal metaphyseal remodeling); and a relatively short or "atrophic" appearance of the femoral head as it slips posteriorly.

An additional useful sign is the modified Klein-Trethowan sign: when compared with the contralateral side, a difference in the amount of epiphysis crossed by the Klein line of greater than 2 mm is considered positive. This quantitative criterion increases sensitivity from approximately 40% (classical qualitative Klein line) to over 80%.

The frog-lateral or true lateral view reveals the posterior slip of the epiphysis as a clear discontinuity between the femoral neck and the epiphysis, with the epiphysis tilted posteriorly. The Southwick angle (head-shaft angle — the angle between the long axis of the femoral neck and a line perpendicular to the line connecting the anterior and posterior aspects of the proximal femoral physis) is measured on this view and quantifies the severity of slip. The true lateral (cross-table lateral) is preferred by some authors over the frog-leg in acute unstable SCFE, where positioning in wide abduction may cause further displacement.

MRI may be useful in three specific situations: (1) early "pre-slip" SCFE, in which the physis shows widening and inflammatory changes but no obvious displacement on plain radiographs; (2) unstable SCFE, in which assessment of vascular compromise to the femoral head is required — absence of perfusion of the head on contrast-enhanced MRI has prognostic value for subsequent AVN; and (3) evaluation of "silent" contralateral SCFE in high-risk patients. CT may be used for three-dimensional surgical planning in severe deformity before modified Dunn or intertrochanteric osteotomy, but is rarely required for initial diagnosis.

## Principles of Treatment

The treatment of SCFE has several core principles:

(1) Immediate cessation of weight-bearing once SCFE is suspected, even before radiographs are obtained, to prevent further displacement. The classical injunction is: "the patient must leave the clinic on a stretcher or in a wheelchair, not walking."

(2) Prompt surgical fixation in all but the most minor cases, because non-surgical management has uniformly poor outcomes. Spica casting has been abandoned virtually everywhere, since it does not reliably prevent further slippage and is associated with a high rate of chondrolysis (historically up to 30% in casted patients).

(3) The goal of fixation in stable SCFE is to stabilize the epiphysis in its currently displaced position with minimal additional manipulation, since aggressive reduction maneuvers risk avascular necrosis. This concept — in situ fixation — is the founding principle of modern treatment.

(4) Recognition that bilateral involvement is common, with consideration of prophylactic contralateral fixation in selected high-risk patients.

(5) In unstable SCFE the decision is contested and depends on surgeon experience, time since symptom onset, and access to structured post-operative care: early in situ fixation with gentle positional reduction, or urgent modified Dunn with anatomic reduction via Ganz surgical hip dislocation.

## In Situ Fixation

In situ fixation with a single percutaneous cannulated screw is the standard treatment for stable SCFE of any severity. The principle is to fix the epiphysis to the metaphysis through the physis, achieving physeal closure and preventing further slip. The procedure is performed on a fracture table under image intensification.

The surgical steps are: positioning the patient on a fracture table with the affected limb in gentle internal rotation; identification of the "ideal" entry point with biplanar fluoroscopy (anterolateral on the proximal femur along the line perpendicular to the displaced physis in both planes — anterolateral on AP, anterior to a true lateral plane on the cross-table); insertion of a 3.2 mm guidewire from the anterolateral cortex of the proximal femur through the metaphysis and physis into the epiphysis, along an axis directed at the center of the displaced head; fluoroscopic confirmation of the position in both planes; drilling over the guidewire; placement of a 7.0 mm or 7.3 mm partially threaded cannulated screw; final confirmation that the screw engages at least 4–5 mm of epiphyseal bone, that all threads have crossed the physis (to permit closure), and that the tip of the screw has not penetrated the articular surface.

The classical test to exclude intra-articular penetration is Moseley's "approach-withdraw" technique: the fluoroscope is rotated through several projections while the screw tip is watched to approach and recede from the subchondral bone. If the screw tip remains discreetly separated from the subchondral bone in all projections, intra-articular penetration is excluded.

A single screw is the standard for most cases; some authors advocate two screws for severe slips or unstable disease, but single-screw fixation has the advantage of fewer complications, easier later removal, and is well-supported by the literature. Two-screw fixation carries the biomechanical advantage of greater rotational stability but also a higher risk of intra-articular penetration and chondrolysis.

<details>
<summary>The perpendicular axis — why direction is critical</summary>

The screw must follow an axis perpendicular to the physis to minimize shear loading and maximize purchase in the epiphysis. Because the physis is displaced posteriorly and medially relative to the metaphysis, the screw entry point shifts increasingly anterior on the femoral neck with greater severity of slip. In severe slips the entry point may be so anterior as to overlie the medial femoral circumflex artery — an anatomic reason to consider alternative treatment by modified Dunn in severe deformity.

## Management of Unstable SCFE

The unstable SCFE presents a particular dilemma. Gentle positional reduction occurs spontaneously when the patient is positioned on the fracture table and the limb is internally rotated; this gentle reduction is generally accepted and does not significantly increase AVN risk. Aggressive reduction maneuvers — forceful internal rotation, adduction, or extension to "reduce" the slip — are associated with high rates of avascular necrosis and are generally avoided. The current consensus under the in situ fixation paradigm is that whatever reduction is achieved by positioning is accepted, and fixation is performed in that position; further reduction is not pursued.

The timing of surgery in unstable SCFE remains debated. The classical hypothesis (the "vascular window") suggests that fixation within the first 24 hours of symptom onset improves outcome through decompression of intracapsular hematoma and restoration of perfusion. More recent meta-analyses by Zaltz and Lowe do not unambiguously confirm this effect; current consensus is that fixation should be performed as soon as possible at an experienced center without, however, risking inexperienced surgery at inappropriate hours.

A subset of authors advocates urgent open reduction of unstable SCFE through a modified Dunn procedure performed through the Ganz surgical hip dislocation approach, described in detail below. Their hypothesis is that anatomic reduction with preservation of the medial femoral circumflex artery provides both optimal biomechanics and direct decompression of the intracapsular hematoma. The Leunig and Ganz series (2007) of 30 patients reported 0% AVN — an exceptional result, until then unmatched by any series with conventional in situ fixation.

Subsequent series from other centers attempting to reproduce these results have reported higher rates of AVN (10–25%), reflecting a steep learning curve. The systematic review by Sankar and colleagues (2013) concluded that the modified Dunn is a reasonable choice for unstable SCFE in centers with appropriate expertise, but cannot be recommended as routine treatment for all centers.

## Modified Dunn and Ganz Surgical Dislocation

The surgical hip dislocation approach of Ganz (Ganz, Gill, Krügel, Beck, Bardakos, 2001) is the approach that made safe execution of anatomic subcapital reduction in SCFE possible. The procedure is a modified version of the original subcapital wedge osteotomy of Dunn (1964), whose initial series reported AVN rates above 30%. Ganz's modification preserves the medial femoral circumflex artery throughout the procedure and has lowered reported AVN risk to below 10% in expert centers.

The surgical steps of the modified Dunn via the Ganz approach:

1. Patient positioning in lateral decubitus with the hip in a central position; Kocher-Langenbeck-type approach with anterior reflection of gluteus maximus.

2. Identification and preservation of the deep branch of the MFCA along its course between gemellus inferior and obturator externus; trochanteric "flip" osteotomy with preservation of the insertions of gluteus medius proximally and vastus lateralis distally on the osteotomy fragment.

3. Z-shaped or C-shaped capsulotomy preserving the capsular perforators of the retinacular vessels. Dislocation of the femoral head into a safe position (flexion, external rotation, adduction).

4. Identification of the retinacular flap — the mobile subsynovial slip from the posterosuperior capsule carrying the vessels of Weitbrecht — and its disciplined preservation throughout the rest of the procedure. This flap is elevated from the metaphysis as a biological pedicle and the vessels are verified by laser-Doppler or direct assessment.

5. Exposure of the slip. Resection of the posterosuperior metaphyseal spur (the "callar collar" or "metaphyseal beak"), which is often a source of cam impingement and cannot remodel spontaneously.

6. Subcapital osteotomy through the original physeal scar. Anatomic reduction of the epiphysis onto the resected and trimmed metaphyseal end.

7. Fixation with two or three headless cannulated screws (typically 4.5 mm), inserted through the lateral trochanteric ridge across the metaphysis into the epiphysis. Stability is verified by gentle rotation.

8. Reconstruction: reduction of the trochanteric fragment with two 4.5 mm screws; closure of the capsule with resorbable sutures.

Recommended postoperative care: complete protected weight-bearing for 6–8 weeks, followed by progressive weight-bearing with crutches for a further 6 weeks. First follow-up imaging with MRI for assessment of perfusion at 3 months.

Even in the most experienced centers the modified Dunn is not the routine treatment for all SCFE. Relative contraindications include: a patient with prior fixation (typically an adult with a missed chronic SCFE and established arthritic deformity); a patient with active AVN on preoperative MRI (the procedure does not reverse established necrosis); a patient with a mild deformity (Southwick <30°) in whom the benefit of surgical dislocation does not justify the risk; and a center without sufficient experience with the Ganz surgical dislocation.

## Prophylactic Contralateral Fixation

The decision to fix the contralateral asymptomatic hip prophylactically remains debated. Arguments in favor include: a 40–60% rate of bilateral involvement (often within 18–24 months); the risk of missing a developing contralateral slip in asymptomatic patients; the morbidity of a future second operation; and the not-infrequent failure of families to comply with repeat clinic visits. Arguments against include: unnecessary surgery in the majority of patients who will not develop a contralateral slip; the small but real complication rate of the prophylactic procedure (AVN under 1%, chondrolysis under 2%); and potential interference with later imaging.

Current consensus is that prophylactic fixation should be considered in patients at high risk of contralateral slip:

- patients with endocrine disorders (hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, renal osteodystrophy);
- patients younger than typical (in whom more residual growth means more time for contralateral slip);
- patients with a modified Oxford skeletal maturity score of 16 or below;
- patients with previous evidence of contralateral physeal change on imaging;
- patients in whom compliance with follow-up is uncertain (geographic, social, or family circumstances).

The Loder modified Oxford skeletal maturity score is one tool used to predict the residual risk and is currently the best-validated in the literature.

## Severe Deformity Correction

Severe chronic SCFE with established deformity may require corrective osteotomy to address the persistent deformity and to treat secondary femoroacetabular impingement (FAI). Three principal levels of osteotomy have been described, each with different risk-benefit profiles:

Subcapital osteotomy (modified Dunn / Ganz approach): osteotomy at the level of the slip, providing the most complete deformity correction but with the highest historical risk of avascular necrosis. The procedure is now performed principally through the surgical hip dislocation approach (Ganz approach) with preservation of the medial femoral circumflex artery, which has substantially reduced but not eliminated the AVN risk (typically 5–10% in expert hands versus the historical 30%).

Femoral neck base osteotomy (Kramer or Barmada osteotomy): osteotomy at the base of the femoral neck, an extra-articular location, with intermediate correction (typically 30–35° maximum) and intermediate AVN risk (around 2–5%). The advantage is greater technical feasibility; the disadvantage is incomplete correction in severe deformities.

Intertrochanteric osteotomy (Imhauser, Southwick, or biplanar osteotomies): osteotomy in the intertrochanteric region, providing extra-articular correction without disturbing the femoral neck vascularity. The AVN risk is the lowest of the osteotomy options (under 1%), but the correction is incomplete and the patient is left with persistent intra-articular deformity that contributes to ongoing femoroacetabular impingement. The classical indication today is the adult patient with a missed or previously treated SCFE in whom subcapital reduction is no longer possible.

The choice between these osteotomies depends on the severity of deformity, the patient's symptoms, the surgeon's experience, and the assessment of risk-benefit for the individual patient. An increasing number of centers offer combined procedures: an Imhauser intertrochanteric osteotomy plus arthroscopic or open osteochondroplasty of the head-neck junction to address the cam impingement.

## Complications

## Avascular Necrosis (AVN)

Avascular necrosis is the most consequential complication of SCFE, with overall rates of approximately 5–10%. The risk is substantially higher in unstable SCFE (approximately 20–50% depending on the series), with aggressive reduction attempts, with subcapital osteotomy, and with delayed treatment.

The pathogenesis is disruption of the medial femoral circumflex artery supply to the femoral head, either by the original slip event (in unstable disease via intracapsular tamponade or direct retinacular vessel injury) or by surgical manipulation (aggressive reduction, malpositioned screw, faulty subcapital osteotomy technique).

AVN presents within 6–24 months of the index event with progressive hip pain, decreased range of motion, and characteristic radiographic changes of femoral head sclerosis, fragmentation, and collapse. Staging follows the Ficat-Arlet or ARCO system, analogous to adult femoral head AVN.

Treatment options depend on stage and patient age: in early disease without collapse — core decompression, often combined with biological adjuncts (bone marrow concentrate, BMP, vascularized fibular graft); in collapsed disease in a young patient — consideration of Ganz surgical dislocation with head remodeling and possible mosaicplasty; in end-stage disease — total hip arthroplasty, delayed as long as possible because of the young age of this population.

## Chondrolysis

Chondrolysis — destruction of the articular cartilage of the hip with severe stiffness and pain — was historically a major complication of SCFE, with rates as high as 30% in some series, particularly with spica casting. The pathogenesis is uncertain but is thought to involve a combination of mechanical factors (intra-articular screw penetration, persistent intra-articular deformity), autoimmune factors, and possibly racial factors (chondrolysis was historically more common in African American patients).

Modern attention to avoiding intra-articular screw penetration (via the approach-withdraw technique), the move toward a single centrally placed screw, and the abandonment of spica casting have markedly reduced chondrolysis rates to approximately 1–7% in current series.

Clinical presentation is with progressive hip stiffness over the months following surgery, with reduction in all rotations, pain on motion, and characteristic narrowing of the joint space on plain radiographs. MRI shows articular cartilage fragmentation, bone marrow edema, and often synovitis.

Initial treatment is conservative: NSAIDs, gentle mobilization physiotherapy, protected weight-bearing, and possibly intra-articular corticosteroid injection. Surgical treatment with subcapsular decompression or with total hip arthroplasty is reserved for cases with persistent severe pain.

## Femoroacetabular Impingement (FAI)

Femoroacetabular impingement is now recognized as a near-universal late consequence of SCFE, even in adequately fixed disease. The displaced femoral head and metaphyseal spur produce cam-type impingement against the acetabular rim with hip flexion and internal rotation, producing pain, decreased range of motion, and progressive labral and cartilage damage.

The classical imaging features of post-SCFE cam impingement are: a Nötzli alpha angle (measured on oblique axial MRI sections through the femoral neck) exceeding 50–55°; a widened and irregular anterosuperior metaphyseal spur ("pistol grip" deformity); and associated labral lesions and chondral damage, particularly of the acetabulum.

Treatment options include:

- arthroscopic osteochondroplasty of the head-neck junction with resection of the cam deformity plus labral repair if torn. Suitable for mild to moderate residual deformity (Southwick <30°);
- open surgical hip dislocation by the Ganz approach with osteochondroplasty — the standard for more severe deformity;
- intertrochanteric Imhauser osteotomy with or without separate osteochondroplasty — for deformities where remodeling of the proximal femur alone is insufficient;
- modified Dunn as a delayed corrective procedure in young patients with severe deformity and relatively preserved cartilage.

## Other Complications

Other recognized complications include:

- progressive slip after fixation (rare with adequate single-screw fixation; described predominantly in patients with endocrine disorders in whom the physis remains open despite fixation);
- nonunion of the physis (uncommon; described in renal osteodystrophy);
- implant-related problems (screw breakage, migration, pain at removal);
- leg-length discrepancy (typically 1–2 cm in moderate SCFE; greater in severe cases);
- persistent external rotation of the limb during gait;
- post-SCFE arthritis in adulthood.

## Long-term Outcomes

The long-term outcome of SCFE is one of progressive risk of degenerative arthritis. The classical long-term follow-up series of Carney, Weinstein, and Ponseti (40+ years of follow-up) demonstrate a clear gradient: patients with mild SCFE treated by in situ fixation have excellent arthroplasty-free survival (about 75% at 40 years); patients with moderate SCFE about 50%; patients with severe SCFE under 30%. Overall about 10–30% of patients require total hip arthroplasty by middle age.

The two principal mechanisms of degeneration are:

(1) Established AVN with head collapse, leading to rapid arthrosis in the first 5–15 years after treatment;

(2) Cam-type femoroacetabular impingement from the residual deformity, leading to slow progressive arthrosis over decades. This second group is particularly important, since it represents the patients in whom an earlier corrective procedure (arthroscopic or open osteochondroplasty) may delay or prevent arthroplasty.

Functional outcomes (Harris Hip Score, WOMAC, iHOT-33) in adequately treated mild or moderate SCFE are very good in the first 10–20 years after treatment but begin to deteriorate thereafter, reflecting the developing arthrosis.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis is one of the most important diagnoses in adolescent orthopedics, with the typical patient being an obese adolescent male in late puberty presenting with hip or knee pain. The diagnosis is established by clinical examination (with the cardinal finding of decreased internal rotation and the Drehmann sign) and by plain radiographs (AP pelvis and frog-lateral, with attention to the Klein line, the metaphyseal blanch sign, and the lateral-view discontinuity).

The Loder classification of stable versus unstable SCFE, based on the ability to weight-bear, is the most important prognostic indicator: stable disease has approximately 0–10% AVN risk; unstable disease, 20–50% AVN risk. Treatment is urgent surgical fixation with a single percutaneous cannulated screw in situ for the great majority of cases; gentle positional reduction is acceptable for unstable SCFE, while aggressive reduction maneuvers are avoided.

The modified Dunn via Ganz surgical dislocation is an alternative approach for unstable SCFE and for severe chronic deformity, with promising but still variable results depending on center experience. Severe deformity may require corrective osteotomy (modified Dunn / Ganz, base of neck, or intertrochanteric), each with different risk profiles.

The cardinal teaching points are: examine the hip in any adolescent with knee pain; image both hips with AP and lateral views; do not delay diagnosis or surgical fixation; consider endocrine workup in atypical presentations; recognize and manage the late complications of AVN, chondrolysis, and femoroacetabular impingement. The single greatest improvement in SCFE care has been the recognition that prompt diagnosis and stabilization is the principal determinant of long-term outcome.

## Local Contributions

Treatment of SCFE in Central and Eastern Europe reflects the broader European school, with accents from the German orthopedic tradition (Imhauser, Schmidt, Dietz) and from the Bernese repertoire of Switzerland (Ganz, Leunig, Siebenrock). The contributions selected below are ordered by country in alphabetical order.

Bulgaria: Bulgarian pediatric orthopedics follows the leading European (predominantly German and Swiss) schools in the treatment of SCFE, with in situ fixation by a single cannulated screw as the standard procedure for stable disease. Spiridon Boychev and his successors at the Department of Pediatric Orthopedics of the Medical University — Sofia have adopted the classical screw fixation approach with consideration of prophylactic contralateral fixation in endocrinopathies. The sub-specialty of reconstructive young-adult hip surgery follows the modern principles of surgical hip dislocation and FAI correction, but the modified Dunn remains restricted to individual specialized centers.

Germany: the German school has produced multiple landmark contributions. Drehmann described the pathognomonic sign of obligatory external rotation on flexion as early as the beginning of the twentieth century. Imhauser (Cologne, 1960s–1970s) developed and popularized the triplane intertrochanteric osteotomy for late correction of missed SCFE — an operation that remains a standard tool in the modern repertoire. Tönnis (Dortmund) presented in his classical textbook *Congenital Dysplasia and Dislocation of the Hip in Children* (1987) a comparative analysis of pediatric hip osteotomies, including the post-SCFE deformity.

Italy: the series of Marcheggiani Muccioli, Zaffagnini, and colleagues at the Rizzoli Institute, Bologna, on long-term follow-up of patients with SCFE treated by Imhauser intertrochanteric osteotomy remains one of the most cited in Europe. The Italian school favors a more conservative approach in mild and moderate deformities, with intertrochanteric correction in preference to early subcapital surgery.

Poland: Kryczewski and colleagues at Warsaw have reported long-term results of screw fixation in SCFE, with emphasis on the importance of prophylactic contralateral fixation in patients with a confirmed endocrinopathy.

Romania: the Romanian school follows the Central European model with screw fixation and intertrochanteric correction in later presentations. The sub-specialty of hip preservation in several university hospitals in Bucharest and Cluj has developed experience with the arthroscopic treatment of post-SCFE FAI.

Serbia: the Serbian orthopedic community, particularly at the Institute of Orthopedic Surgical Diseases "Banja Koviljača" and at the Clinical Center of Serbia, applies in situ screw fixation as standard, with referral of complex cases to the Belgrade university center for consideration of the modified Dunn.

Turkey: the Turkish orthopedic school has been particularly active in publication on the modern treatment of SCFE. The series of Aksoy, Yilgor, and colleagues at Hacettepe (Ankara) presents comparative data between in situ fixation and the modified Dunn in unstable SCFE, with emphasis on the importance of early (under 24 hours) surgical treatment. Turkish centers were among the first in the region to adopt routine arthroscopic osteochondroplasty for post-SCFE FAI.

Switzerland: the Swiss contribution has been central to modern SCFE surgery. Ganz (Reinhold Ganz, Bern) developed the surgical hip dislocation approach (Ganz, Gill, Krügel, Beck, Bardakos 2001) and together with Leunig and Siebenrock described the modified Dunn as a safe alternative for unstable SCFE. The Bernese school remains the world reference for the surgical hip dislocation and for all variants of its applications — post-SCFE correction, FAI treatment, mosaicplasty for early AVN.
