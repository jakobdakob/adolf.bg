## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Benign bone tumors are clonal neoplastic or hamartomatous proliferations of bone-derived or matrix-producing cells that, in contrast to malignant lesions, demonstrate limited growth potential, no metastatic capacity, and (in most cases) predictable natural histories. This chapter covers the clinical, imaging, and pathological features of the major entities — osteoid osteoma and osteoblastoma, osteochondroma and hereditary multiple exostoses, enchondroma and the enchondromatoses (Ollier and Maffucci), chondroblastoma, chondromyxoid fibroma, giant cell tumor of bone, non-ossifying fibroma, intraosseous hemangioma, and the rare entities of intraosseous lipoma, schwannoma, and the low-grade malignant adamantinoma — alongside the surgical principles of Enneking and modern adjuvants (denosumab, percutaneous radiofrequency ablation, selective arterial embolization). The Bulgarian orthopaedic tradition, represented by Boychev's school and contemporary authors, remains part of the national curriculum in skeletal oncology.

</div>
</details>
</section>

<summary>Classification and Biology</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### WHO Grouping
<details id="who-grouping">
<summary>WHO Grouping</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The World Health Organization classification (4th edition 2013, updated 2020) groups bone tumors by predominant matrix or cell of origin. Benign and intermediate (locally aggressive or rarely metastasizing) lesions fall into six principal categories. **Chondrogenic** includes the benign osteochondroma, enchondroma, periosteal chondroma, osteochondromyxoma and subungual exostosis; the intermediate locally aggressive — chondromyxoid fibroma and atypical cartilaginous tumour (synonymous with grade 1 chondrosarcoma); the intermediate rarely metastasizing — chondroblastoma. **Osteogenic** includes the benign osteoma, osteoid osteoma and osteoblastoma. **Fibrogenic** includes the intermediate aggressive desmoplastic fibroma of bone. **Fibrohistiocytic** includes the benign fibrous histiocytoma and non-ossifying fibroma (often considered a single entity with it). **Osteoclastic giant-cell-rich lesions** include giant cell tumour (intermediate, rarely metastasizing), aneurysmal bone cyst (now reframed as a neoplasm with USP6 rearrangements) and the giant cell lesion of the small bones. **Vascular, notochordal and miscellaneous** — haemangioma, glomangioma, schwannoma, intraosseous lipoma.

A complementary biological framework — Enneking's grading (1986) — orders benign lesions as latent, active or aggressive, providing the direct conceptual link between lesion natural history and surgical strategy.

</div>
</details>


### Enneking Staging
<details id="enneking-staging">
<summary>Enneking Staging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Musculoskeletal Tumor Society / Enneking biological staging of benign bone tumours is based on natural history and radiographic features, not on histological aggressiveness in the malignant sense.

**Stage 1 (latent).** Asymptomatic, well-circumscribed lesion with a mature bony rim; does not expand the bone, does not breach the cortex. Radiographically, a geographic lesion with a narrow zone of transition and dense sclerotic margin. Examples: incidentally noted solitary osteochondroma in an adult, small enchondroma of the proximal humerus, "polka-dot" vertebral haemangioma. Surgical strategy: observation with serial imaging; excision only for symptom-specific reasons.

**Stage 2 (active).** Lesion grows, expands the bone, thins the cortex but is contained by a thin reactive periosteal shell and is usually symptomatic. The zone of transition is wider than stage 1, but the border remains sharp. Examples: enchondroma of the hand with pathological fracture, chondroblastoma, chondromyxoid fibroma, most giant cell tumours at presentation. Surgical strategy: intralesional curettage with cancellous bone grafting.

**Stage 3 (aggressive).** Lesion breaches the cortex, extends into surrounding soft tissue, has no clear reactive rim, with a wide and ill-defined zone of transition. Examples: Campanacci III giant cell tumour, aggressive osteoblastoma with soft-tissue extension, active haemangiomas with neural compression. Surgical strategy: extended intralesional curettage with high-speed burring and adjuvant (phenol, cement, cryotherapy) or wide en-bloc resection in expendable bones.

The Campanacci radiographic grading (I–III), used predominantly for giant cell tumour and some cartilaginous lesions, correlates with growth pattern, local recurrence risk and surgical recommendation, and is commonly used alongside Enneking.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Stage 1 — observation; stage 2 — curettage and bone grafting; stage 3 — extended curettage with adjuvant or wide resection.</li>
    <li>Stage 2 and stage 3 GCT require high-speed burring (removes the microscopic tumour fringe in trabecular bone) and a local adjuvant (cement, phenol, cryotherapy).</li>
    <li>Campanacci I–III is a radiographic grading used predominantly for GCT — applied alongside Enneking, not as a replacement.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Molecular Biology
<details id="molecular">
<summary>Molecular Biology</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The molecular characterization of benign bone tumours has transformed classification and, in several entities, treatment. **Giant cell tumour** carries an activating H3F3A G34W (or less commonly G34V) mutation in the mononuclear stromal cells — the actual neoplastic component, interspersed with reactive osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells. The stromal cells overexpress RANKL, which recruits osteoclasts and drives the osteolytic process; this is the molecular rationale for denosumab, a monoclonal antibody against RANKL. **Chondroblastoma** carries the characteristic H3F3B K36M mutation (less commonly H3F3A), useful for histological distinction from radiographically similar adult-onset lesions. **Osteoid osteoma and osteoblastoma** share FOS and less commonly FOSB rearrangements (de novo t(2;17) and similar translocations), supporting their classification as a single neoplastic spectrum, with nidus size as the main biological and clinical discriminator. **Chondromyxoid fibroma** shows GRM1 rearrangements. **Hereditary multiple exostoses** are caused by mutations in EXT1 (8q24), EXT2 (11p11), or rarely EXT3 — genes encoding glycosyltransferases that synthesise heparan sulphate proteoglycans in the cartilaginous matrix; EXT1 mutations carry a higher risk of chondrosarcoma transformation than EXT2.

**Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome** are associated with post-zygotic mosaic mutations in IDH1 (R132C most commonly) or IDH2; the same mutations occur in central chondrosarcomas, explaining the high transformation risk (25–30% in Ollier, approaching 100% in Maffucci) and the concomitant non-skeletal malignancies (ovarian and pancreatic cancers, brain gliomas).

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>GCT: H3F3A G34W/G34V (stromal cells), RANKL — denosumab rationale. Chondroblastoma: H3F3B K36M. Osteoid osteoma and osteoblastoma: FOS/FOSB rearrangements, single spectrum. HME: EXT1 (higher risk) or EXT2. Ollier/Maffucci: IDH1/IDH2 mosaicism.</p>
</aside>

## Imaging and Workup
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="imaging">
<details>
<summary>Imaging and Workup</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Systematic plain radiographic assessment of a bone lesion remains the foundation of imaging diagnosis and prevents a substantial portion of unnecessary biopsies. **Location within the bone** — epiphyseal (chondroblastoma, GCT in skeletally mature, some Brodie abscesses), metaphyseal (the vast majority of benign and malignant tumours), diaphyseal (Ewing sarcoma, lymphoma, fibrous dysplasia, adamantinoma) or subarticular — gives the first strong differential clue. **Location within the cross-section** — central, eccentric, cortical, parosteal or surface — adds the second. **Zone of transition and margins** with host bone — narrow and sclerotic (latent or active benign tumour), wide permeative (aggressive or malignant lesion), geographic with narrow zone (most active benign tumours, with GCT as the leading example) — correlate with biological aggressiveness. **Matrix mineralisation** — cartilage matrix ("rings and arcs", "popcorn") distinguishes chondrogenic from osteogenic tumours ("cloudy" mineralisation in osteosarcoma, dense central nucleus in osteoid osteoma). **Periosteal reaction** — single solid layer (chronic or mature process), solid periosteal bone (benign reaction), Codman triangle, "sunburst" or "onion-skin" patterns (malignant).

**CT** is gold standard for visualizing the nidus in osteoid osteoma, for assessing matrix mineralisation, cortical destruction and cortical thinning. **MRI** is the modality of choice for soft-tissue extension, marrow oedema (typically intense and out of proportion to nidus size in osteoid osteoma), subchondral involvement, and for distinguishing the cartilage cap from subcutaneous tissue in osteochondroma (a T2-high-signal cap exceeding 1–2 cm in adulthood is a warning sign of chondrosarcoma). **Bone scan** is intensely positive in osteoid osteoma ("headlight in the fog" — central focus within a wider halo), chondroblastoma and active GCT; useful for detecting multiple lesions (HME, Ollier, Langerhans cell histiocytosis). **18F-FDG PET-CT** has a limited role in primary benign tumour assessment but is helpful when malignant transformation is suspected in HME or Ollier (increased uptake in a previously stable lesion) and for metastasis detection in GCT.

**Biopsy.** Performed according to Enneking principles — needle biopsy under image guidance (CT, ultrasound), or open along the line of the future definitive resection so the biopsy tract can be resected. Cardinal rules: one-sided approach, minimal soft-tissue dissection, haemostasis with bipolar cautery, no transgression of adjacent compartments, and always complete imaging assessment before biopsy — otherwise post-biopsy oedema and haemorrhage can mask the lesion on MRI.

<div class="ts-body">


### Osteoid Osteoma
<details id="osteoid-osteoma">
<summary>Osteoid Osteoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Osteoid osteoma is a small, benign, bone-forming tumour characterised by a central vascular nidus of osteoid and woven bone surrounded by a halo of reactive sclerosis. It accounts for approximately 10–12% of benign bone tumours. Peak incidence is in the second decade, with a male predilection of roughly 2–3:1. Histologically the nidus consists of interlacing trabeculae of woven bone and osteoid lined by plump osteoblasts within a richly vascular, loose connective tissue stroma; the surrounding reactive bone is dense lamellar bone. The nidus rarely exceeds 1.5 cm — a critical size cut-off, since lesions larger than this are by definition osteoblastomas. Molecular work demonstrates FOS and less commonly FOSB rearrangements in both entities, supporting their classification as a single neoplastic spectrum.

**Clinical features.** The cardinal symptom is pain — characteristically nocturnal, progressively severe, and dramatically relieved by aspirin or other NSAIDs. This response is so consistent as to be considered virtually diagnostic, and it reflects the high intra-nidal concentration of prostaglandins (particularly PGE2) which mediate both the pain and the surrounding vascular and bony reaction. The lesion has a strong predilection for the diaphysis and metaphysis of long bones, most commonly the proximal femur, tibia, fibula and humerus. Spinal lesions (≈10% of cases) sit in the posterior elements of the lumbar spine and are an important cause of painful scoliosis in children and adolescents — the curve is concave on the side of the lesion, the tumour sits at or near the apex, and the curve is at least partially reversible if the lesion is treated before structural change. Intra-articular osteoid osteomas (hip, elbow) can produce synovitis with painful effusion mimicking inflammatory arthritis. In children, long-standing lesions can produce lengthening and bowing of the long bone.

**Imaging.** Plain radiographs — small radiolucent nidus surrounded by dense reactive sclerosis, often intracortical or adjacent to the cortex. In cortical lesions the sclerosis may obscure the nidus; in cancellous or intra-articular locations sclerosis is milder. **Thin-section CT (1 mm)** is gold standard — identifies the nidus as a well-defined round or oval lucent area, sometimes with central mineralisation (Jaffe "annular sequestrum"), surrounded by sclerotic bone. **MRI** often shows extensive marrow and soft-tissue oedema disproportionate to nidus size, which can mislead. **Bone scan** is invariably hot with the characteristic "double density" sign — a central focus within a wider halo ("headlight in the fog") — useful when initial imaging is non-diagnostic.

**Treatment.** Natural history studies show the lesion "burns out" over several years, but the severity of pain makes conservative management with chronic NSAID therapy unattractive. The classical surgical option — en-bloc excision or wide curettage of the nidus — is curative but at the cost of substantial cortical bone removal and the need for internal fixation in many long-bone cases. Since the mid-1990s **percutaneous radiofrequency ablation under CT guidance** has become the treatment of choice for accessible lesions outside the spinal canal and major nerves. Under general or regional anaesthesia, a radiofrequency probe is advanced to the centre of the nidus and heated to 90 °C for around 6 minutes, producing complete coagulative necrosis. Success exceeds 90% with minimal morbidity, no postoperative immobilisation, and same-day discharge. **Contraindications:** lesions within 1 cm of the spinal cord or major nerves and lesions in subcutaneous bones (digits) where thermal injury to overlying skin is a concern; open excision remains the standard in these cases. For spinal osteoid osteomas causing painful scoliosis, removing the nidus in early childhood typically produces spontaneous correction of the curve within 12–18 months if treated before structural vertebral remodelling.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Nocturnal pain dramatically relieved by aspirin or NSAIDs is virtually diagnostic of osteoid osteoma — mediated by high intra-nidal PGE2. Nidus size 1.5 cm is the hard cut-off: smaller — osteoid osteoma; larger — osteoblastoma. The two share FOS rearrangements.</p>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Percutaneous CT-guided radiofrequency ablation at 90 °C, 6 minutes — treatment of choice for accessible lesions; success above 90%.</li>
    <li>RFA contraindications: lesions within 1 cm of cord or major nerves; lesions in subcutaneous bones (digits).</li>
    <li>Spinal osteoid osteoma with painful scoliosis — early removal of the nidus produces spontaneous curve correction if treated before structural remodelling.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Osteoblastoma
<details id="osteoblastoma">
<summary>Osteoblastoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Osteoblastoma is a benign bone-forming tumour that resembles osteoid osteoma histologically (sharing FOS/FOSB rearrangements) but differs in three important respects: it is larger (>1.5–2 cm), it lacks the surrounding intense reactive sclerosis, and it is more prone to aggressive behaviour with local recurrence. Half of all osteoblastomas occur in the spine, predominantly in the posterior elements (laminae, articular processes, pedicles); about a quarter occur in long bones (predominantly femur and tibia); the remainder in flat bones and pelvis. Age distribution is similar to osteoid osteoma (second and third decades), with a male predominance around 2:1.

**Clinical features and imaging.** Dull aching pain that, in contrast to osteoid osteoma, is less consistently relieved by NSAIDs and less commonly nocturnal. Spinal osteoblastomas can produce nerve-root compression, painful scoliosis, or (in large lesions) myelopathy. Radiographically — a relatively well-defined expansile lytic lesion, sometimes with internal bone-matrix mineralisation and "blow-out" expansion of the cortex with a thin periosteal shell. In aggressive forms the borders blur and the lesion can mimic a malignant process. CT is critical for assessing matrix radiodensity and distinguishing from aneurysmal bone cyst (which frequently coexists, particularly in the spine). MRI shows intense peritumoral soft-tissue oedema. Bone scan is markedly positive.

**Differential diagnosis.** Osteoid osteoma (smaller, more sclerotic), aneurysmal bone cyst (often arises within osteoblastomas or mimics them), Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and most critically the aggressive osteoblastoma-like osteosarcoma — a variant of conventional osteosarcoma that can histologically resemble osteoblastoma and demands expert pathology review on any case with worrying radiographic features.

**Treatment.** Extended intralesional curettage with high-speed burring and adjuvant therapy (phenol, polymethylmethacrylate cement, cryotherapy) is the standard, with reported recurrence rates of 10–20%. En-bloc resection is reserved for stage 3 aggressive lesions, lesions with significant soft-tissue extension, recurrent disease in expendable bones, and large spinal lesions with substantial invasion. **Preoperative selective arterial embolization** is helpful for reducing intraoperative blood loss in large spinal or pelvic lesions — osteoblastoma is characteristically highly vascular.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Aggressive osteoblastoma-like osteosarcoma (a conventional osteosarcoma variant) histologically resembles osteoblastoma — expert pathology review is mandatory in any case with concerning aggressive features.</li>
    <li>Aneurysmal bone cyst often arises within spinal osteoblastomas or mimics them — preoperative CT matrix assessment and consideration of embolization before biopsy.</li>
    <li>Without high-speed burring and adjuvant, simple curettage of osteoblastoma yields local recurrence over 30%.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Osteoma and Bone Islands
<details id="osteoma">
<summary>Osteoma and Bone Islands</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Osteoma is a dense, well-circumscribed bony outgrowth from the cortical surface, most commonly of the skull, facial bones, and paranasal sinuses (where it can produce sinus obstruction and mucocele). Histologically — compact lamellar bone without features of aggressive growth. It sits at the most benign end of the "osteoma–osteosarcoma" spectrum. **Gardner syndrome** (familial adenomatous polyposis + osteomas + fibromatoses + epidermoid cysts) mandates colorectal screening in a patient with multiple facial osteomas.

**Bone island (enostosis).** Incidentally noted dense intramedullary sclerotic focus, usually in the epiphysis or metaphysis of long bones or in the pelvis. Size up to 1–2 cm. Spiculated "brush border" margins blending into surrounding trabecular bone distinguish enostosis from sclerotic metastasis or osteoblastic lesion. Management — observation; biopsy reserved for growing lesions or those with a "hot" bone scan, which might represent osteoblastic metastasis.

</div>
</details>

### Osteochondroma (Exostosis)
<details id="osteochondroma">
<summary>Osteochondroma (Exostosis)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The solitary osteochondroma (osteocartilaginous exostosis) is the commonest benign bone tumour, accounting for 35–50% of all benign bone tumours and arising in approximately 1–2% of the general population (Campanacci notes that, in surgical specimens, only non-ossifying fibroma is more common). It is best understood as a developmental hamartoma: a fragment of physeal cartilage herniates beneath the periosteum through the perichondrial ring of LaCroix during skeletal growth, gives rise to a bony outgrowth, and grows by enchondral ossification at its cartilaginous cap. **Growth ceases at skeletal maturity** — continued growth in adulthood or new pain in a previously asymptomatic lesion must raise concern for secondary chondrosarcoma.

**Radiographic hallmark.** Cortical continuity between osteochondroma and parent bone and continuity of the medullary cavity — the pathognomonic feature distinguishing osteochondroma from any surface neoplasm. The lesion arises in the metaphysis of any long bone formed by enchondral ossification; with growth it migrates towards the diaphysis. Strong predilection for the distal femur, proximal humerus, proximal tibia and proximal fibula. Does not occur in bones formed by membranous ossification (skull) nor in epiphyses, carpal and tarsal bones (except the calcaneus). Two morphological subtypes: **pedunculated** (stalked, "mushroom" or "cauliflower"), classically pointing away from the adjacent joint; and **sessile** (broad-based), typical for the medial cortex of the proximal humerus. The cartilage cap is 1–10 mm or more in children (akin to physeal cartilage); in adults it is thinner and often discontinuous. **A cap greater than 1–2 cm in adulthood is a worrisome feature** for chondrosarcoma, most reliably assessed by T2-high-signal MRI.

**Clinical features.** Most osteochondromas are asymptomatic, discovered incidentally. Symptomatic lesions may produce:
- a painless or painful palpable bony mass;
- mechanical impingement on tendons or neurovascular structures — especially in the popliteal fossa with **popliteal artery pseudoaneurysm risk** (Campanacci 1968 describes the phenomenon in detail);
- bursitis overlying the cap (acute swelling with pain, easily misdiagnosed as malignant transformation);
- fracture through the stalk of a pedunculated lesion;
- snapping scapula in subscapular exostoses;
- neurovascular compression in spinal locations (exceptional).

**Treatment.** Asymptomatic lesions — observation. Indications for surgical excision: persistent pain, mechanical or neurovascular impingement, cosmetic concern, fracture, and any concern for malignant transformation. Excision must include **the bony stalk, the entire cartilaginous cap, and the overlying perichondrium** — piecemeal removal leaving cartilaginous remnants predictably leads to local recurrence. In children, with their higher recurrence risk and complex anatomic relationships (axilla, hip, popliteal fossa), excision is typically delayed until late puberty unless aggressive growth dictates earlier intervention. Lifetime risk of malignant transformation in solitary disease is quoted at about 1% (lower in distal extremities, higher in spine, pelvis and proximal limbs).

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>The osteochondroma is continuous with the medullary cavity of the parent bone — the radiographic hallmark separating it from every surface neoplasm. Continued growth after physeal closure or new pain in an adult — suspect secondary chondrosarcoma (risk ~1% in solitary disease, up to 10% in HME, higher with EXT1).</p>
</aside>

### Hereditary Multiple Exostoses
<details id="hme">
<summary>Hereditary Multiple Exostoses</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-2/hme-knee.jpg" data-figure-alt="Multiple osteochondromas around the knee in HME" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/ortho-2/hme-knee.jpg" alt="Multiple osteochondromas around the knee in HME" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Hereditary multiple exostoses: multiple metaphyseal osteochondromas around the knee, typically with secondary deformity of the limb axis.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Bovée JV., <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Multiple_osteochondromas_around_the_knee.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY 2.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Hereditary multiple exostoses (HME), also known as multiple hereditary osteochondromatosis or **diaphyseal aclasis**, is an autosomal-dominant disorder — approximately ten times rarer than the solitary form. Mutations in **EXT1 on 8q24**, **EXT2 on 11p11**, or rarely EXT3 — genes encoding glycosyltransferases that synthesise heparan sulphate proteoglycans in the cartilaginous matrix. About two-thirds of cases have a family history. Manifests before age 10 with multiple palpable bony lumps, more symmetric in distribution but usually asymmetric in size.

**Skeletal phenotype.** Multiple metaphyseal exostoses — around the knee, shoulder, ankle, wrist, hip and elbow in decreasing order. Circumferential metaphyseal involvement disturbs normal modelling and tubulation (the metaphyses remain wide and deformed). Frequently associated with:
- **short stature** (not due to a generalized growth-plate defect, but to dispersal of growth potential into the exostoses);
- **limb-length discrepancy** (usually under 2 cm);
- **forearm deformity** — short ulna with bowing of the radius, tilting and tapering of the distal radial epiphysis towards the ulna, dislocation of the radial head, ulnar deviation and a Madelung-like wrist;
- **ankle valgus and varus** — from a relatively short fibula with fibular bowing and tibiofibular diastasis;
- **genu valgum** of tibial origin — corrected by hemiepiphysiodesis or valgus-correcting osteotomy.

**Risk of chondrosarcoma transformation** is significantly higher than in solitary disease — quoted variably between 1% and 10% over a lifetime, with **EXT1 mutations carrying a higher risk** than EXT2. Preferential sites: pelvis, shoulder girdle, and knee area. Lifelong surveillance with clinical examination, serial imaging of proximal lesions, and patient education to report immediately any new pain or growth.

**Management.** Observation, excision of symptomatic individual lesions (with the same principles as solitary exostosis), correction of secondary skeletal deformities (forearm osteotomies, hemiepiphysiodesis for genu valgum, limb lengthening for significant discrepancy). No systemic medical therapy is yet available, but heparan sulphate inhibitors are under investigation.

</div>
</details>


### Enchondroma
<details id="enchondroma">
<summary>Enchondroma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-2/enchondroma.jpg" data-figure-alt="Enchondroma of a phalanx" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/ortho-2/enchondroma.jpg" alt="Enchondroma of a phalanx" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Enchondroma of a phalanx: central well-circumscribed lytic lesion with punctate or "popcorn" mineralisation.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Sameem Arif, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Enchondroma_2.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Enchondroma is a benign cartilaginous tumour arising within the medullary cavity of bone, accounting for approximately 10% of benign bone tumours. The pathogenesis involves a focus of cartilage that fails to undergo normal enchondral ossification during growth. Strong predilection for the short tubular bones of the hands (metacarpals and phalanges, where it is the commonest primary bone tumour); also commonly arising in the proximal humerus, distal femur and proximal tibia. Histologically — lobules of mature hyaline cartilage with bland chondrocytes in lacunae, surrounded by a thin shell of cortical bone.

**Clinical features and imaging.** Most enchondromas in long bones are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally. Hand enchondromas may present with pathological fracture through the lesion. On plain radiographs — a well-defined lytic lesion with characteristic **"rings and arcs"** internal calcification, endosteal scalloping, and absent periosteal reaction. On MRI — a lobulated T2-high-signal lesion, pathognomonic for cartilaginous matrix.

**Distinction from grade 1 chondrosarcoma.** One of the most difficult problems in bone pathology. Rests on a combination of clinical, radiographic and histological features:
- **Size** — low-grade chondrosarcoma is typically >5 cm; long-bone enchondromas rarely exceed 5 cm;
- **Endosteal scalloping** — deep scalloping (>2/3 of cortical thickness) suggests chondrosarcoma;
- **Cortical breach or soft-tissue mass** — almost diagnostic of chondrosarcoma;
- **Age and symptoms** — low-grade chondrosarcoma is more common in adults (40–60) and is symptomatic; asymptomatic hand lesions in a young patient are almost always enchondromas;
- **Bone scan** — enchondromas have mild to moderate uptake; chondrosarcomas are typically hotter;
- **Histology** — cellularity, binucleate cells and permeation of host bone. These features are frequently misinterpreted by pathologists with limited experience in bone pathology — grounds for expert review.

**Treatment.** Asymptomatic long-bone enchondromas — observation with serial imaging (radiograph or MRI at 6 and 12 months, then annually). Symptomatic lesions, pathological fractures and diagnostically uncertain lesions — curettage with bone grafting. In the hand, curettage with or without bone grafting yields consistently good results; in the distal femur and proximal tibia — curettage with high-speed burring and cancellous or composite grafting. **Always send the curettage tissue for pathology** — incompetent pathology review of "enchondroma" remains the commonest cause of missed grade 1 chondrosarcoma.

</div>
</details>


### Ollier Disease and Maffucci Syndrome
<details id="ollier-maffucci">
<summary>Ollier Disease and Maffucci Syndrome</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Multiple enchondromatosis (**Ollier disease**) is a non-hereditary developmental disorder characterised by multiple enchondromas, often with marked **hemisomic asymmetry** of distribution. Presentation in childhood is with multiple palpable bony swellings, limb-length discrepancy, angular deformity of the long bones from physeal involvement, and pathological fracture. The lesions may be enormous in childhood.

**Maffucci syndrome** combines multiple enchondromas with **multiple soft-tissue haemangiomas** (often visible as bluish phleboliths on radiographs or subcutaneous blue nodules). Classically described by Maffucci 1881.

**Genetics.** Both syndromes are associated with **post-zygotic mosaic mutations in IDH1 (R132C/H most common) or IDH2** — the same mutations found in central chondrosarcoma, explaining the high transformation risk. The mosaicism explains the hemisomic distribution.

**Risk of chondrosarcoma transformation** — about **25–30% in Ollier disease** and **approaching 100% over a lifetime in Maffucci syndrome** (Campanacci quotes 30–40% across both diseases combined). Maffucci also carries substantial risk of **non-skeletal malignancies**, including ovarian and pancreatic carcinoma and brain gliomas (astrocytoma).

**Management.** Lifelong surveillance with low threshold for biopsy of any painful or growing cartilaginous lesion in adulthood. Correction of skeletal deformity with osteotomies and/or limb lengthening. Occasional amputation of severely deformed and non-functional digits. On malignant transformation — treatment follows central chondrosarcoma principles.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Ollier disease — IDH1/IDH2 mosaic mutation, 25–30% lifetime chondrosarcoma risk. Maffucci syndrome — enchondromas + soft-tissue haemangiomas, risk approaching 100% + non-skeletal cancers (ovarian, pancreatic, gliomas). Biopsy any painful or growing cartilaginous lesion in adulthood.</p>
</aside>

### Periosteal Chondroma
<details id="periosteal-chondroma">
<summary>Periosteal Chondroma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Rare surface cartilaginous lesion lying beneath the periosteum of a long bone, characteristically in the proximal humerus, femur, or in metacarpal and phalangeal bones. Second and third decades. Radiographically — a well-defined eccentric surface lesion with cortical erosion, an overlying periosteal new bone, and a "buttress" reaction at the margins. Size usually under 3 cm. Histologically — mature hyaline cartilage with higher cellularity than the classical enchondroma. Distinction from low-grade periosteal chondrosarcoma: the latter is larger (>4–5 cm), more aggressive and arises more commonly in older patients. Treatment is marginal en-bloc excision with the underlying reactive bone.

</div>
</details>


### Chondroblastoma
<details id="chondroblastoma">
<summary>Chondroblastoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Chondroblastoma — historically called **Codman tumour** for its predilection for the proximal humerus, described by Codman 1931 — is a benign cartilaginous tumour with a strong predilection for the **epiphyses of long bones in the immature skeleton**. It accounts for approximately 1% of benign bone tumours. Most patients are aged 10–25 years with **open or recently closed physis**; male predominance around 3:1.

**Location.** Proximal humerus (head or greater tuberosity), distal and proximal femur, proximal tibia, in decreasing order. Less commonly — talus, calcaneus, apophyses (greater and lesser trochanter, tibial tuberosity), pelvis around the Y-cartilage. Exceptionally rare — scapula, ribs, patella, vertebral body. The lesion begins in the epiphysis and may breach the physis into the metaphysis with growth.

**Histology.** Polygonal chondroblastic cells with characteristic **longitudinal nuclear grooving**, scattered **osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells** (reactive, not neoplastic), focal chondroid matrix, and a characteristic **"chicken-wire" calcification pattern** — fine calcium granules deposited around individual cells in a delicate pericellular network. Immunohistochemically — cells are S-100 positive. The characteristic molecular mutation is **H3F3B K36M**.

**Clinical features and imaging.** Persistent joint pain, sometimes with effusion and antalgic gait — a clinical picture often mimicking juvenile joint synovitis, the reason for prolonged diagnostic latency. Radiographically — a well-defined, round-to-oval, **eccentric lytic epiphyseal lesion**, often with internal stippled mineralisation, a thin sclerotic margin, and minimal expansion. Size is typically 1–2 cm to 6–7 cm. Bone scan is hot. MRI shows adjacent marrow oedema and periarticular inflammation. CT best demonstrates the characteristic stippled matrix calcifications.

**Treatment.** Curettage with high-speed burring and either bone grafting or polymethylmethacrylate cementation. Standard approach is via the metaphysis with protection of the physeal plate and subchondral bone; for very small lesions a percutaneous CT-guided curettage technique is feasible. Recurrence rates 10–25%. **Pulmonary metastases** (rare, under 2%) — described particularly after intralesional surgery — represent biologically benign, slow-growing lesions usually responsive to observation or resection; they remain a topic of speculation regarding the true biological nature of this otherwise benign lesion. **Spinal chondroblastoma** — very rare — requires extended en-bloc vertebrectomy because of proximity to spinal cord structures.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Chondroblastoma is epiphyseal in the immature skeleton (or just-closed physis), with H3F3B K36M mutation and characteristic "chicken-wire" pericellular calcification. "Knee pain in an adolescent" with an eccentric lytic epiphyseal lesion and stippled calcifications — mandatory preoperative CT and interpretation by an expert pathologist before curettage.</p>
</aside>

### Chondromyxoid Fibroma
<details id="cmf">
<summary>Chondromyxoid Fibroma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Chondromyxoid fibroma (CMF) is a rare benign tumour — the rarest of the chondrogenic tumours, accounting for less than 1% of benign bone tumours. Predilection for the **metaphyses of long bones** in the second and third decades, particularly the proximal tibia, distal femur and small bones of the foot. Pain is mild and slowly progressive, sometimes with a palpable mass in more superficial locations.

**Imaging.** Radiographically — **eccentric, lytic, multilobulated and well-defined**, often with a sclerotic margin and characteristic septation pattern (thin bony partitions). Cortical expansion is common, and the lesion can be misinterpreted as a more aggressive tumour. On MRI — T2-high-signal lesion from the myxoid matrix.

**Histology.** Stellate or spindle-shaped cells in chondroid or myxoid matrix arranged in **lobules with sharp boundaries** (hypocellular centrally, more cellular peripherally). Less chondroid-differentiated than classical cartilage. Molecularly — **GRM1 rearrangements**.

**Differential diagnosis.** Chondroblastoma (epiphyseal, not metaphyseal; different histology), aneurysmal bone cyst, low-grade chondrosarcoma (more aggressive with permeative growth). Expert pathology review is critical.

**Treatment.** Extended intralesional curettage with high-speed burring and cancellous bone grafting — standard approach. Recurrence rates approach 15–25%, higher in young patients and in pelvic or spinal locations. **En-bloc resection** is reserved for lesions in expendable bones (fibula, rib) and for recurrent disease.

## Giant Cell Tumor of Bone
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="gct">
<details>
<summary>Giant Cell Tumor of Bone</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-2/gct.jpg" data-figure-alt="Giant cell tumour of the distal radius" aria-label="Enlarge">

  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Giant cell tumour: eccentric epiphyseal lytic lesion with subarticular location, typically in skeletally mature young adults.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Sameem Arif, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Giant_cell_tumor_bone_1.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>

Giant cell tumour of bone (GCT, osteoclastoma) — first defined as a distinct entity by Jaffe, Lichtenstein and Portis 1940 — is a benign but locally aggressive tumour, classified in the WHO scheme as an **intermediate (rarely metastasising)** lesion. It accounts for approximately 5% of primary bone neoplasms and around 20% of benign bone tumours.

**Epidemiology.** The age distribution is unusual for a bone neoplasm: GCT arises **after physeal closure**, with peak incidence in the third and fourth decades. It is rare in skeletally immature patients (under 2% of cases) and in patients over 50. Slight female predominance (Campanacci reports 51.5%).

**Location.** Marked predilection for the **ends of long bones around mobile joints**: distal femur and proximal tibia (together accounting for over 50% of cases, "around the knee"), distal radius, proximal humerus, proximal femur, distal tibia, proximal fibula — in decreasing order. Axial locations (**vertebral body, sacrum, pelvis**) pose substantial surgical challenges due to proximity to neural and visceral structures. Exceptionally rare — scapula, ribs, sternum, clavicle, skull. In long bones the tumour involves both metaphysis and epiphysis (with closed physis it reaches subchondral bone and abuts cartilage); in the exceptional pre-closure cases it is metaphyseal.

**Pathology.** Sheets of **mononuclear stromal cells** — the actual neoplastic component — interspersed with abundant **osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells** (with up to 100 nuclei identical to those of the stromal cells). Giant cells are reactive (monocyte–macrophage lineage), while stromal cells carry the **activating H3F3A G34W (less commonly G34V) mutation**. Stromal cells **overexpress RANKL** — the basis for denosumab therapy. Immunohistochemically the cells express both osteoclast markers (vimentin, CD68, acid phosphatase) and monocyte–macrophage markers. Haemorrhagic and cystic components mimicking aneurysmal bone cyst — "secondary ABC change" — occur in 10–15%.

**Clinical features.** Pain, swelling and limited motion in the adjacent joint. **Pathological fracture** is the presenting feature in 5–10%. With sacral or spinal GCT — radicular pain, neurological deficit, slowly enlarging palpable pelvic or spinal mass.

**Imaging.** **Eccentric, expansile, purely lytic lesion of epiphysis and metaphysis** (extending to subchondral bone in adults with closed growth plates), with a narrow zone of transition, **no matrix mineralisation**, no periosteal reaction, and absent or minimal sclerotic margin — the characteristic "geographic, purely lytic" pattern (Campanacci: "puddle on the sand"). The cortex is thinned and expanded, typically replaced by a thin neocortex of reactive bone. CT — homogeneous tumour with muscle-like attenuation; often with cystic cavitations, occasionally with fluid–fluid levels. MRI — T1 low to isointense, T2 high. Bone scan — typically active with stronger peripheral and weaker central uptake (Campanacci "peripheral rim"). **The Campanacci radiographic grading**:
- **Grade I** — well-circumscribed with intact cortex, narrow zone of transition;
- **Grade II** — thinned, expanded but intact cortex;
- **Grade III** — breached cortex, soft-tissue extension — aggressive.

**Treatment.** **Extended intralesional curettage with high-speed burring and adjuvant** — standard; achieves local control in 70–90% of cases. Adjuvants: phenol (5%, 30 s exposure), hydrogen peroxide, cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen, two freeze-thaw cycles) or **polymethylmethacrylate cement (PMMA)**, which provides mechanical stabilisation and a local cytotoxic effect from the heat of polymerisation (thermal kill zone of several millimetres). Cement offers the advantage of early weight-bearing recovery but carries a risk of chondrolysis of adjacent cartilage; in sensitive locations (near subchondral bone of knee or wrist) a buffer layer of cancellous bone graft is interposed between cement and cartilage.

**Wide en-bloc resection** is reserved for: Campanacci III lesions with extensive cortical destruction; recurrent disease in expendable bones; distal radius locations where resection is well tolerated; sacral GCT difficult to address intralesionally. Reconstruction varies between arthrodesis, endoprosthesis, structural allograft, or composite.

**Denosumab** (monoclonal antibody against RANKL) has transformed the medical management of GCT: it halts the osteolytic process and shifts balance toward bone formation. Administered subcutaneously (120 mg every 4 weeks with loading doses on days 8 and 15). Uses:
- **preoperatively** — to facilitate resection of difficult lesions (reduced blood loss, denser matrix easier to curette);
- **primary medical** — for unresectable spinal and pelvic lesions;
- **palliative** — in metastatic disease.

**The "denosumab dilemma".** Recurrence after discontinuation in primary tumours has prompted protocols of **short preoperative courses (3–6 months)** followed by definitive surgery, rather than prolonged primary medical therapy. Side effects: hypocalcaemia, osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical subtrochanteric fractures, rebound hypercalcaemia on discontinuation.

**Metastasis.** Approximately 2% of GCTs metastasize, almost always to the lung — slow-growing, biologically benign **"benign pulmonary metastases"** with the same histology as the primary tumour, often responsive to local resection or even observation. True malignant transformation (under 5%, particularly after prior radiotherapy) presents as osteosarcoma or fibrosarcoma with poor prognosis.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>GCT arises only after physeal closure (peak third-fourth decades), is epi-metaphyseal, eccentric, purely lytic with a narrow zone of transition and no matrix mineralisation. The stromal cell carries the H3F3A G34W/G34V mutation and overexpresses RANKL, which is the rationale for denosumab.</p>
</aside>

  <strong>NB</strong>

## Fibrous and Fibrohistiocytic Lesions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="fibrous">

<summary>Fibrous and Fibrohistiocytic Lesions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Non-Ossifying Fibroma
<details id="nof">
<summary>Non-Ossifying Fibroma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Non-ossifying fibroma (NOF; fibrous cortical defect, metaphyseal fibrous defect, historically called **"histiocytic fibroma" after Jaffe-Lichtenstein 1942**) is the most common benign bony lesion — Campanacci notes that in surgical specimens it is even more frequent than osteochondroma. It occurs in approximately 30% of children under 15 as an incidental radiographic finding — most lesions remain subclinical and spontaneously regress. It is not a true neoplasm but a **developmental (hamartomatous) metaphyseal lesion**, reflecting a localised defect in normal metaphyseal modelling during growth.

**Location and imaging.** Metaphyseal, eccentric, cortical or subcortical lesion, most commonly in the distal femur, proximal and distal tibia and fibula. Radiographically — a well-defined lytic lesion with a thin sclerotic margin, septated ("bubbly") and often lobulated. Small lesions are called "fibrous cortical defect"; larger ones "non-ossifying fibroma" — the distinction is one of size, not nature.

**Natural history.** Lesions reach maximum size around puberty, then are spontaneously replaced by sclerotic bone after physeal closure. Usually asymptomatic. Pathological fracture is possible in large lesions occupying over 50% of the cross-sectional diameter or over 33 mm in length in the distal femur.

**Treatment.** Observation for asymptomatic lesions without fracture risk. **Curettage and bone grafting** for large symptomatic lesions or after pathological fracture.

**Jaffe-Campanacci syndrome** — a rare polyostotic syndrome with multiple NOFs in long-bone metaphyses, café au lait macules, mental retardation, hypogonadism and cardiovascular anomalies. Described by Campanacci et al. 1983; often overlapping clinically with neurofibromatosis type 1.

</div>
</details>


### Benign Fibrous Histiocytoma
<details id="bfh">
<summary>Benign Fibrous Histiocytoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Benign fibrous histiocytoma of bone (BFH, xanthoma, fibroxanthoma) is a rare lesion histologically identical to active-stage non-ossifying fibroma but defined as a distinct entity by virtue of age (adults) and location (epiphysis or flat skeleton). Clinico-radiographically it resembles a quiescent GCT — a well-defined lytic metaphyseal–epiphyseal lesion with thinned cortex. Histologically — storiform pattern with foam cells and scattered multinucleated giant cells. Treatment — curettage with local adjuvant; recurrence is uncommon.

</div>
</details>

### Hemangioma of Bone
<details id="hemangioma">
<summary>Hemangioma of Bone</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Hemangioma of bone is a benign vascular lesion (likely a hamartoma per Campanacci) that occurs most commonly in the **vertebral bodies** (where it accounts for the great majority of incidentally noted vertebral lesions — over 10% of autopsy series) and the **skull**. The lesion consists of thin-walled vascular channels — capillary, cavernous, or mixed — within bone. Histologically the capillary type predominates in active lesions, the cavernous in inactive lesions; the transition between the two reflects stages of development, not distinct tumours.

**Vertebral hemangioma.** Characteristic radiographic appearance of a vertebral body with prominent vertical trabeculae — **"corduroy" or "honeycomb" on plain radiographs**, **"polka-dot" on axial CT** — reflecting the vertical alignment of residual trabeculae between vascular spaces. On MRI — typically high signal on both T1 and T2 sequences due to fat content (a diagnostic key distinguishing hemangioma from metastasis, which is T1-low-signal). The vast majority are asymptomatic and require no treatment.

**Aggressive (symptomatic) hemangioma** — characterised by extra-osseous extension, soft-tissue mass, cortical destruction, neurological compression from epidural extension, or pathological fracture. A T1-low-signal lesion with intravertebral soft-tissue mass is almost diagnostic of an aggressive hemangioma and warrants treatment. Options:
- **Vertebroplasty** with PMMA — obliterates the vascular spaces and provides mechanical reinforcement;
- **Selective arterial embolization** — preoperative or as standalone therapy (Campanacci describes patients cured by repeated embolization alone);
- **Open decompression** for significant neurological symptoms;
- **Radiotherapy** — reserved for unresectable lesions because of secondary sarcoma risk.

**Long-bone hemangiomas.** Less common than vertebral; radiographically — a well-defined lytic lesion with coarse trabeculae ("honeycomb"). Histological assessment after aspiration carries the risk of potentially catastrophic bleeding — mandatory preoperative selective arterial embolization.

</details>


### Intraosseous Lipoma
<details id="lipoma">
<summary>Intraosseous Lipoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Intraosseous lipoma is a rare benign tumour of fat within bone. The commonest locations are the **calcaneus** (particularly within the central triangle of reduced trabecular density) and the **intertrochanteric region of the proximal femur**. The lesion is usually asymptomatic. Radiographically — a well-defined lytic area, sometimes with **central calcification or ossification** (histologically — a focus of fat necrosis). MRI is diagnostic by demonstrating **fat signal** — T1-high, T2-high with loss on fat-suppressed sequences. The Milgram (1988) classification:
- **Stage 1** — solid fat lesion;
- **Stage 2** — partial fat necrosis with central calcification;
- **Stage 3** — complete necrosis, sclerosis, sometimes cystic change.

Treatment — observation for asymptomatic lesions. Symptomatic lesions or those with fracture risk (proximal femur) are curetted and grafted.

</div>
</details>


### Intraosseous Schwannoma
<details id="schwannoma">
<summary>Intraosseous Schwannoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Primary intraosseous schwannoma (neurilemmoma) is rare and occurs most commonly in the **mandible** (along the mandibular nerve), **sacrum** and **vertebral bodies**. The lesion is typically a well-defined, expansile, lytic lesion with a thin sclerotic margin. Histologically — a soft-tissue schwannoma with Antoni A areas (compact spindle cells with Verocay bodies) and Antoni B areas (loose myxoid component). S-100 positive. Treatment is curettage or marginal excision; recurrence is uncommon.

</div>
</details>


### Adamantinoma
<details id="adamantinoma">
<summary>Adamantinoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Adamantinoma is a **rare, locally aggressive, low-grade malignant** epithelial tumour of bone with a strong predilection for the **anterior diaphyseal cortex of the tibia** (about 90% of cases). Peak age — second to third decade. The name reflects histological similarity to the ameloblastoma of the jaw.

Although low-grade and slow-growing, it has **metastatic potential** (around 15–20%, usually pulmonary after repeated local recurrences) and is therefore not strictly benign; it is included here for the radiographic differential, since it shares many features with **osteofibrous dysplasia and fibrous dysplasia** (the "OFD–adamantinoma" spectrum — some lesions begin as osteofibrous dysplasia in childhood and evolve to adamantinoma later).

**Imaging.** Multifocal, lobulated, lytic lesion with sclerosis and cortical expansion of the anterior tibial cortex ("**soap bubble**" appearance); multiple adjacent satellite lesions in the cortex.

**Histology.** Epithelial islands (resembling ameloblastoma) within a fibrous stroma. Immunohistochemistry — epithelial cells are keratin-positive.

**Treatment.** **Wide en-bloc resection with reconstruction** is the standard treatment because of malignant potential and high recurrence after intralesional surgery (>50%). Reconstruction is usually a segmental tibial resection with vascularised fibular autograft or intercalary allograft.

## Surgical Management — Principles
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Surgical Management — Principles</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The surgical management of benign bone tumours follows a hierarchy based on Enneking grading and the specific behaviour of each lesion. The Hoppenfeld principle — "the penalty of the approach must not exceed the benefit of the procedure" — remains directive: minimally invasive adjuvants are not an end in themselves but a choice for the appropriate pathology and the experienced surgeon.


### Observation
<details id="observation">
<summary>Observation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Appropriate for **latent (stage 1) lesions** that are asymptomatic, mechanically stable, and demonstrate no concerning radiographic features — the typical incidentally discovered osteochondroma in an adult, the small enchondroma of the proximal humerus, the "polka-dot" vertebral hemangioma, the asymptomatic distal-femoral NOF in a child. Serial follow-up with radiograph or MRI at 6 and 12 months, then annually; monitor for pain, growth, cortical change, new periosteal reaction, or change in matrix mineralisation. The patient must be instructed to report immediately on pain or growth — especially important in hereditary multiple exostoses, Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome because of chondrosarcoma transformation risk.

</div>
</details>


### Curettage and Adjuvants
<details id="curettage">
<summary>Curettage and Adjuvants</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Intralesional curettage** — the historical mainstay; appropriate for active (stage 2) lesions such as enchondroma, chondroblastoma, chondromyxoid fibroma and non-ossifying fibroma. The lesion is debulked through a **wide cortical window** (the Enneking rule — a window covering the entire lesion in length; an inadequately exposed window leaves viable tissue); the cavity is examined and aggressively curetted with a series of curettes; mirror or endoscopy is routinely used to visualize blind angles (particularly in epiphyseal lesions under subchondral bone).

**Extended intralesional curettage** (the standard for aggressive Stage 3 benign lesions, particularly GCT and aggressive osteoblastoma) adds:
- **High-speed burring** of the cavity walls (diamond or spherical burr at 50,000–80,000 rpm) — removes the macroscopically invisible fringe of viable tumour cells extending into trabecular bone to 2–5 mm depth;
- **Local adjuvant**:
  - **Polymethylmethacrylate cement (PMMA)** — exothermic polymerisation (60–80 °C) creates a thermal kill zone of 2–3 mm; also provides mechanical stabilisation and early weight-bearing;
  - **Phenol** — 5%, 30 s exposure, followed by copious saline and absolute alcohol rinse; toxicity to adjacent soft tissues (caution near neurovascular bundle);
  - **Cryotherapy** — liquid nitrogen (−196 °C), one or two freeze-thaw cycles; mandatory protection of skin and adjacent soft tissues;
  - **Argon-beam coagulation** — a modern thermal alternative to electrocautery with shallower penetration;
  - **Ethanol** (95–100%) — topical application; milder cytotoxic effect than phenol.

The choice of adjuvant depends on location, presence of sensitive adjacent structures (cartilage, neurovascular bundle), and surgical preference; PMMA remains the most widely used for distal femur, proximal tibia and distal radius GCT.

</div>
</details>


### En-Bloc Resection
<details id="resection">
<summary>En-Bloc Resection</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Wide en-bloc resection** with margins of normal bone and soft tissue is reserved for:
- Campanacci III GCT with extensive cortical destruction and soft-tissue extension;
- Recurrent aggressive lesions in expendable bones (proximal fibula, distal ulna, bones of the hand);
- Distal radius locations where the functional result after curettage is unacceptable;
- Suspected low-grade chondrosarcoma masquerading as enchondroma;
- Adamantinoma (standard treatment);
- Sacral GCT difficult to address intralesionally (when technically feasible while preserving S2–S3 roots for pelvic reservoir function).

**Marginal resection** (along the capsule or reactive zone) — an alternative for stage 2 lesions in expendable bones, particularly when wide resection would damage vital structures (for example the V3 segment of the vertebral artery in upper cervical tumours).

</div>
</details>


### Reconstruction
<details id="reconstruction">
<summary>Reconstruction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Reconstruction after benign tumour surgery varies with defect and anatomic location.

**Small contained defects** — packing with autogenous cancellous bone graft (iliac crest or distal femur) — biologically optimal but limited by volume and donor-site morbidity; or allogeneic cancellous graft (bone bank) for larger volumes; or PMMA cement (in GCT for early weight-bearing).

**Segmental diaphyseal defects** — structural cortical allograft (intercalary), vascularised fibular autograft (especially in young patients with long life expectancy), or Masquelet induced-membrane technique (two-stage: first stage PMMA spacer to induce a biological membrane, second stage replacement with autogenous graft).

**Periarticular defects** — where joint salvage is impossible — endoprosthetic replacement (megaprosthesis); arthrodesis (particularly the wrist after distal radius resection); allograft-prosthesis composite.

**Biological principles of incorporation:**
- **Creeping substitution** — with cancellous autograft; gradual remodeling from vascular invasion and ossification over 6–18 months;
- **Slower remodeling** with allograft — risks of non-union, fracture, and late resorption;
- **Primary union** with vascularised autograft — fastest incorporation and hypertrophy with weight-bearing.

The choice also dictates the postoperative rehabilitation protocol — graduated weight-bearing, dynamic strengthening, and where appropriate an interim off-loading brace.

</div>
</details>


### Medical Adjuvants
<details id="medical-adjuvants">
<summary>Medical Adjuvants</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Denosumab** — monoclonal antibody against RANKL — has revolutionised the management of GCT (see "Giant Cell Tumor" section). Side effects: hypocalcaemia (monitor serum calcium and vitamin D), osteonecrosis of the jaw (preoperative dental review mandatory), atypical subtrochanteric fractures with prolonged use, rebound hypercalcaemia on discontinuation in childhood.

**Percutaneous CT-guided radiofrequency ablation** — treatment of choice for accessible osteoid osteomas (90% success).

**Selective arterial embolization** — preoperative reduction of intraoperative blood loss for large spinal, pelvic or sacral lesions (aggressive hemangioma, spinal osteoblastoma, sacral GCT). As standalone treatment for unresectable aggressive vertebral hemangioma (repeated embolization with polyvinyl alcohol — Campanacci describes patients cured by embolization alone).

**Bisphosphonates** (zoledronate, pamidronate) — mainstays for fibrous dysplasia (see ortho-3); experimentally for GCT before the denosumab era.

**Radiotherapy** — used today only for surgically unresectable lesions (aggressive hemangioma, sacral GCT refractory to denosumab) because of secondary sarcoma risk (1–3% lifetime risk 10–20 years after radiotherapy); a reserve modality.

</div>
</details>

</div>
</details>

## Outcomes and Complications
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Complications of surgical management of benign bone tumours vary by lesion type and method.

**Curettage and grafting:** local recurrence (10–25% for GCT after extended curettage with adjuvant, 10–20% for osteoblastoma and chondroblastoma, 15–25% for chondromyxoid fibroma); pathological fracture through the reconstructed bone (3–7%, more common after cement filling in load-bearing positions); infection (1–3%); iatrogenic joint instability in subchondral lesions; cartilage chondrolysis from cement thermal effect in subchondral position without buffer bone layer; transient neuropraxia near major nerves.

**En-bloc resection:** complications depend on anatomic location and reconstruction. Allograft — non-union (10–15%), fracture (5–10% for intercalary grafts), resorption, infection (5–10% — higher than with autograft). Vascularised autograft — donor-site morbidity (fibular fracture, flexor hallucis longus weakness), partial or complete result failure on microsurgical failure. Endoprosthesis — aseptic loosening, periprosthetic infection, polyethylene wear, prosthetic dislocation.

**Percutaneous radiofrequency ablation:** thermal injury to overlying skin (thermal burn, about 1–2% with proper technique); incomplete ablation and recurrence (5–10%); rarely injury to adjacent neural structures.

**Denosumab:** hypocalcaemia, osteonecrosis of the jaw (~1% with long-term therapy), atypical fractures, rebound hypercalcaemia in childhood, GCT recurrence after discontinuation (motivating short preoperative courses).

**Selective arterial embolization:** iatrogenic embolization of non-target vessels (cord medullary artery in spinal interventions — risk of spinal cord infarction); post-embolization syndrome (pain, fever, raised CRP for 48–72 hours).

**Long-term outcomes.** Benign bone tumours have a good long-term prognosis with appropriate treatment. Local control exceeds 90% in stage 1, 80–90% in stage 2, and 70–85% in stage 3. Functional results (Musculoskeletal Tumor Society Score, MSTS) typically exceed 75–85% of normal after curettage with reconstruction and 65–80% after resection with endoprosthesis. **The single most consequential pitfall** remains misdiagnosing a low-grade central chondrosarcoma as enchondroma in an adult with newly painful or growing cartilaginous lesion; every orthopaedic oncologist must entertain malignancy as a possibility in any cartilaginous lesion of adult onset.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>The single most consequential error is mislabeling a low-grade central chondrosarcoma as an enchondroma. Red flags in an adult: size >5 cm, deep endosteal scalloping (>two-thirds of cortical thickness), cortical breach or soft-tissue mass, and new pain in a previously silent cartilaginous lesion. Expert pathology review and MDT discussion before definitive management.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school and the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) contains detailed operative descriptions for bone tumour surgery in the Bulgarian tradition — particularly the chapters on curettage with bone grafting for benign bone tumours and pelvic surgery for large lesions — which define the terminology and surgical strategy of the national curriculum.

**P. Tanchev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Bulgarian modification of percutaneous bone biopsy (with G. Kaymakchiev, 1983) — thick needle with stylet and a cancellous-bitten wedge key to obtain a solid bone core — useful for deep vertebral lesions suspected of GCT, chordoma or aggressive hemangioma, where matrix character requires a larger sample. The Bulgarian group also leads contemporary practice in instrumented resection with reconstruction for spinal tumours.

**M. V. Volkov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1923–2003).** The classical figure of paediatric bone pathology in the Soviet orthopaedic tradition, director of CITO (Moscow). The three-volume *"Bone Pathology of Childhood"* (1968, multiple reprints) remains a reference foundation in the Bulgarian curriculum in paediatric skeletal oncology; the chapters on osteochondromatosis, Ollier disease, and fibrous dysplasia in childhood are widely used in resident preparation. Volkov's early resection of giant cell tumours in childhood with allograft replacement is a historical foundation for contemporary paediatric protocols.

**S. T. Zatsepin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1923–2006).** Classic of bone oncology in the Soviet CITO school, author of the monograph *"Bone Pathology of Adults"* (2001) — a comprehensive summary of his 50-year operative experience with over 6 000 patients with bone tumours. He describes precise techniques of extended intralesional curettage with local adjuvants (phenol, cement), segmental pelvic resections, and reconstruction with allografts. Zatsepin's concept of combining wide resection for aggressive benign lesions (GCT Campanacci III, aggressive hemangioma) with reliable reconstruction remains a methodological foundation in Bulgarian practice.

**A. A. Korzh <sup class="natflag">UA</sup> (1924–2010).** The school of the Sytenko Institute of Pathology of the Spine and Joints in Kharkov. Precise descriptions of spinal benign tumours (osteoblastoma, aggressive hemangioma, spinal GCT) and the corresponding anterior and posterior surgical approaches. Modification of the vertebrodesis technique for spinal tumours with interbody bone grafting remains applicable in selected cases today.

**N. N. Trapeznikov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1928–2001).** Director of the Russian Cancer Research Centre "N. N. Blokhin" (Moscow). Classical contribution to adjuvant chemotherapy of aggressive and low-grade malignant bone tumours; the concept of the multidisciplinary oncology team (MDT) and of pre- and postoperative chemotherapy in borderline diagnoses (adamantinoma, aggressive GCT) was adopted by the Bulgarian oncology school from the 1980s onwards.

**V. D. Chaklin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1892–1976).** The school of the Urals Institute of Traumatology and Orthopaedics. The classical *"Foundations of Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology"*; chapters on reconstructive bone surgery and on reconstruction after bone tumour resection remain widely cited in the Bulgarian curriculum.

</div>
</details>

## Classical Sources
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">
<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical lineage — bone tumour pathology</strong>
  <p>Bone tumours were described in the general surgical pathologies of the late nineteenth century before modern histopathology. The distinct entities took their modern shape in the first half of the twentieth century in the works of Jaffe, Lichtenstein and Codman, and the classical epidemiological and operative foundation was laid by Dahlin (Mayo Clinic) and Campanacci (Rizzoli, Bologna).</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Billroth 1882</strong> — *Allgemeine chirurgische Pathologie und Therapie*; general principles of tumour surgery and the first classifications of bone neoplasms (<a href="https://archive.org/details/dieallgemeinechi1882bill">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Lexer 1904</strong> — *Lehrbuch der allgemeinen Chirurgie*; description of "Knochenneubildungen" — the substrate from which osteoid osteoma and other benign bone lesions were later separated (<a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderallg01lexegoog">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Hoffa 1905</strong> — *Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie*; orthopaedic perspective on bone neoplasms before the era of specialised skeletal oncology (<a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderortho00hoff">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Codman 1931</strong> — description of the "Codman tumour" (chondroblastoma) of the proximal humerus.</li>
    <li><strong>Jaffe 1935</strong> — separation of osteoid osteoma as a distinct entity.</li>
    <li><strong>Jaffe, Lichtenstein, Portis 1940</strong> — separation of giant cell tumour from other giant-cell lesions (histiocytic fibroma, chondroblastoma, ABC).</li>
    <li><strong>Jaffe-Lichtenstein 1942</strong> — description of non-ossifying fibroma (histiocytic fibroma).</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Classical descriptions by entity: **Maffucci 1881** (enchondromatosis with haemangiomas syndrome); **Ollier 1899** (multiple enchondromatosis); **Codman 1931** (chondroblastoma); **Jaffe 1935** (osteoid osteoma); **Jaffe-Lichtenstein-Portis 1940** (GCT); **Jaffe-Lichtenstein 1942** (non-ossifying fibroma); **Lichtenstein 1956** (osteoblastoma); **Enneking 1980** (benign-lesion staging and surgical strategy); **Campanacci 1983** (Jaffe-Campanacci syndrome); **Campanacci 1986** (radiographic grading of GCT).

Classical monographs and reference volumes: **Lichtenstein L.**, *Bone Tumors*, 5th edition, Mosby (St. Louis, 1977); **Jaffe HL.**, *Tumors and Tumorous Conditions of the Bones and Joints*, Lea & Febiger (Philadelphia, 1958); **Dahlin DC.**, *Bone Tumors: General Aspects and Data on 6,221 Cases*, Charles C. Thomas (Springfield, 3rd edition 1978; continued as Unni & Inwards, Dahlin's Bone Tumors, Lippincott 2010); **Schajowicz F.**, *Tumors and Tumorlike Lesions of Bone*, Springer (Berlin, 2nd edition 1994); **Campanacci M.**, *Bone and Soft Tissue Tumors: Clinical Features, Imaging, Pathology and Treatment*, Springer-Verlag/Piccin (Vienna, 2nd edition 1999) — the Rizzoli Institute tradition. **Mirra JM.**, *Bone Tumors: Clinical, Radiologic, and Pathologic Correlations*, Lea & Febiger (Philadelphia, 1989) — radiographic-pathological correlation in three volumes.

Classical conceptual framework by Enneking: *Musculoskeletal Tumor Society Surgical Staging System*, 1980 — the foundation of modern surgical planning strategy for bone tumours.
