## Introduction and Conceptual Framework

Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is a clinical syndrome in which abnormal mechanical contact between the proximal femur and the acetabular rim during hip motion produces progressive damage to the acetabular labrum, the chondrolabral junction, and the underlying articular cartilage. The concept of FAI, articulated principally by Reinhold Ganz and his collaborators in Bern, Switzerland, in the late 1990s and early 2000s, has transformed the understanding of pre-arthritic hip disease in young adults and has provided a unifying framework that connects the mechanical morphology of the hip to the clinical syndromes of hip pain, labral pathology, and the early development of hip osteoarthritis. The recognition that FAI is a major cause of hip pain in young adults — particularly in athletes and in patients with prior hip pathology such as Perthes disease, SCFE, and developmental dysplasia — has led to the rapid expansion of hip arthroscopy and surgical hip dislocation as treatments for this condition. This chapter, synthesizing content from *Operative Hip Arthroscopy* (Byrd), *Hip Arthroscopy and Hip Joint Preservation Surgery* (Nho, Leunig, Kelly), *The Adult Hip* (Callaghan), and Apley & Solomon's, addresses the morphological subtypes of FAI, the clinical and imaging diagnosis, the natural history and association with osteoarthritis, and the surgical management options including hip arthroscopy and surgical hip dislocation.

## Historical Development of the Concept

The concept of FAI is the product of a gradual accumulation of observations dating from the early twentieth century. Smith-Petersen described in 1936 an osteotomy of the anterior acetabular rim in patients with an "overhanging acetabulum" — one of the first surgical recognitions that a bony rim prominence could mechanically damage the hip. Stulberg and colleagues (1975) popularized the term "pistol-grip" deformity of the proximal femur in adults with idiopathic osteoarthritis, noting frequent association with childhood hip disorders (Legg–Calvé–Perthes, SCFE) and suggesting a causal link between residual deformity and OA development. Murray (1965) and later Solomon (1976) argued that many cases of "primary" coxarthrosis are in fact secondary to antecedent subclinical hip morphology. Through the 1970s and 1980s, Harris and Aronson coalesced these observations into the concept of "subclinical dysplasia" as a cause of early coxarthrosis. The conceptual and surgical leap was made by Reinhold Ganz in the late 1990s, who formalized the FAI concept, described the mechanisms of cam and pincer impingement as distinct but often overlapping processes, and introduced surgical hip dislocation as a controlled, vascularly safe approach for their direct treatment. The first targeted arthroscopic surgeries for FAI were reported by Byrd and Sampson in the early 2000s, and over the following decade hip arthroscopy expanded exponentially as a minimally invasive alternative to open surgical dislocation.

## Morphological Subtypes

Three principal morphological subtypes of FAI are described, each with distinct mechanical features and clinical implications.

## Cam Impingement

Cam impingement arises from a non-spherical, often "pistol-grip" deformity of the femoral head-neck junction, in which the head-neck offset is reduced and a bony prominence at the anterolateral femoral head-neck junction enters the acetabulum during hip flexion and internal rotation. The aspherical head pinches against the acetabular rim and "shears" against the chondrolabral junction, producing a characteristic pattern of cartilage delamination at the anterosuperior acetabulum with the labrum often relatively preserved early in the disease.

The aspherical morphology is quantified by the alpha angle, measured on a radial cross-sectional MRI or oblique-axial CT image: the angle between (1) a line from the center of the femoral head along the long axis of the femoral neck and (2) a line from the center of the femoral head to the point where the bony contour first exceeds the radius of the head. Alpha angles greater than 50–55° are considered abnormal, with higher values indicating more severe deformity. Additional quantitative measures include the head-neck offset (normal >8 mm on the lateral view), the offset/head-diameter ratio (normal >0.17), and the Nötzli alpha angle on oblique-axial MRI.

Cam impingement is more common in men, athletes, and patients with a history of childhood hip disorders (Perthes, SCFE, post-traumatic deformity). The aspherical morphology often develops during the adolescent growth spurt, particularly in young male athletes engaged in pivoting sports (football, hockey, soccer), suggesting that the morphology may be acquired in response to mechanical demands during physeal maturation. The studies of Siebenrock and Agricola in adolescent athletes demonstrate significantly higher rates of alpha angles >60° compared with inactive controls as early as ages 14–18, with stabilization of morphology after physeal closure.

## Pincer Impingement

Pincer impingement arises from excessive bony coverage of the femoral head by the acetabulum, producing a mechanical block to hip flexion as the femoral neck contacts the over-prominent acetabular rim. The labrum is the principal structure injured, with progressive crushing, ossification, and degeneration of the labrum at the impingement site. Cartilage damage is typically less severe than in cam impingement, and is often restricted to a "contre-coup" pattern at the posteroinferior aspect of the acetabulum from the levering effect of the impingement.

Several specific anatomical variants produce pincer impingement: acetabular retroversion (the acetabulum facing more anteriorly than the normal slight anteversion); coxa profunda (a deep acetabulum with the floor extending to or beyond the ilio-ischial line); protrusio acetabuli (the femoral head extending medial to the ilio-ischial line); and focal anterior rim overcoverage from os acetabuli or other rim anomalies.

Pincer impingement is more common in women than men, often in middle-aged women, and is often associated with relative inactivity rather than the athletic profile of cam impingement. Global overcoverage (coxa profunda, protrusio) and focal anterior overcoverage (acetabular retroversion) represent different pathomechanical variants and require different surgical planning: global overcoverage often requires extensive circumferential acetabuloplasty, while retroversion may be addressed by focal anterior rim trimming or by reverse periacetabular osteotomy.

## Combined (Mixed) Impingement

Combined impingement — features of both cam and pincer — is the commonest pattern in clinical practice, with most patients showing some degree of both abnormal femoral head-neck offset and abnormal acetabular coverage. The treatment must address both components. The series of Beck and colleagues (Bern) showed that among patients operated for FAI, pure cam and pure pincer types account for less than 30% of cases, with the remaining 70% mixed, with a variable dominant component.

## Extra-articular Impingement Variants

In addition to classical intra-articular cam and pincer impingement, several extra-articular forms of hip impingement are recognized, which may reproduce or accompany classical FAI:

Subspine (AIIS) impingement: A prominent or distally extended anterior inferior iliac spine can mechanically block hip flexion and internal rotation, particularly in patients with post-traumatic or avulsion-related AIIS deformity from sports injury. Treatment is arthroscopic or open decortication of the AIIS.

Ischiofemoral impingement: Narrowing of the space between the lesser trochanter and the ischial tuberosity can produce impingement of the quadratus femoris muscle, with posterior hip pain worsened by extension and adduction. Diagnosis is made by MRI (reduced ischiofemoral space, edema of quadratus femoris); treatment is initially conservative, with surgical decompression or lesser trochanter resection for persistent symptoms.

Iliopsoas impingement: Impingement of the iliopsoas tendon against the anterior acetabular rim or anterior capsule, particularly in patients with focal anterior capsulolabral abnormality, may produce anterior groin pain and snapping. Arthroscopic iliopsoas release in the central or peripheral compartment is the standard treatment for persistent cases.

## Pathophysiology and Natural History

The repeated mechanical impingement of the femur against the acetabulum during hip motion produces progressive damage to the chondrolabral junction. In cam impingement, the principal pattern is cartilage delamination at the anterosuperior acetabulum, with a relatively well-preserved labrum until later in the disease — the so-called "inside-out shear injury," in which the cam bump acts as a shearing instrument against the cartilage, leaving the labrum relatively spared. In pincer impingement, the labrum is the principal structure damaged, with progressive degeneration, fraying, ossification, and cyst formation in the labral substance — the levering motion of the neck against the rim crushes the labrum, without significant force transfer to the cartilage. In both patterns, the eventual outcome is progressive joint damage with development of secondary osteoarthritis.

Histopathological studies by Beck and Leunig demonstrate that in cam impingement, cartilage delamination begins as separation of cartilage from the subchondral bone plate along the chondrolabral junction in the anterosuperior zone (12 to 3 o'clock on the right acetabulum by the clock convention), with preservation of the lamina splendens and superficial layers, and progresses to a full-thickness defect. In pincer impingement, the early histological findings in the labrum include hyaline degeneration, mucinous deformation, and focal intralabral ossification.

## Epidemiology and Association with Osteoarthritis

The association of FAI with hip osteoarthritis is one of the major clinical insights of the past two decades. Morphological features of cam impingement (alpha angle >55–60°) are found in 15–25% of asymptomatic adults in the general population on systematic screening, with prevalence significantly higher in young male athletes (30–50%). Morphological features of pincer impingement (positive crossover sign) are found in 5–15% of asymptomatic adults. Not all individuals with morphological features of FAI develop symptomatic disease — an essential distinction that informs clinical management and explains why prophylactic surgery in asymptomatic FAI is not justified.

Several large cohort studies have demonstrated that morphological features of FAI in young adults are strongly predictive of the development of symptomatic hip osteoarthritis in middle age. The Copenhagen Osteoarthritis Study (Nicholls et al., Arthritis Rheum 2011) followed patients with baseline hip radiographs and showed that patients with alpha angle >60° had a 9-fold increased risk of total hip arthroplasty within 19 years. The CHECK Study (Cohort Hip and Cohort Knee, Netherlands; Agricola et al.) and the Framingham Study established similar risk associations. The absolute proportion of patients with morphological FAI who develop symptomatic OA remains variable and depends on the severity of the morphological abnormality, the activity level, and concomitant factors.

## Clinical Features

The classical presentation is of an active young adult (most commonly in the third or fourth decade) with insidious onset of activity-related groin pain. The pain is typically worse with prolonged sitting (particularly in deep-seated chairs or driving for long periods), with hip flexion activities (squatting, sitting on the floor), and with athletic activities involving pivoting, rotation, or sustained flexion. The "C-sign" — the patient cups the affected hip with the fingers anteriorly and the thumb posteriorly to indicate the location of pain — is a characteristic gesture and pattern of pain localization. Mechanical symptoms (clicking, catching, locking) are common when labral tearing has occurred. The pain is often poorly localized initially; differentiation from lumbar radicular pain, sports hernia, athletic pubalgia, and other causes of hip and groin pain may require careful clinical assessment and targeted imaging.

Examination reveals: decreased hip flexion (often by 10–20° compared with the contralateral side); decreased internal rotation in flexion (the most reliable single examination finding); pain on the anterior impingement test (FADIR — flexion, adduction, internal rotation — reproduces the patient's symptoms); pain on the posterior impingement test (extension and external rotation) in pincer-pattern impingement involving the posterior rim; and sometimes tenderness over the anterior hip joint. Range of motion of the lumbar spine, sacroiliac joints, and femoral nerve should be assessed to exclude other sources of pain.

## Physical Examination — Provocation Tests

The systematic physical examination for suspected FAI includes several standardized provocation tests:

Anterior impingement test (FADIR): The patient lies supine. The examiner passively flexes the hip to 90°, adducts it across the midline, and internally rotates it. Reproduction of the patient's groin pain is a positive test. The sensitivity of the test for intra-articular pathology is approximately 88–95%, but specificity is lower since other intra-articular processes (arthritis, osteonecrosis, septic arthritis) can also yield a positive result.

Posterior impingement test: The patient lies supine at the edge of the table with the lower leg hanging freely. The examiner extends the hip and externally rotates it. Reproduction of pain is a positive test for posterior or postero-inferior impingement.

FABER (Patrick) test: The patient lies supine. The hip is flexed, abducted, and externally rotated, with the lateral malleolus placed over the contralateral knee. Reproduction of groin pain indicates intra-articular pathology; posterior pain indicates sacroiliac pathology. The distance from the lateral knee to the table is measured to quantify the restriction.

Log roll test: The patient lies supine with extended leg; the examiner passively rotates the leg internally and externally. Pain or restriction indicates intra-articular pathology; the test is sensitive but nonspecific.

Stinchfield test: The patient actively elevates the extended leg against resistance at 30° flexion; reproduction of pain indicates intra-articular or iliopsoas pathology.

Anterior apprehension test: Extension and external rotation in the supine patient, to assess anterior instability in the context of microinstability.

## Imaging

## Plain Radiographs

The standard imaging assessment begins with two views: a well-centered AP pelvis radiograph (with attention to standardized positioning, since rotation of the pelvis substantially alters the apparent morphology) and a lateral view of the hip (Dunn 45° or 90° view, cross-table lateral, or frog-lateral). The AP pelvis is assessed for:

Acetabular morphology and version: The center-edge angle of Wiberg quantifies lateral acetabular coverage (normal 25–40°, with greater than 40° indicating overcoverage and less than 25° indicating undercoverage); the acetabular index (Tönnis angle) quantifies the slope of the acetabular roof (normal 0–10°, with negative values indicating overcoverage); the crossover sign (the anterior wall and posterior wall of the acetabulum crossing on the AP view rather than the normal pattern with the anterior wall lying medial to the posterior wall) indicates focal anterior overcoverage with relative acetabular retroversion; the posterior wall sign (the posterior wall failing to extend to the center of the femoral head) indicates a deficient posterior wall; the ischial spine sign (the ischial spine projecting medial to the ilio-ischial line) is a marker of acetabular retroversion; coxa profunda (the floor of the acetabulum extending to or beyond the ilio-ischial line) and protrusio acetabuli (the femoral head extending medial to the ilio-ischial line) are signs of overcoverage.

Femoral morphology: Reduced head-neck offset, asphericity of the femoral head, "pistol-grip" appearance of the proximal femur, and direct visualization of cam-type bumps at the anterolateral or anterior femoral head-neck junction.

The lateral view (typically Dunn 45° lateral) provides better assessment of the femoral head-neck offset and the anterior cam morphology, with quantification by the alpha angle (>50–55° abnormal).

## Standardized Pelvic Positioning

Small changes in pelvic positioning significantly alter the apparent morphology on the AP view and may create spurious signs of overcoverage or undercoverage. Standardized positioning requires: supine patient; X-ray tube centered over the midline of the pelvis at the level midway between the pubic symphysis and the anterior superior iliac spine; tube-to-detector distance of 100–120 cm; patient with the limbs slightly internally rotated (10–15°) to compensate for normal femoral anteversion and to profile the greater trochanter.

Image quality: Adequate centering is confirmed by three criteria: (1) the distance from the tip of the coccyx to the upper edge of the pubic symphysis is 1–3 cm (indicator of neutral pelvic tilt in the sagittal plane); (2) the obturator foramina are symmetric in shape (indicator of neutral rotation); and (3) the pubic symphysis is centered on the midline. Mispositioning can create or mask the crossover and ischial spine signs and must be corrected before interpretation.

## Magnetic Resonance Arthrography

MR arthrography with intra-articular gadolinium has become the standard imaging study for definitive diagnosis. It provides high-resolution assessment of the labrum (with intra-articular contrast highlighting tears as contrast extending into the labral substance or chondrolabral junction), the cartilage (with characterization of focal cartilage defects and delamination), the femoral head-neck morphology (with measurement of alpha angles on radial cuts), and any associated pathology including osteonecrosis, paralabral cysts, and synovial chondromatosis. Intra-articular injection of local anesthetic at the time of arthrography also serves as a diagnostic test: complete relief of pain after intra-articular anesthetic strongly supports an intra-articular source of pain.

Radial sequences (cross-sectional images oriented perpendicular to the femoral neck axis and rotated around the neck at 15–30° intervals) are an indispensable component of the FAI imaging protocol, as they allow alpha angle measurement at every clock position of the acetabular clock face and identification of the maximally aspherical point, which is often missed on standard Dunn views. The chondral signal is evaluated for delamination (the characteristic "carpet sign" of McCarthy — detached cartilage over an intact bony plate) and full-thickness defects. Paralabral cysts associated with labral tears appear as focal hyperintense lesions adjacent to the labrum.

## CT and 3D Imaging

CT with three-dimensional reconstruction provides the most accurate bony morphological assessment and is increasingly used for surgical planning, particularly for complex cam morphology and for assessment of acetabular version and femoral version. Dynamic simulation through computer-assisted software allows preoperative assessment of impingement in different hip positions and precise planning of cam resection. Analysis of femoral version (normally 10–20° anteversion) and acetabular version (normally 15–20° anteversion) identifies patients with combined femoro-acetabular retroversion, in whom isolated rim trimming may produce residual impingement and femoral derotational osteotomy may be indicated.

## Diagnostic Intra-articular Injection

Intra-articular injection of local anesthetic (lidocaine or bupivacaine), with or without concurrent corticosteroid, under fluoroscopic, ultrasound, or MRI guidance, remains a valuable diagnostic tool in patients with poorly localized pain or suspicion of multiple concurrent pain sources. Complete or near-complete relief of pain for 1–4 hours after injection strongly supports an intra-articular source of pain and predicts a good response to arthroscopic surgery. A positive response is among the predictors of successful surgical outcome in multiple series and is a recommended preoperative test in patients with a mixed clinical picture.

## Differential Diagnosis

Groin and anterior hip pain in young adults requires a broad differential diagnosis:

Lumbar radiculopathy (L1–L2): Pain along the anteromedial thigh with a radicular distribution. Distinguished from FAI by the absence of positive impingement tests and by positive nerve-tension tests.

Sports hernia and athletic pubalgia: Lower abdominal and pubic pain in athletes, worsened by sit-ups and resisted abdominal effort. Often coexists with FAI in elite athletes — up to 40% of athletes with athletic pubalgia have concomitant FAI.

Iliopsoas tendinitis or snapping iliopsoas: Anterior bundle pain and snapping over the anterior hip. The resisted hip flexion test is more provocative than the FADIR test.

Sacroiliac pathology: Posterior and buttock pain, worsened by single-leg stance. The Gaenslen, FABER, and compression tests distinguish from FAI.

Avascular necrosis of the femoral head: Groin pain with progression. MRI is definitive.

Femoral neck stress fracture: Groin pain on rhythmic activities (running), often missed on plain radiographs. MRI with STIR sequences is definitive.

Transient osteoporosis of the hip: Acute pain with diffuse marrow edema on MRI, without structural abnormality, is characteristic.

Inflammatory arthropathy (ankylosing spondylitis, RA): Systemic symptoms, morning stiffness, elevated inflammatory markers.

## Treatment

## Non-Operative Management

Initial management of FAI is conservative in most patients. The components include: activity modification (avoidance of provoking positions and activities, with continuation of low-impact alternatives); physical therapy directed at hip range of motion, posterior chain strengthening, core stabilization, and gait retraining; non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications; and intra-articular corticosteroid injection for inflammatory flares.

The structured physiotherapy protocol developed by Enseki, Kemp and colleagues encompasses: (1) an unloading and pain-reduction phase (2–4 weeks) emphasizing isometric exercises and pain control; (2) a range-of-motion restoration phase, with selective avoidance of deep flexion and end-range internal rotation; (3) a neuromuscular control phase with core stabilization and abductor strengthening; (4) a functional restoration and return-to-sport phase in athletes. The success of non-operative management is variable — some patients achieve long-term symptom control, others progress to surgical treatment. Recent randomized trials (FIRST, FASHIoN, UK FASHIoN) have provided more definitive guidance on the timing of surgical intervention.

## Hip Arthroscopy

Hip arthroscopy has become the principal surgical approach for FAI management since the early 2000s. The procedure is performed with the patient supine on a traction table (or lateral with traction) under general anesthesia. Standard arthroscopy portals (anterolateral, anterior, mid-anterior, posterolateral) provide access to the central compartment (cotyloid fossa, ligamentum teres, central acetabulum, central femoral head) and the peripheral compartment (femoral head-neck junction, capsule, labrum).

## Positioning and Portals

The patient is placed supine on a traction table with a padded perineal post (minimum 9 cm diameter to reduce the risk of pudendal nerve compression) and foot holders. The contralateral limb is in abduction to allow C-arm access. Traction is applied gradually until 8–10 mm joint distraction is achieved under fluoroscopic control. Traction time should be limited to less than 2 hours to minimize the risk of pudendal nerve and peripheral sensory nerve injury.

The standard portals are: anterolateral (approximately 1 cm proximal and 1 cm anterior to the tip of the greater trochanter; the first portal, placed under fluoroscopic control); mid-anterior (approximately 7 cm distal and 2 cm lateral to the anterolateral portal); and accessory portals for distal anterolateral arthroscopy (DALA), posterolateral, and peripheral (for work in the peripheral compartment with traction released). Portal choice depends on the planned procedure and patient anatomy.

## Femoral Osteochondroplasty

Femoral osteochondroplasty — resection of the cam bump at the femoral head-neck junction to restore normal head-neck offset — is the most consequential component of cam-type FAI surgery. The procedure is performed in the peripheral compartment after traction release and dynamic impingement assessment. Resection is performed with a motorized bur (typically 4 or 5.5 mm) under direct arthroscopic vision and periodic fluoroscopic control.

Adequate resection is critical to both symptomatic relief and long-term success: insufficient resection produces persistent impingement and is a leading cause of revision; excessive resection risks femoral neck fracture (biomechanical studies show that resection exceeding 30% of neck diameter significantly increases fracture risk) and compromises the residual blood supply of the femoral head. The recommended resection depth aims to restore an alpha angle <40–45° on all radial cuts, with preservation of structural integrity of the neck. Intraoperative dynamic fluoroscopic assessment in multiple projections confirms the adequacy of resection.

## Acetabuloplasty and Rim Trimming

Acetabuloplasty (rim trimming) is resection of the over-prominent acetabular rim in pincer impingement. The procedure is performed in the central compartment with traction and requires prior takedown of the labrum to access the rim. After rim trimming, the labrum is reattached or refixed with suture anchors. The depth of resection is determined by the amount of overcoverage, typically 2–5 mm at the zone of maximum prominence, with the goal of achieving a center-edge angle of 25–35° (normal lateral coverage). Excessive rim trimming, particularly in borderline dysplasia, risks iatrogenic instability and must be carefully avoided.

## Labral Management — Repair vs Reconstruction

Labral tears are managed by anatomical repair when sufficient labral tissue is preserved. Suture anchors (typically 2.3–2.9 mm biocomposite or all-suture) are placed in the acetabular rim through dedicated cannulas with guide systems under direct arthroscopic vision. Suture configurations may be simple (around the labrum), looped vertical for tissue capture (loop-around), or base-stitches (without penetration of the labral substance to avoid iatrogenic damage to the chondrolabral junction). The goal is restoration of anatomical positioning of the labrum on the rim, of "sealing" contact with the femoral head, and of labral hydrodynamic function (maintenance of the intra-articular fluid layer).

Labral reconstruction is performed for irreparable cases: extensively degenerated or missing labrum, labral ossifications, failed prior repair. Described autografts include the iliotibial band (Philippon and colleagues), semitendinosus, and gracilis. Allografts (anterior tibialis tendon or peroneus longus) offer the advantages of no donor-site morbidity and are increasingly used in most centers. Segmental reconstruction replaces a focal missing portion of the labrum; circumferential reconstruction replaces the entire anterior labrum. Reported outcomes from large series (Philippon, Domb, Chahla) show comparable short- and mid-term functional outcomes to repair in well-selected cases.

## Capsular Management

The hip capsule is an important anatomical structure contributing to static and dynamic stability of the joint. The iliofemoral ligament is the strongest and most extensive capsular structure. Intra-articular access requires capsulotomy, typically performed as an interportal transverse capsulotomy (between the anterolateral and mid-anterior portals) or as a T-capsulotomy (with an additional vertical cut along the neck).

Capsular closure or plication at the end of the procedure is the standard in contemporary hip arthroscopy. Closure restores capsular tension, reduces the risk of postoperative instability and microinstability, and improves functional outcomes. Selective capsular plication (overlapping the capsular flaps to shorten the capsule) is performed in patients with borderline dysplasia, generalized joint hyperlaxity, or history of instability. Suture materials are nonabsorbable No. 2 (FiberWire, Orthocord) with simple or mattress sutures.

## Management of Associated Pathology

Arthroscopic FAI surgery often addresses associated pathology concurrently: loose bodies (removal), cartilage defects (managed by debridement, microfracture, or in selected cases autologous chondrocyte implantation or chondrocyte-matrix implants), ligamentum teres tears (debridement, or reconstruction in complete ruptures with instability), synovial pathology (synovectomy), and iliopsoas impingement (with fractional tendon release).

## Surgical Hip Dislocation (Ganz Approach)

The surgical hip dislocation, developed by Ganz and colleagues in the 1990s, provides open access to the entire hip joint with preservation of the femoral head vascular supply. The procedure involves: a posterior-based trochanteric flip osteotomy that preserves the attachment of the short external rotators (piriformis, obturator externus, quadratus femoris); capsulotomy with Z- or T-shaped incision with complete preservation of the medial femoral circumflex artery (the main blood supply to the femoral head); dislocation of the femoral head anteriorly; circumferential inspection and treatment of all intra-articular pathology; reduction; and refixation of the trochanteric osteotomy with 3.5 mm screws.

The approach allows comprehensive treatment of complex FAI, particularly in cases with severe deformity that cannot be adequately addressed arthroscopically, and is the gold standard for surgical training in hip preservation. The series of Ganz and Beck demonstrated a 0% rate of osteonecrosis with proper technique, confirming the vascular safety of the approach. The disadvantages include the morbidity of the open approach, the prolonged rehabilitation (with protected weight-bearing for 6–8 weeks), and the small risk of trochanteric osteotomy complications (nonunion 1–3%, migration).

## Periacetabular Osteotomy

The Bernese periacetabular osteotomy (PAO, Ganz osteotomy) is the procedure of choice for symptomatic acetabular dysplasia in the young adult, as discussed in the chapter on developmental dysplasia of the hip. In selected cases of FAI with dysplastic features, PAO is performed alone or in combination with arthroscopic treatment of the femoral cam to address both the under-coverage and the impingement. The reverse PAO was developed by Siebenrock as a treatment for severe acetabular retroversion — the retroverted acetabular fragment is rotated toward anteversion, correcting global pincer impingement.

## Choice Between Arthroscopy and Open Surgery

The choice between arthroscopic and open surgical approaches for FAI depends on multiple factors: the morphology of the deformity (severe global pincer impingement, complex multidirectional cam deformity, and extensive labral pathology may be better addressed by open surgery); the associated pathology (extensive labral reconstruction, complex chondral lesions, or associated dysplasia may favor open or combined approaches); the surgeon's experience; the patient's preference; and the available facilities. Hip arthroscopy is the dominant approach for typical FAI in most contemporary practice, with open surgical hip dislocation reserved for complex cases and for training surgeons in hip preservation.

## Complications of FAI Surgery

Hip arthroscopy has an overall complication rate of 1–5% in large series and registries. The principal complications include:

Neuropraxias: Transient pudendal nerve dysfunction (from traction against the perineal post; typically resolves within days to weeks), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (from proximity of the anterior portal; paresthesias and hypesthesia in the lateral thigh), and peroneal nerve (from the foot holder; rare). Minimized by limiting traction time (<2 h), use of padded large-diameter perineal posts, and careful foot positioning.

Heterotopic ossification: Occurs in 1–5% of patients; risk factors include male sex, extensive bony resection, and inadequate joint lavage. NSAID prophylaxis (indomethacin or COX-2 inhibitor) for 2–4 weeks postoperatively reduces the rate.

Femoral neck fracture: Rare (<0.5%) but catastrophic complication, typically related to excessive resection during cam osteochondroplasty. Requires emergency internal fixation.

Avascular necrosis of the femoral head: Extremely rare with well-performed arthroscopy, but described, particularly with aggressive peripheral work that compromises the medial femoral circumflex vessel.

Fluid extravasation: Egress of irrigation fluid into the peritoneal space or perineal tissues during prolonged operations or with drainage injuries. Requires urgent surgical decompression for abdominal compartment syndrome.

Adhesions and capsular contracture: Postoperative intra-articular adhesions can produce pain and restricted motion. Prevention includes early mobilization, range-of-motion exercises, and in persistent cases repeat arthroscopy for adhesion lysis.

Persistent impingement: The commonest cause of failure of primary surgery and need for revision. Linked to inadequate cam resection or uncorrected acetabular component.

Microinstability and iatrogenic instability: Postoperative capsular instability, particularly in patients with borderline dysplasia or hyperlaxity, if the capsule has not been adequately closed.

## Postoperative Rehabilitation

The standardized rehabilitation protocol after arthroscopy for FAI encompasses four phases:

Phase 1 (0–4 weeks): Protected phase. Partial weight-bearing (50% body weight) with crutches for 2–4 weeks after cam osteochondroplasty (to offload the resection zone); full weight-bearing after isolated labral or capsular treatment. Avoidance of passive extension beyond neutral and of end-range internal and external rotation. Isometric quadriceps and gluteal exercises. Stationary cycling without traction from week 1.

Phase 2 (4–8 weeks): Range-of-motion restoration. Full weight-bearing. Progressive restoration of passive and active range. Begin core stabilization, bridges, side planks.

Phase 3 (8–16 weeks): Strengthening and neuromuscular control. Progressive increase in exercise intensity. Begin closed-chain strengthening, single-leg balancing, light jogging from week 12.

Phase 4 (16+ weeks): Return to sport. Sport-specific training, plyometric exercises, functional return tests. Non-contact sports allow return at 4–6 months; contact and pivoting sports at 6–9 months.

## Outcomes

The outcomes of FAI surgery in well-selected patients are generally good. Multiple large series, randomized trials, and registry data have demonstrated meaningful improvements in pain and function for the majority of patients, with arthroscopic hip surgery showing modest to substantial improvements in patient-reported outcomes compared with non-operative management.

The proportion of patients who achieve substantial clinical improvement (defined as the minimal clinically important difference on iHOT-33, HOS-ADL, or mHHS) is approximately 70–85% in most series, with a failure rate (defined variably as conversion to total hip arthroplasty, re-operation, or unacceptable symptoms) of approximately 5–15% at 5-year follow-up. Mid-term (5–10 yr) outcomes remain stable in patients who achieve meaningful improvement at the 2-year mark; long-term (>10 yr) data are now maturing.

The principal predictors of poor outcome are: established hip osteoarthritis at the time of surgery (Tönnis grade 2 or higher; joint space less than 2 mm); older age (>50 years); female sex (in some series); associated dysplasia not corrected by simultaneous PAO; severe labral pathology not amenable to repair or reconstruction; and obesity. Patient selection has emerged as the principal determinant of outcome, with the recognition that FAI surgery is not curative in the patient who has already developed significant osteoarthritis.

## Randomized Controlled Trials

The past five years have seen several large randomized controlled trials comparing arthroscopic FAI surgery with conservative management:

UK FASHIoN (Griffin et al., Lancet 2018): 348 patients randomized to arthroscopic surgery (n=171) or standardized personalized physiotherapy (n=177). At 12 months, iHOT-33 (the primary outcome) was 58.8 in the surgical group vs 49.7 in the control group — a statistically significant difference of 6.8 points (above the minimal clinically important difference of 6.1). The results support a moderate superiority of surgery over structured physiotherapy in the medium term.

FIRST (Femoroacetabular Impingement Randomised controlled Trial; Ayeni et al., Canadian multicenter): Compared arthroscopic osteochondroplasty plus labral treatment with "lavage" arthroscopy (labral debridement without bony work). Demonstrated a significant advantage of complete FAI correction over debridement.

ANCHOR (Swedish–Danish registry study): Large registry cohort demonstrating substantial improvement in patient-reported outcomes after arthroscopic surgery with durability over time.

These randomized trials strengthen the recommendation for surgical treatment in patients with symptomatic FAI in whom structured conservative treatment has failed for 3–6 months.

The long-term effect of FAI surgery on the progression to osteoarthritis remains under investigation. Several observational studies have suggested a delay in arthritis progression in patients who undergo successful FAI surgery, but a definitive demonstration of disease-modifying effect awaits longer follow-up and the maturation of ongoing randomized trials.

## Special Considerations

## FAI in Specific Populations

Athletes: FAI is particularly common in athletes engaged in pivoting sports (football, hockey, basketball), with return to sport rates after surgery of 80–90% in many series. The timing of return is typically 4–6 months for non-contact sports and 6–9 months for contact sports. Elite athletes require an individualized approach and close collaboration with sports medicine.

Adolescent athletes: The recognition of cam-type morphology developing during the adolescent growth spurt in athletes has raised questions about preventive measures and the role of early surgery. Current consensus is that surgery is reserved for symptomatic disease, with morphological abnormalities alone not warranting surgical intervention.

Post-SCFE patients: Patients with healed SCFE have a high rate of cam-type FAI with the residual deformity from the original slip. Arthroscopic osteochondroplasty, sometimes combined with proximal femoral osteotomy (intertrochanteric Imhäuser osteotomy) for severe deformity, addresses the residual impingement.

Post-Perthes patients: Healed Perthes disease produces a variable combination of cam (from the deformed femoral head), pincer (from the dysplastic acetabulum), and dysplasia. Treatment is individualized and may combine arthroscopic osteochondroplasty with PAO or femoral osteotomy. In complex cases, relative femoral neck lengthening (described by Ganz and Leunig) through surgical dislocation is considered.

## Borderline Acetabular Dysplasia

The patient with borderline acetabular dysplasia (center-edge angle 20–25°) presents a particularly challenging clinical situation, with features of both dysplasia and FAI. Aggressive labral debridement or rim trimming in this population can produce iatrogenic instability with disastrous outcomes. Modern management favors gentle labral repair without rim trimming, combined with capsular plication; PAO is considered for patients with predominantly dysplastic features, particularly when center-edge angle <20° or acetabular index >15°.

## Hip Microinstability

Hip microinstability — a syndrome of subtle multidirectional hip instability without frank dislocation — is increasingly recognized as a cause of hip pain that overlaps with FAI. Clinical features include women with generalized joint hyperlaxity (positive Beighton score), active dancers and gymnasts, and patients with dysplasia. Symptoms include anterior pain in certain positions, sensation of "giving way," and snapping. Treatment combines physical therapy with emphasis on capsular stabilization and abductor strengthening, capsular plication at arthroscopy, and avoidance of capsular release or wide capsular dissection.

## Local Contributions

Within the regional orthopaedic literature, observations on FAI and hip preservation surgery have been published by several European groups.

**Bulgaria.** Orthopaedic teams in Sofia and Burgas have reported cases of arthroscopic treatment of labral tears in young patients, including national-team athletes. The Department of Orthopaedics and Traumatology at University Hospital "St. Ivan Rilski," Sofia, introduced hip arthroscopy into clinical practice in the mid-2010s, with emphasis on labral repair and cam osteoplasty. Bulgarian-language review articles have addressed the imaging diagnosis of FAI with particular attention to standardization of the AP pelvis view.

**Germany.** German groups in Heidelberg (Aldinger), Munich (Müller), and Dresden (the Tönnis school) have made substantial contributions to hip preservation surgery, publishing on long-term outcomes of PAO, proximal femoral osteotomy, and combined procedures for complex deformities.

**Greece.** The Athens orthopaedic centre has published series on the arthroscopic management of FAI in athletes and on the association with pubalgia in footballers.

**Romania.** The Bucharest university orthopaedic centre introduced hip arthroscopy on a national scale and published supervised series of early functional outcomes.

**Serbia.** The Belgrade Institute of Orthopaedics has worked on diagnostic algorithms for FAI in young athletes and on the integration of physiotherapy and surgery.

**Switzerland.** The Canton of Bern (Ganz, Leunig, Siebenrock, Beck, Steppacher) remains the international centre for the conceptual development of FAI and for the major operative techniques: formalization of the surgical dislocation, the Bernese PAO, the reverse PAO, relative femoral neck lengthening, and the cartilage and labral injury classifications. The Zurich group (Dora, Naal) has contributed to development of the minimally invasive approaches and the functional outcome instruments.

## Summary and Take-Home Points

Femoroacetabular impingement is a clinical syndrome in which abnormal mechanical contact between the proximal femur and acetabular rim produces progressive damage to the labrum, chondrolabral junction, and underlying cartilage. The morphological subtypes — cam impingement from aspherical femoral head-neck junction, pincer impingement from acetabular overcoverage, and combined impingement — are diagnosed by characteristic radiographic features (alpha angle, crossover sign, center-edge angle, ischial spine sign) and confirmed by MR arthrography. The clinical presentation is typically an active young adult with activity-related groin pain, decreased hip internal rotation in flexion, and positive anterior impingement test. Treatment is initially conservative with activity modification, physical therapy, and selective intra-articular injection; surgical management — predominantly by hip arthroscopy with femoral osteochondroplasty, acetabular rim trimming, and labral repair or reconstruction; less commonly by open surgical hip dislocation (Ganz approach) or by periacetabular osteotomy for associated dysplasia — addresses the underlying morphology and produces meaningful improvement in 70–85% of well-selected patients. Patient selection is the principal determinant of outcome, with established osteoarthritis being the strongest predictor of failure. Randomized controlled trials (UK FASHIoN, FIRST) strengthen the recommendation for surgical treatment in patients with persistent symptoms despite structured conservative management. The recognition of FAI as a major cause of pre-arthritic hip disease and a contributor to the development of hip osteoarthritis has transformed the orthopaedic understanding of hip pain in young adults over the past two decades.
