## Introduction

Coxarthrosis — osteoarthritis of the hip joint — is one of the commonest and most disabling musculoskeletal conditions in adults and one of the major single causes of lost quality-adjusted years of life in developed societies. The disease produces progressive pain, stiffness, and functional limitation, and ultimately reduces quality of life to the point where total hip arthroplasty (THA) becomes the most cost-effective intervention in modern orthopedic surgery. Understanding of hip osteoarthritis has evolved substantially over the last three decades: the great majority of cases previously classified as "idiopathic" or "primary" osteoarthritis are in fact secondary to subtle developmental abnormalities of the hip — femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), mild acetabular dysplasia, healed Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease, slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) — that produce abnormal joint mechanics over decades and lead to progressive cartilage breakdown. This chapter synthesizes content from Callaghan, Rosenberg & Rubash *The Adult Hip* (3rd edition), Berry & Lieberman *Surgery of the Hip*, Pauwels *Biomechanics of the Normal and Diseased Hip*, Tönnis *Congenital Dysplasia and Dislocation of the Hip* and Alshryda et al. *The Pediatric and Adolescent Hip*. The chapter addresses the historical perspective, epidemiology and risk factors, biomechanics, pathophysiology, clinical features, imaging, classifications, non-operative management, joint-preserving surgery, and total hip arthroplasty for advanced disease, with a concluding section on local contributions ordered by country.

## Historical Perspective

Hip surgery for arthritis has a rich and instructive history spanning more than 130 years. The first interventions for severe hip pain in the nineteenth century were arthrodeses and resection arthroplasties; interposition arthroplasties using autologous or xenogeneic tissues (muscle, fascia, pig skin, gold foil) had limited and unpredictable success. Smith-Petersen introduced a glass cup over the femoral head in 1923, later refined to a vitallium "mould arthroplasty" in 1938; the procedure was popular for thirty years but produced modest results. Femoral stems for hemiarthroplasty were developed by Moore and Thompson in the 1950s and retain a role today in selected femoral neck fractures in the elderly. Total hip arthroplasty in its modern form begins with Sir John Charnley at Wrightington (England) in 1958–1962 with the concept of "low friction arthroplasty," combining a small femoral head (22.225 mm), polymethyl methacrylate bone cement for fixation, and a polyethylene bearing surface. The Charnley principles — biomechanical analysis of the joint, cement fixation, low-friction bearing — remain fundamental. Maurice Müller of Bern introduced the modified concept and the Müller stem (1960s). Pauwels of Aachen published his monumental work on hip biomechanics (1973–1976), which to this day defines the way surgeons understand load distribution. Uncemented fixation was developed by Lord (France), Engh, Mallory, Galante (USA), and Zweymüller (Austria) through the 1970s–1980s. Reinhold Ganz and colleagues at Bern introduced the periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) in 1984. The concept of FAI as a cause of osteoarthritis was developed by the Ganz group in publications between 2001 and 2003 and has transformed the understanding of the pathogenesis of "idiopathic" coxarthrosis.

## Epidemiology and Risk Factors

The prevalence of symptomatic hip osteoarthritis is approximately 5–10% of adults over the age of 60, rising markedly with age and approaching 20% in those over 80. The lifetime risk of symptomatic hip osteoarthritis is approximately 25%. Worldwide, more than one million total hip arthroplasties have been performed annually over the last decade, with a rate of growth outpacing demographic change.

Risk factors include: increasing age; female sex (slight predominance in symptomatic disease); obesity (the association is less strong for the hip than for the knee, reflecting the deeper joint and the role of other factors); occupational and recreational exposure to heavy loading, jumping, and repetitive impact (farmers, miners, construction workers, ballet dancers, professional athletes); prior hip trauma (especially acetabular fractures and hip dislocations); prior hip disorders in childhood and adolescence (Legg-Calvé-Perthes, SCFE, developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), FAI, septic arthritis of the hip); inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis); avascular necrosis of the femoral head; and strong genetic predisposition (a substantial heritable component is established by twin and family studies, with a relative risk of 2–3 in first-degree relatives).

The "primary" versus "secondary" classification of hip osteoarthritis is increasingly recognized as a false dichotomy: with careful radiographic and clinical assessment, most "primary" cases reveal an underlying predisposing condition. Cam-type FAI (an aspherical femoral head with a "pistol-grip" deformity of the head-neck junction) is the most commonly recognized predisposing morphology in men with hip osteoarthritis, while acetabular dysplasia is the principal predisposition in women. Pincer-type FAI (acetabular over-coverage, retroversion, coxa profunda) is less strongly associated with progression to severe arthritis. The Bern group (Ganz and colleagues) has proposed the "triad of pathogenesis" of hip osteoarthritis — dysplasia, impingement, and combined forms — as mutually exclusive categories for the majority of "idiopathic" cases.

## Hip Biomechanics

The biomechanical principles of the hip joint were formulated systematically by Friedrich Pauwels (1885–1980) of Aachen. Pauwels developed the concept of the hip as a first-class lever with the fulcrum at the centre of the femoral head, in which body weight (applied medially to the axis) is balanced by the abductor muscle force (applied laterally, with a much shorter lever arm). This explains why the hip joint reactive force during one-legged stance amounts to about three times body weight (BW), peaking at 4 BW during walking and up to 5 BW during running. Pauwels' insight defines several key concepts:

First, the abductor mechanism is central to normal hip function; its disruption — by weakness, pain inhibition, or anatomical shortening of the lever — produces the Trendelenburg gait and increases joint loading.

Second, a cane held in the contralateral hand reduces hip joint reactive force by 20–40% because it creates a counterweight to body weight, unloading the abductor mechanism.

Third, surgical interventions that modify the abductor lever arm (trochanteric osteotomy with lateralization, joint-preserving procedures, neck lengthening at THA) improve biomechanical efficiency and may reduce pain and wear. Pauwels developed "intertrochanteric osteotomies" (varus, valgus, and combined with medialization) as joint-preserving procedures that, in the pre-THA era, offered substantial improvement and remain an option today in specific morphological abnormalities.

Fourth, the orientation of the main trabeculae in the femoral head and neck (Ward's medial and lateral trabecular systems) reflects load distribution and changes predictably in pathological states, providing radiographic clues to underlying biomechanics.

Fifth, in arthritis the concentrated load in the superolateral quadrant of the joint (where the superior femoral head contacts the acetabular roof) produces progressive cartilage loss precisely in this zone — the basis for the radiographic finding of superolateral joint-space narrowing as the earliest sign.

## Pathophysiology

The pathological process of hip osteoarthritis follows the general principles of osteoarthritis at any joint. The earliest changes occur in the cartilage matrix, with disruption of the proteoglycan–collagen architecture, increased water content, surface fibrillation, and progressive loss of cartilage thickness. The chondrocytes initially attempt repair through increased synthetic activity, with matrix metalloproteinases, aggrecanases, and collagenase activity in imbalance with their natural inhibitors (TIMPs). Cytokine signalling — interleukin-1β, TNF-α, IL-6 — sustains the inflammatory state and chondrocyte dysfunction. Eventually the chondrocytes undergo apoptosis, leaving an acellular fibrous tissue that progressively breaks down.

The exposed subchondral bone responds with increased density (sclerosis), formation of subchondral cysts (geodes) from synovial fluid intrusion through micro-cracks in the subchondral plate, and peripheral osteophyte formation at the joint margins through endochondral ossification of the marginal cartilage. The synovium becomes inflamed with low-grade chronic synovitis, contributing to symptoms; synovial macrophages and infiltrating lymphocytes produce pro-inflammatory cytokines that sustain matrix breakdown. The capsule thickens and contracts, producing progressive limitation of motion — particularly internal rotation, the most sensitive examination measurement for hip joint pathology. In late disease, complete loss of cartilage with bone-on-bone contact, marked osteophyte formation, femoral head migration, and joint deformity produces the characteristic radiographic and clinical features of end-stage disease.

Specific to the hip, the pathogenetic pathway "dysplasia → impingement → arthritis" is central. In acetabular dysplasia with insufficient femoral head coverage, the reactive force concentrates over a smaller area of acetabular cartilage, producing increased contact stress, premature cartilage loss, and progressive lateralization of the femoral head. In cam-FAI, the aspherical superolateral head-neck transition produces repetitive abnormal bony contact with the acetabular rim during flexion and internal rotation, leading to peripheral chondrolabral damage, progressive delamination of the acetabular cartilage from the subchondral bone, and eventually global arthritis.

## Clinical Features

### History

The classical presentation is of insidious onset of groin pain, typically in middle age or later, often poorly localized initially. The pain may radiate to the buttock, the lateral thigh, the anterior thigh, or — characteristically — the medial knee through the obturator nerve. This "referred pain to the knee" is a well-known pitfall that often leads to delayed diagnosis when initial work-up is directed only at the knee. The "C-sign" — the patient grips the hip with an open hand forming the letter C between thumb and index finger, with the thumb over the trochanter and the index finger anteriorly in the groin — is almost pathognomonic of intra-articular hip pain.

The classical pattern of mechanical pain — worse with activity and weight-bearing, better with rest — gives way over months to years to "rest pain" with continued discomfort even at rest, and ultimately to night pain and pain at all times. The onset of night pain is an important prognostic marker and often the decisive symptom that leads the patient to total hip arthroplasty.

Stiffness, particularly of internal rotation, develops progressively and is among the most reliable functional indicators of disease progression. Functional limitations follow predictable patterns: difficulty putting on socks and shoes (especially on the affected side), difficulty climbing stairs (especially descending), difficulty getting in and out of low chairs or cars, difficulty cutting toenails, limp, progressive reduction of walking distance, and need for a cane. Standardised functional scores — Harris Hip Score, WOMAC, Oxford Hip Score, HOOS — provide quantitative tracking of these changes.

### Physical Examination

Examination findings progress with disease severity. The classical findings of established hip osteoarthritis include:

- Antalgic gait (with reduced stance phase on the affected side);
- Trendelenburg gait or lateral lurch from abductor weakness or inhibition by pain;
- Decreased range of motion, with internal rotation typically affected first and most severely, followed by flexion and abduction; the end-stage hip may be completely blocked in internal rotation (loss of 90° or more);
- Positive Thomas test for fixed flexion deformity (the affected hip remains flexed when the contralateral hip is fully flexed);
- Positive flexion-abduction-external rotation (FABER, Patrick) test producing groin pain;
- Positive FADIR test (flexion-adduction-internal rotation) — especially with underlying FAI;
- Apparent leg-length discrepancy with shortening of the affected side;
- Tenderness over the anterior hip joint.

In the end stage the hip is held in fixed flexion, adduction, and external rotation, with severe limitation of all motion. The contralateral hip often shows compensatory hypermobility, and the lumbar spine compensatory hyperlordosis or a fixed sagittal deformity (important for the concept of "spinopelvic balance" in THA planning).

## Imaging

### Plain Radiographs

The standard imaging assessment is bilateral standing AP pelvis and lateral views of the hip (cross-table lateral or Lauenstein "frog-leg"). When planning joint-preserving surgery or THA, additional views are added: Lequesne false-profile, Dunn views at 45° or 90° flexion for head-neck assessment (important in FAI), and on indication functional views (extension–abduction–external rotation for congruency assessment).

The features of hip osteoarthritis follow the universal radiographic criteria of osteoarthritis with hip-specific manifestations:

1. Joint-space narrowing, typically asymmetric with the superior aspect of the joint (between the superior femoral head and the acetabular roof) most commonly involved early in disease;
2. Subchondral sclerosis of both the femoral head and the acetabular roof;
3. Subchondral cyst formation (geodes) in both the femoral head and acetabulum, often at the base of the head (cyst of Brown) and in the acetabular roof;
4. Osteophyte formation at the joint margins — particularly the superior acetabular rim, the inferior femoral head and neck, the medial acetabular floor (buttress osteophyte), and the cranial pole of the femoral head;
5. Loss of femoral head sphericity with progressive cartilage and bone destruction;
6. Migration of the femoral head — typically superior or superolateral in idiopathic OA, medial in protrusio acetabuli, medial/protrusion in rheumatoid arthritis.

Additional plain radiographic assessments include: Tönnis acetabular angle (normal <10°; >10° increasing dysplasia); LCEA (lateral center-edge angle of Wiberg, normal >25°; 20–25° borderline dysplasia; <20° dysplasia; >40° pincer impingement); acetabular depth index; crossover sign and posterior wall sign for acetabular retroversion; head-neck alpha angle (normal <55°; >60° cam morphology) on the Dunn view; femoral head offset; and analysis of coxoprofile dimensions (offset, neck length, neck-shaft angle) in preoperative THA planning.

### MRI and Other Advanced Imaging

MRI is not routinely required for the diagnosis of hip osteoarthritis but is useful in:

- Early disease where plain radiographs may be normal but symptoms suggest joint pathology;
- Assessment of associated FAI and labral pathology;
- Evaluation for alternative diagnoses (avascular necrosis, occult fracture, stress fracture of the femoral head or neck, septic arthritis, synovial chondromatosis, pigmented villonodular synovitis, neoplasm);
- Preoperative planning in complex deformities or revision surgery (cartilage mapping, soft-tissue assessment).

MR arthrography with intra-articular gadolinium contrast identifies labral tears, chondral defects, and loose bodies more sensitively than non-arthrographic MRI and is preferred in the assessment of pre-arthritic disease (especially in young patients with symptoms compatible with FAI or labral tears).

Specialized cartilage-sensitive MRI sequences — dGEMRIC (delayed gadolinium-enhanced MRI of cartilage), T2 mapping, T1-rho mapping — provide quantitative assessment of cartilage biochemical composition (glycosaminoglycan concentration, collagen integrity, water content) and are used principally in research for early detection of cartilage degeneration.

CT with three-dimensional reconstruction and patient-specific models is used in complex deformities, preoperative planning of osteotomies or THA with severe dysplasia, and in revision surgery for the analysis of acetabular and femoral defects.

## Classification

### Kellgren–Lawrence Classification

The Kellgren–Lawrence classification (1957) is the standard radiographic grading, applicable to all joints and widely used in epidemiological and clinical studies:

- Grade 0: normal radiograph;
- Grade 1: doubtful joint-space narrowing and possible osteophyte;
- Grade 2: definite osteophyte with possible joint-space narrowing;
- Grade 3: multiple osteophytes with definite joint-space narrowing, sclerosis, and possible deformity;
- Grade 4: large osteophytes, marked joint-space narrowing, severe sclerosis, and definite deformity of the head.

### Tönnis Classification

Tönnis grading is a similar 0–3 system specifically developed for the hip and widely used in Europe:

- Grade 0: no signs of osteoarthritis;
- Grade 1: mild changes — increased sclerosis of the head and acetabulum, mild joint-space narrowing, mild lateral joint-margin lipping;
- Grade 2: moderate changes — small cysts in the head and/or acetabulum, moderate joint-space narrowing, moderate loss of head sphericity;
- Grade 3: severe changes — large cysts, severe joint-space narrowing or obliteration, severe head deformity, signs of necrosis.

Tönnis grading is often preferred in surgical planning — particularly for joint-preserving procedures, where Tönnis 0–1 is a condition for good results of PAO or arthroscopy, while Tönnis 2 and 3 generally contraindicate joint-preserving surgery.

### Crowe Classification for Dysplasia

The Crowe classification (1979) quantitatively grades the severity of proximal femoral head migration in dysplasia:

- I: <50% subluxation;
- II: 50–75%;
- III: 75–100%;
- IV: >100% (complete dislocation, the femoral head sits above the acetabular pole).

The classification guides the choice of surgical technique in THA for the dysplastic hip — particularly the need for a femoral shortening osteotomy (Crowe III–IV) and for bone graft or specific acetabular components.

### Hartofilakidis Classification

An alternative classification of the dysplastic hip in adults (Hartofilakidis, 1996):

- A (dysplasia): the head articulates with the true acetabulum but with insufficient coverage;
- B (low dislocation): the head articulates with a false acetabulum that partially overlaps the true one;
- C (high dislocation): the head articulates with a false acetabulum completely separate from the true one.

### Paprosky Classification

In revision THA, the Paprosky classification of acetabular and femoral bone defects guides reconstructive planning:

Acetabulum:

- I: intact rims;
- IIA: superomedial defect <2 cm;
- IIB: superolateral defect <2 cm;
- IIC: medial defect through the Köhler line;
- IIIA: large superior defect with upward migration <3 cm;
- IIIB: severe defect with migration >3 cm and pelvic discontinuity.

Femur:

- I: minimal metaphyseal damage, intact diaphysis;
- II: severe metaphyseal damage, intact diaphysis;
- IIIA: severe meta- and proximal diaphyseal loss with intact distal diaphysis >4 cm;
- IIIB: extended diaphyseal involvement, <4 cm distal diaphysis for fixation;
- IV: widely extended damage with overexpanded and thin bone.

## Non-Operative Management

The non-operative management of hip osteoarthritis combines patient education, lifestyle modification, pharmacological treatment, and specific physical therapy interventions. Current OARSI (2019), ACR (2019) and EULAR guidelines summarize the evidence.

### Patient Education

Education addresses the chronicity and natural history of the disease, expectations for treatment, and the importance of physical activity and weight management. The educated patient is more likely to participate actively in treatment, maintain realistic expectations of interventions, and defer THA until it is truly indicated.

### Weight Reduction

Weight loss in overweight or obese patients reduces both pain and the rate of progression, with documented benefit per kilogram of weight lost. Targets are modest but consistent reductions sustained over years — typically 5–10% of baseline body weight. Bariatric surgery in morbid obesity is an additional option, with documented improvement of joint symptoms and improved candidacy for THA.

### Physical Therapy

Physical therapy combines range-of-motion exercises (preventing further loss of motion), strengthening of the hip abductors and quadriceps, and core stabilization. Aquatic exercise (hydrotherapy) is particularly useful in patients with significant pain or weight-bearing limitations, because hydrostatic pressure reduces land-based loading while offering resistance for strengthening. Evidence-based exercise programmes (GLAD, Good Life with osteoArthritis in Denmark) are structured 6–12 week courses.

### Activity Modification

Activity modification addresses the avoidance of high-impact and pivoting activities, the use of assistive devices (cane held in the opposite hand reduces hip joint reaction force by 20–40%, per the Pauwels principle), and progressive return to lower-impact activities — swimming, stationary cycling, low-level aerobics, tai chi.

### Pharmacological Treatment

Pharmacological treatment follows the standard OA stepwise approach:

- Paracetamol (acetaminophen) for mild symptoms (with limited efficacy in current meta-analyses);
- Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) — oral or topical — for moderate symptoms, with attention to cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and renal risks; selective COX-2 inhibitors in patients with high GI risk;
- Duloxetine — a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor with an approved indication for chronic musculoskeletal pain;
- Opioids reserved for severe symptoms unresponsive to other measures, with awareness of the long-term consequences of opioid use (dependence, tolerance, hyperalgesia, increased mortality);
- Intra-articular corticosteroid injection for inflammatory flares (with caution about the cumulative effects of repeated injections, which may damage cartilage; the recent FDA caution about cartilage loss with repeated intra-articular steroid therapy has tempered enthusiasm; the injection is performed mandatorily under ultrasound or fluoroscopic guidance because of the deep anatomy of the hip);
- Hyaluronic acid injection (less commonly used in the hip than in the knee, with mixed evidence for efficacy);
- Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection and other biological injections — under continued investigation, with promising but unconfirmed results.

### Nutritional Supplements

Glucosamine and chondroitin are widely used by patients but have inconsistent evidence for symptom relief and no clearly established effect on disease progression; current major guidelines (OARSI, ACR) do not recommend routine use, nor do they contraindicate it.

## Joint-Preserving Surgery

For appropriate younger patients with pre-arthritic or early arthritic hip disease, joint-preserving surgical procedures aim to correct the underlying mechanical abnormality and delay or prevent the progression to end-stage osteoarthritis. The choice of procedure depends on the underlying morphology, the severity of cartilage damage, and the patient's age and activity demands.

### Hip Arthroscopy

Hip arthroscopy is applied in FAI, labral tears, loose bodies, and selected chondral lesions. The procedure is performed in the supine (or lateral) position with limb traction. Standard portals include the anterolateral, anterior (modified mid-anterior), and peritrochanteric portals. Aims include femoroplasty (removal of the cam morphology), acetabuloplasty (rim trim or ostectomy of the pincer morphology), labral repair or reconstruction, and addressing chondral lesions (microfracture, AMIC). The role in established osteoarthritis is limited, with poor outcomes when significant cartilage loss is present (Tönnis ≥2). Arthroscopy is discussed in detail in the FAI chapter.

### Periacetabular Osteotomy (Bernese / Ganz PAO)

Periacetabular osteotomy is applied for symptomatic acetabular dysplasia in the young adult (typically 15–40 years) with preserved joint space (Tönnis 0–1) and a congruent joint. The procedure, developed by Reinhold Ganz and colleagues in Bern (1984), involves three osteotomy cuts — iliac, ischial, and pubic — that isolate the acetabular fragment while preserving the posterior column for pelvic stability. The fragment is rotated for improved coverage (increasing LCEA to 30–40° and reducing the Tönnis acetabular angle to 0–10°) and fixed with screws. Expected benefits include durable improvement in pain and function and delay of progression to THA. In the original Ganz series and in long-term data from Bern, Boston (Murphy, Millis), Iowa (Clohisy), and Chiba (Naito), 20-year survivorship free of THA is 60–80%. Complications include acetabular fragment necrosis (rare with proper technique), posterior column disruption, sciatic or femoral nerve injury, thromboembolic events, and heterotopic ossification. PAO is discussed in detail in the DDH chapter.

### Femoral Osteotomy

Proximal femoral osteotomy — varus, valgus, extension, flexion, rotational, or combinations — is applied for specific morphological abnormalities. Pauwels designed the intertrochanteric varus osteotomy for coxa valga and varus for coxa vara to optimize joint congruency and redistribute load. Sugioka described a transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy for AVN of the femoral head, applied principally in Japan. Today femoral osteotomies in arthritis are rarely used in the West, primarily for residual deformity after Perthes disease (with coxa magna and low offset), SCFE (with chronic retroversion of the head relative to the neck), and in selected cases of coxa vara or valga. Results are variable and depend substantially on patient selection.

### Hip Resurfacing Arthroplasty

Hip resurfacing arthroplasty is a partial joint replacement that preserves the proximal femoral bone stock by replacing only the articular surfaces of the femoral head and acetabulum. Birmingham Hip Resurfacing (McMinn, 1997) and Conserve Plus (Wright, 1996) are the most widely used implants. The procedure had a resurgence in the 2000s but has been substantially curtailed by recognition of the complications of metal-on-metal bearing surfaces (cobalt-chromium ion release, adverse local tissue reactions (ALTR), pseudotumor formation, elevated blood and urine metal ion concentrations). The procedure remains an option in selected young active male patients with appropriate anatomy (especially with a large femoral head), but its use is now restricted to specialist centres with continuing interest. Women and patients with smaller heads have significantly higher revision rates and are not candidates for resurfacing under most current guidelines.

## Total Hip Arthroplasty (THA)

### Indications

Total hip arthroplasty is indicated for end-stage hip osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence 3–4, Tönnis 2–3) producing symptoms unresponsive to adequate non-operative management, with attention to the patient's functional demands, comorbidities, life expectancy, and bone quality. THA is also performed for advanced rheumatoid arthritis, post-traumatic arthritis (after acetabular fractures, femoral neck fractures), avascular necrosis, ankylosing spondylitis with hip involvement, and other causes of end-stage hip destruction.

Classical indications include: persistent night pain, rest pain, failure of conservative management over a sufficient period (typically 3–6 months), significant functional limitation (PROMs — Harris Hip Score <70, Oxford Hip Score <30), and radiographic findings correlating with clinical severity. Age is no longer considered an absolute contraindication (THA is performed in both 50-year-olds and 90-year-olds with appropriate clinical judgement), but the young patient demands particular attention to outcome durability and the potential for future revision surgery.

### Implant Design Considerations

Modern THA implants combine four principal components: a femoral stem, a femoral head, an acetabular cup, and an acetabular liner (bearing surface).

Femoral stem: cemented or uncemented. Cemented stems (including the polished Charnley-type, the polished tapered Exeter-type, and the rough anatomic Stanmore-type) were the historic standard and remain preferred in Scandinavia and the United Kingdom, particularly in elderly patients. Uncemented stems (proximally porous coated, fully porous coated, hydroxyapatite-coated, tapered wedge, fit-and-fill, modular) are dominant in the USA and in most of Europe. Modern shorter anatomic stems and neck-preserving stems (Mayo, Metha, SMF, Optimys) preserve more proximal femoral bone for younger patients. Results from modern cemented and uncemented stems are comparable with appropriate selection.

Femoral head: typically 28–40 mm diameter. Larger heads (36 mm and above) provide greater articular stability and reduced dislocation rates, especially in posterior approach. Smaller heads (28 mm) have better tribological properties with low-quality XLPE, but today most surgeons prefer 32–36 mm with XLPE. The material is ceramic (alumina, zirconia-toughened alumina ZTA — e.g. Delta) or cobalt-chromium. The bi-modular head (an adapter for adjustment of length and offset) is a technical advantage but contributed to certain complications (trunnion fretting and corrosion).

Acetabular cup: cemented (now rarely used in most Western countries) or uncemented (dominant choice). Uncemented cups are hemispherical metal shells (titanium alloy or cobalt-chromium) with porous or plasma coating for bony integration, often plus hydroxyapatite coating. Sizes range from 44 to 70 mm and above. Supplementary screw fixation is an option when initial stability is poor.

Acetabular liner: the bearing surface, with several options:

- Polyethylene (today almost exclusively highly cross-linked — XLPE — with or without vitamin E for additional oxidative stability);
- Ceramic (alumina, ZTA);
- Metal (cobalt-chromium, today almost exclusively abandoned outside resurfacing);
- Dual mobility — a system with a small inner head articulating with a larger mobile polyethylene liner that articulates with a metal or ceramic cup; substantially reduces dislocation risk and is preferred in high-risk patients.

### Bearing Surfaces

The choice of bearing surface is one of the central decisions in modern THA.

Metal-on-conventional-polyethylene was the historic standard from Charnley onward, but had high rates of late osteolysis from polyethylene wear particles leading to aseptic loosening.

Metal-on-highly-cross-linked-polyethylene (XLPE) — introduced in the late 1990s — substantially reduced wear rates (below 0.1 mm/year versus 0.1–0.2 mm/year for conventional polyethylene) and is now the standard for most patients.

Ceramic-on-XLPE provides further wear reduction and better biocompatibility of the wear particles, and is preferred in younger patients.

Ceramic-on-ceramic provides the lowest wear of all bearings (on the order of <0.01 mm/year), but carries small risks of squeaking (1–3%), ceramic fracture (<<1%), and does not allow dual-mobility liners.

Metal-on-metal bearings (with cobalt-chromium surfaces) had a brief popularity in the 2000s but have been substantially withdrawn from practice because of the systemic and local effects of metal ion release. A substantial body of literature documents adverse local tissue reactions, pseudotumor formation, elevated serum cobalt and chromium concentrations, and the failure of large-head metal-on-metal implants. Regulatory agencies (FDA, MHRA, EMA) have issued warnings and surveillance guidelines for patients with metal-on-metal implants.

### Surgical Approaches

Several surgical approaches to the hip are used for THA, each with advantages and disadvantages:

Posterior approach (Moore, Southern, Kocher–Langenbeck): The most widely used worldwide. The approach splits the gluteus maximus along its fibres, identifies and protects the sciatic nerve, divides the short external rotators (piriformis, obturators, gemelli, quadratus femoris) — which are repaired at the end of the procedure — and exposes the hip through a capsulotomy. Advantages include excellent visualization, extensile capacity for revision surgery, avoidance of disruption to the abductor mechanism, and a short learning curve. The principal historical disadvantage has been the relatively higher rate of dislocation, but this has been substantially reduced by careful repair of the short external rotators and capsule, larger femoral heads, and dual mobility.

Anterolateral approach (Watson-Jones, Hardinge): Approaches the hip between the tensor fasciae latae and the gluteus medius (Watson-Jones) or by splitting the gluteus medius (Hardinge). Lower dislocation rate than posterior but with potential abductor weakness and sometimes persistent limping (Hardinge limp). The Hardinge approach was developed by Kevin Hardinge in Sheffield (1982) and remains popular in the United Kingdom and Scandinavia.

Direct anterior approach (Smith-Petersen, Hueter): The approach uses the internervous plane between the sartorius and tensor fasciae latae (laterally) and the rectus femoris (medially). Marius Smith-Petersen described the approach in 1917; in modern THA Joel Matta popularized the direct anterior approach with a traction table through the 1990s–2000s. Advantages include lower dislocation rate, faster initial recovery, the ability to perform the procedure supine with intraoperative radiographic confirmation of leg length and implant position, and potential cosmetic benefit (smaller scar). Disadvantages include the learning curve (substantial — typically 50–100 cases for stable outcomes), the risk of lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury (paresthesias in 10–15% of patients, often transient), increased risk of periprosthetic femoral fracture with shorter stems, and the more limited extensile capacity for revision surgery. The direct anterior approach has grown rapidly in popularity since the early 2000s and is now performed by a substantial fraction of modern hip surgeons, especially in the USA and France.

Other approaches include: direct lateral (Bauer modification of Hardinge), super-lateral (Berger micro-incision), posterolateral mini-incision (Tampa Bay), and two-portal (anterior + lateral). Minimally invasive (MIS) techniques are under continued evaluation, with initial promising but ambiguous long-term results.

### Specific Surgical Techniques

The technical principles of THA include:

- Appropriate preoperative templating of implant sizes and positions (Astoff or MediCAD templates, digital templates on calibrated radiographs);
- Meticulous attention to acetabular cup orientation (target 40° inclination, 15° anteversion in most cases, with patient-specific adjustments);
- Attention to femoral stem positioning (anteversion 10–20°, neutral varus/valgus, appropriate size and press-fit for uncemented) or cementing technique for cemented stems (dense 2–3 mm cement mantle, third-generation cementing techniques);
- Careful restoration of leg length and offset (with intraoperative measurement — pin or marking methods over the pelvis and trochanter);
- Precise component reduction;
- Intraoperative stability assessment (telescoping, dropkick test, stability in positions at risk of dislocation).

The Lewinnek "safe zone" for cup position (30–50° inclination, 5–25° anteversion) has been refined to more individualized concepts of "functional anteversion" that account for spinopelvic mobility and the resting position of the pelvis in different functional activities. Hypo- and hypermobile spinopelvic transitions (especially after spinal fusion or with fixed sagittal deformity) require modified targets for cup orientation to reduce the risk of dislocation. The classification by Phan and colleagues at the Rothman Institute is one of the recently disseminated schemes for spinopelvic assessment.

### Outcomes of THA

The outcomes of modern THA are excellent, with reported survivorship of approximately 95% at 10 years and 85–90% at 20 years in many large series and national registries (Swedish, Finnish, Norwegian, Australian, NJR of England and Wales). The Swedish Hip Arthroplasty Register — pioneering (started 1979) and one of the largest in the world — has documented these trends for decades.

Implants now perform durably for a substantial fraction of patients' lifetime, particularly in older patients undergoing THA in their 60s or 70s. Younger patients (under 60) have less favorable long-term survivorship, with revision rates exceeding 20% at 20 years in some series, reflecting higher activity demands and the cumulative effects of bearing wear.

Improvements in patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) — Harris Hip Score, Oxford Hip Score, WOMAC, EQ-5D, SF-36 — are substantial and durable. Approximately 90% of patients report excellent or good satisfaction with outcomes, with significant improvement in function, pain, and quality of life.

## Complications of THA

The principal complications of total hip arthroplasty include:

### Infection

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI): Rate of 1–2% for primary THA, rising to 5% in revisions, with substantial morbidity. Diagnosis follows the MSIS (Musculoskeletal Infection Society) and ICM (International Consensus Meeting) criteria. The Tsukayama classification distinguishes: Type I — positive intraoperative culture; Type II — early postoperative (<4 weeks); Type III — acute haematogenous; Type IV — chronic late. Modern prevention strategies include perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis (commonly cefazolin within 60 minutes of incision, continued for 24 hours), antibiotic-loaded bone cement in selected cases, laminar flow operating rooms, body exhaust suits in some centres, careful skin preparation (chlorhexidine-alcohol), preoperative nasal decolonization for Staphylococcus aureus, and meticulous surgical technique with attention to operative time. Treatment of PJI is extensive and discussed in the chapter on bone and joint infections.

### Dislocation

Rate of 1–3% in modern primary THA, higher (5–10%) in revision surgery and in patients with neuromuscular conditions. Causes include implant malposition (especially a vertical cup or insufficient anteversion), soft-tissue imbalance, muscle weakness, too small a head, spinopelvic instability, and neurological disease (Parkinson's, stroke, dementia). Treatment of acute dislocation is closed reduction in the emergency department or operating room under sedation; recurrent dislocation requires revision surgery, typically with revision of component position, use of a larger femoral head, dual-mobility cup, or constrained liner.

### Thromboembolic Disease

Deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism are recognized risks; prophylaxis with chemical (low-molecular-weight heparin, fondaparinux, aspirin, direct oral anticoagulants — apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) and mechanical (sequential compression devices, early mobilization) measures has substantially reduced rates. Standard prophylaxis duration is 2–5 weeks. Current AAOS and ACCP guidelines recommend aspirin in low-risk patients and DOACs in high-risk patients.

### Periprosthetic Fracture

Both intraoperative (femoral fracture during stem insertion, acetabular fracture during cup insertion) and postoperative (atraumatic in osteoporotic bone or traumatic after a fall) fractures occur. The Vancouver classification of postoperative periprosthetic femoral fractures (Duncan and Masri, 1995) guides treatment:

- Type A: trochanteric — AG (greater trochanter), AL (lesser trochanter);
- Type B1: at the stem with a well-fixed stem;
- Type B2: at the stem with a loose stem, good bone stock;
- Type B3: at the stem with a loose stem and poor bone stock;
- Type C: distal to the stem.

Treatment principles: B1 — open reduction and fixation with plates and cerclage; B2 — revision with a longer stem; B3 — revision with a long stem plus structural allograft or megaprosthesis; C — fixation below the tip of the stem with a plate.

### Aseptic Loosening

Once a major cause of long-term failure, now substantially reduced by improved implant designs, bearing surfaces, and cementing techniques. Modern uncemented implants show very low rates of aseptic loosening in long-term follow-up. When present, diagnosis is made by clinical criteria (mechanical pain on weight-bearing and pivoting), radiography (radiolucent lines at the interface, zonal osteolysis by Gruen for the femur or DeLee–Charnley for the acetabulum, component migration), CT in ambiguous cases, and scintigraphy on indication.

### Osteolysis and Wear

The historical complication of polyethylene wear with secondary osteolysis has been substantially reduced by highly cross-linked polyethylene. Long-term follow-up of modern bearings shows wear rates below the threshold for osteolytic complications (<0.1 mm/year). With established osteolysis — observation in asymptomatic cases, revision with debridement of the lesion and bone grafting in progression or significant bone defect.

### Leg-Length Discrepancy

Apparent or true leg-length differences are common (10–50% of patients) and a major cause of dissatisfaction and legal claims. Careful preoperative templating, intraoperative measurement (preoperative pin, intraoperative pelvic markings, computer-assisted navigation, intraoperative fluoroscopy in the direct anterior approach), and patient counselling regarding expectations are essential. Differences up to 1 cm are usually tolerated; above 1 cm often require a shoe lift.

### Heterotopic Ossification

Bone formation in the soft tissues around the hip can produce stiffness and discomfort. The Brooker classification (1973) grades the severity (I — isolated bone islands; II — separate bone masses with at least 1 cm gap; III — bone masses with less than 1 cm gap; IV — joint ankylosis). Risk factors include male sex, hypertrophic osteoarthritis, prior heterotopic ossification, ankylosing spondylitis, diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), and certain underlying conditions. Prophylaxis options include perioperative indomethacin (typically 75 mg twice daily for 2–6 weeks) or single-dose perioperative radiotherapy (700–800 cGy within 24–48 hours of surgery) in high-risk patients.

### Other Complications

- Sciatic, femoral, or lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury;
- Vascular injury (femoral artery, obturator artery — rare but potentially catastrophic);
- Trochanteric bursitis;
- Squeaking in ceramic-on-ceramic;
- Trunnion corrosion in modular implants;
- Persistent pain of various etiologies (including spinal);
- Psychological complications (unrealistic expectations, depressive reaction).

## Special Considerations

### Young Patients and Hip Preservation

The young patient with hip pain demands particularly careful assessment for hip-preserving options before consideration of THA. The decision is informed by:

- Morphological features (FAI, dysplasia, prior childhood disease, AVN sequelae, fracture);
- Radiographic stage of arthritis (Tönnis 0–1 — preservation possible; Tönnis 2–3 — preservation typically not successful);
- Patient's functional demands;
- Realistic expectations of joint-preserving surgery in delaying rather than preventing the eventual need for arthroplasty;
- Possibility of future revision arthroplasty.

When THA is indicated in the young patient, the choice of bearing surface is critical — XLPE or ceramic-on-ceramic are the standard, with additional preference for ZTA ceramic in patients with high functional demands. Shorter stems and more conservative proximal femoral resection preserve bone stock for future revision.

### THA in Hip Dysplasia

THA in the dysplastic hip is technically challenging because of the small, anteverted, shallow acetabulum, the high-riding femoral head with high anteversion, soft-tissue contractures (especially the abductors and hip flexors), and abnormal bony architecture. Specific techniques include:

- Placement of the acetabular cup at the level of the true (medialized and lowered) acetabulum — with or without bone graft for superior coverage;
- Use of small-diameter cups (sometimes 44 mm) in high-grade dysplasia;
- Femoral shortening osteotomy (typically subtrochanteric, with or without a derotational component) in severe shortening (Crowe III–IV);
- Careful soft-tissue release (psoas, adductors) to reduce the risk of nerve injury with limb lengthening;
- Specific uncemented stems with a dysplastic morphology (narrow and rover modular).

In younger dysplastic patients, joint-preserving PAO is generally preferred; THA is reserved for established arthritis. The experience of Berry and Lieberman, Charnley and Feagin, and Berry at Mayo Clinic is particularly instructive.

### THA in Avascular Necrosis

THA is the standard for advanced AVN with collapse (Ficat III–IV, Steinberg IV–VI). Technical issues are similar to those for primary OA, with attention to the often-younger age of the patient and the choice of durable bearings. Persistent risk factors (continued corticosteroid therapy, hemoglobinopathy, ongoing alcohol abuse, Gaucher disease) require consideration of modification possibilities and their effect on future revision.

### THA in Inflammatory Arthritis

THA in rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory arthropathies follows similar principles but with attention to:

- Systemic medical management of the underlying disease;
- Perioperative coordination with the rheumatologist regarding DMARDs and biological agents (the timing of biological agent suspension and resumption follows specific ACR/AAHKS guidelines, e.g. holding TNF inhibitors for at least one cycle before surgery);
- Elevated infection risks in patients on biological therapies;
- Poor bone quality (from chronic inflammatory disease and corticosteroid therapy);
- Cervical spine instability (atlantoaxial subluxation), requiring attention at intubation;
- The affected contralateral hip and other joints influencing rehabilitation.

### Revision THA

Revision of a failed THA is technically demanding, with management of bone defects (using bone grafts, augments, cone implants, cup-cage constructs, or revision components), the choice of bearings appropriate to the revision setting (typically dual mobility for stability), and the management of infection if present. The Paprosky classification of acetabular and femoral bone defects guides reconstruction planning. Additional concepts include:

- Tantalum trabecular metal augments for large acetabular defects;
- Cup-cage constructs (Burch-Schneider or Stryker Trident TM Custom) for severe defects with pelvic discontinuity;
- Modular revision stems (Restoration, Reclaim, Profemur Modular) for severe femoral defects;
- Femoral impaction bone grafting per Slooff and Ling in selected cases;
- Structural allografts for massive defects;
- Total proximal femoral replacements (megaprostheses) for tumor or massive bone loss.

### Spinopelvic Considerations

Recent understanding of the significance of spinopelvic mobility in THA is one of the important conceptual developments of the 2010s. Patients with fixed sagittal deformity (from spinal fusion, ankylosing spondylitis, or degenerative disease) have a fixed pelvis without the normal posterior tilt on sitting, which changes the effective orientation of the acetabular cup in functional activities. This increases the risk of impingement, dislocation, and wear. Preoperative assessment with lateral standing and sitting radiographs (analysis of pelvic tilt, sacral slope, lumbar lordosis) identifies at-risk patients and guides individualized cup orientation targets.

## Local Contributions

In keeping with the international character of hip surgery, this section presents key local contributions by country in alphabetical order.

### Austria

Karl Zweymüller of Vienna developed the uncemented Zweymüller stem (1979) — a quadrangular tapered titanium implant with a rectangular cross-section, achieving primary stability through cortical contact and continuing to be manufactured and applied in various modifications (SL-Plus, Alloclassic). The Austrian school has also contributed to the characterization of dysplasia in adults.

### Bulgaria

The Bulgarian orthopedic school has significant traditions in hip surgery. Boycho Boychev (1902–1971) in the mid-twentieth century described techniques for hip surgery in the Bulgarian literature in his work *Surgical Orthopedics*. Bulgarian surgeons subsequently adopted the modern principles of total hip arthroplasty, joint-preserving procedures for dysplasia, and approaches to FAI. The country has a number of centres that apply modern uncemented implants, all bearing surfaces in use (including ceramic-on-ceramic and dual mobility), and the direct anterior and posterior approaches. The Bulgarian Society of Orthopedics and Traumatology coordinates scientific and educational activities in this area, and collaboration with leading European centres (particularly in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland) sustains continuing education and the implementation of technical innovations.

### Denmark

The Danish school has made important contributions to the epidemiology of hip osteoarthritis and to the GLAD programme (Good Life with osteoArthritis in Denmark), which standardizes the physiotherapy approach and patient education as first-line treatment. The Danish hip register is among the largest in Europe and sustains continuing follow-up of THA outcomes.

### France

Robert Judet and his brother Jean Judet developed acrylic interposition arthroplasty (1946) — a precursor of modern arthroplasty. Letournel and Judet formulated the classical classification of acetabular fractures (1964), which remains the standard. Lord (Lyon, 1970s) was among the pioneers of uncemented THA with ribbed coating. Gilles Bousquet (Saint-Étienne) developed the concept of dual mobility (1970s), which has had a resurgence in the 2000s as a means of reducing dislocation risk. The French school has continued substantial contributions to bearing surfaces (including ceramic) and to surgical approaches.

### Germany

The German contribution to hip surgery is fundamental. Friedrich Pauwels (Aachen, 1885–1980) formulated the biomechanical principles of the hip joint and developed the intertrochanteric osteotomies — varus, valgus, and combined with medialization — which dominated joint-preserving surgery until the introduction of modern THA. His works *Atlas zur Biomechanik der gesunden und kranken Hüfte* (1973) and *Biomechanics of the Normal and Diseased Hip* (English edition 1976) remain classical references.

Dietrich Tönnis (Dortmund) systematized the assessment and treatment of congenital hip dysplasia over more than four decades. His monograph *Congenital Dysplasia and Dislocation of the Hip* (1987) and the radiographic Tönnis classifications introduced by him (for severity of osteoarthritis and for acetabular coverage) are in wide international use. The Tönnis school in Dortmund developed the Tönnis and Kalchschmidt triple pelvic osteotomy for patients with open growth plates.

Other significant German contributions: Helmut Wagner (Nuremberg) developed the Wagner cone stem, the Wagner resection arthroplasty, and the Wagner trochanteric osteotomy; M.E. Müller (although Swiss, closely connected to the German school) developed the CDH stem and AO instrumentation; the Hardinge approach, developed in Sheffield by a Briton, is particularly popularized in Germany. Stephan Tohtz (Charité, Berlin) has contributed to modern uncemented THA and to the spinopelvic considerations of total hip arthroplasty.

### Italy

Italian contributions include the work of Renato Bombelli, whose classical monograph *Osteoarthritis of the Hip* (1976, 1983) developed the biomechanical principles of Pauwels in a clinical context and emphasized the importance of the superolateral concentration of load. Bombelli and Santore developed the "valgus-extension" osteotomy for specific patterns of coxarthrosis. The Italian school of Vittorio Goldoni and Camillo Pellegrini made classical contributions to the early use of cement fixation and total hip arthroplasty in Europe.

### Japan

The Japanese school has made significant contributions to joint-preserving surgery. Sugioka (Kurume) described a transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy for AVN of the femoral head — a technique that remains popular in Japan and certain other Asian countries. Naito (Chiba) and his group developed modifications of PAO. The Japanese school is also a centre for new approaches to minimally invasive THA and to THA for severely dysplastic hips.

### Sweden

The Swedish Hip Arthroplasty Register (started in 1979 by Henrik Malchau, Peter Herberts, and colleagues in Gothenburg) was the first national registry for hip arthroplasty and a model for similar registers worldwide. The registry has documented THA outcomes across Sweden for decades and has contributed significantly to the refinement of implants and surgical techniques. Lars Linder, Jönsson, and contemporary Swedish groups continue substantial contributions to the THA literature.

### Switzerland

Maurice Müller (Bern, 1918–2009), one of the founders of AO, developed the principles of the Müller stem and modern cemented THA in Europe. Reinhold Ganz (Bern), Müller's successor, described the Bernese periacetabular osteotomy (1984) — a standard procedure for adult dysplasia — and in 2001–2003 with colleagues (Leunig, Beck, Nötzli) formulated the concept of femoroacetabular impingement as a cause of coxarthrosis, transforming the understanding of the pathogenesis of "idiopathic" osteoarthritis. The Swiss groups of Ganz, Leunig, and Siebenrock in Bern remain leading centres for joint-preserving surgery and FAI pathology.

### United Kingdom

Sir John Charnley (Wrightington, 1911–1982) is the founder of modern total hip arthroplasty with the concept of "low friction arthroplasty" (1958–1962) — a small head (22.225 mm), polymethyl methacrylate bone cement, and a polyethylene bearing surface. The Charnley principles remain fundamental. Kevin Hardinge (Sheffield, 1982) described the direct lateral approach that bears his name. Robin Ling and Clive Lee (Exeter, 1970) developed the Exeter polished tapered stem, among the most widely applied cemented stems worldwide; the Exeter group developed the "second" and "third generation" cementing techniques. Derek McMinn (Birmingham, 1997) developed Birmingham Hip Resurfacing — the leading implant for hip resurfacing. The National Joint Registry (NJR) of England, Wales, Northern Ireland, and the Isle of Man is among the largest in the world.

### United States

The American contribution is vast. Marius Smith-Petersen (Boston, 1886–1953) described the internervous anterior approach (1917) and vitallium mould arthroplasty (1923); together with Otto Aufranc he developed the early total hip arthroplasty in Boston. Austin Moore and Frederick Thompson developed the hemiarthroplasty stems (1950s) that remain in use today in selected fractures. Mark Coventry (Mayo Clinic) and Bernard Stulberg made significant contributions to the treatment of dysplasia. Later, Wayne Paprosky (Chicago) developed the classification of acetabular and femoral defects in revision surgery that bears his name. Joel Matta popularized the direct anterior approach with a traction table. Daniel Berry, Mark Pagnano, Robert Trousdale, Tad Mabry, and colleagues at Mayo Clinic continue to contribute to modern principles of THA, including work on spinopelvic mobility and acetabular cup orientation, and the relevant literature (including *Surgery of the Hip*, edited by Berry and Lieberman).

## Summary and Take-Home Points

Coxarthrosis is a common and disabling condition with substantial impact on quality of life and one of the major single causes of lost quality-adjusted years of life in developed societies. The disease is increasingly understood as the late consequence of subtle developmental hip abnormalities — FAI, dysplasia, prior childhood disease, AVN sequelae — that produce abnormal joint mechanics over decades. The biomechanical principles formulated by Pauwels explain load distribution and justify therapeutic interventions.

Clinical assessment by careful history and physical examination (with attention to mechanical pain pattern, radiation to the medial knee, "C-sign", internal rotation loss, Trendelenburg gait, Thomas test, FABER test, FADIR test), combined with standardised plain radiographs (Kellgren–Lawrence and Tönnis grading), establishes the diagnosis and stages the disease. Additional advanced imaging (MRI, MR arthrography, CT) is applied on specific indications.

Non-operative management combining education, weight loss, physical therapy (including structured programmes like GLAD), activity modification, and pharmacological treatment provides the foundation of care for all patients. Joint-preserving surgery — hip arthroscopy for FAI, periacetabular osteotomy per Ganz/Bernese for dysplasia, femoral osteotomy per Pauwels or Sugioka for specific deformities, hip resurfacing in selected young men — is indicated for the appropriate younger patient with pre-arthritic disease and preserved joint space.

Total hip arthroplasty, the most cost-effective intervention in modern orthopedic surgery, is indicated for end-stage disease with appropriate consideration of:

- Bearing surface (XLPE vs. ceramic-on-XLPE vs. ceramic-on-ceramic vs. dual mobility);
- Surgical approach (posterior, anterolateral, direct anterior);
- Stem design (cemented vs. uncemented; conventional vs. shorter vs. neck-preserving);
- Spinopelvic mobility and individualized "functional anteversion";
- Patient factors (age, bone quality, comorbidities, expectations).

Modern THA produces excellent long-term outcomes with 10-year survivorship of approximately 95% and substantial improvement in pain, function, and quality of life. The principal complications — infection (1–2%), dislocation (1–3%), periprosthetic fracture, aseptic loosening, leg-length discrepancy, heterotopic ossification — are managed by careful surgical technique, appropriate implant selection, and prompt recognition of complications when they occur. When THA fails, revision surgery guided by the Paprosky classification produces good results in most patients.

The global contribution to hip surgery reflects extensive international collaboration — from Pauwels and Tönnis in Germany, through Charnley and Hardinge in the United Kingdom, Ganz and Müller in Switzerland, Sugioka and Naito in Japan, Bombelli in Italy, Letournel and Bousquet in France, Smith-Petersen, Moore, Paprosky, Berry, and colleagues in the USA, to the pioneers of the Scandinavian registers in Sweden and Denmark — each of which has contributed significantly to the modern understanding and treatment of this common and important joint disease.
