## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Malignant bone tumors are clonal neoplastic proliferations of bone-resident or matrix-producing cells characterized by autonomous growth, local invasion, and metastatic potential. Unlike the tumor-like and benign lesions of the previous chapters, they demand multidisciplinary care, accurate pre-treatment biopsy, formal staging, and a combined-modality approach in which surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy each play a defined role. Primary bone malignancies are uncommon, accounting for less than 0.2% of all malignant tumors — but they affect predominantly children and young adults, where they remain the second commonest cancer-related cause of death in the second decade. Metastatic bone disease is by contrast far more common: more than 80% of malignant bone lesions encountered in the orthopedic clinic are metastatic, predominantly from breast, lung, prostate, kidney, and thyroid primaries, and the surgical management of impending and actual pathological fractures from metastatic disease occupies a substantial fraction of the orthopedic oncologist's workload.

This chapter synthesizes the modern canon of orthopedic oncology — the Enneking staging system, Mankin's biopsy principles, the Mirels scoring system for impending fracture, the SINS score for spinal instability from tumor, and the limb-salvage philosophy built on the introduction of effective neoadjuvant chemotherapy in the late 1970s. It surveys the principal primary malignancies (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, chondrosarcoma, chordoma, adamantinoma, fibrosarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, multiple myeloma, lymphoma of bone), metastatic bone disease and the principles of palliative surgery, and the management of pathological fractures — curative for primary sarcomas, palliative for metastases, reparative for benign lesions. Sources include Campanacci, Malawer-Sugarbaker-Bickels, Bickels-Wittig-Malawer-Healey, Pettersson-Springfield-Enneking, and the classical Bulgarian and Russian orthopedic literature.

### Enneking Staging System
<details id="enneking">
<summary>Enneking Staging System</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The staging system developed by William F. Enneking and colleagues (Musculoskeletal Tumor Society, 1980; formalized 1983) remains the framework on which the orthopedic oncologist bases surgical decisions for primary sarcomas. The system combines three analytical axes:

**Grade (G).** G1 — low grade (well to moderately differentiated, low metastatic potential); G2 — high grade (poorly differentiated, high metastatic potential). Grading is based on histology, supplemented when needed by molecular and immunohistochemical studies.

**Site (T).** T0 — tumor with a true capsule (intracapsular); applicable mostly to benign lesions. T1 — tumor without a true capsule but confined within an anatomical compartment (natural barrier preserved); T2 — tumor extending extracompartmentally because it has breached fascial, cortical, or articular barriers.

**Metastases (M).** M0 — no regional or distant metastases; M1 — regional or distant metastases (including skip metastases in the same or adjacent bone through the joint).

**Stages for sarcomas.** IA — low-grade intracompartmental (G1 T1 M0); IB — low-grade extracompartmental (G1 T2 M0); IIA — high-grade intracompartmental (G2 T1 M0); IIB — high-grade extracompartmental (G2 T2 M0); IIIA and IIIB — low- or high-grade with metastases (M1).

**Stages for benign tumors.** 1 — latent (stable or healing spontaneously); 2 — active (progressive growth limited by natural barriers); 3 — aggressive (progressive growth not limited by natural barriers). This benign-tumor staging was already used in detail in the topics on tumor-like lesions and benign tumors.

The system maps cleanly to a surgical decision: stage IA — wide resection; stage IIA/IIB — wide or radical resection plus adjuvant chemotherapy; stage III — multimodality therapy, where surgery plays a role in local control and palliation. Campanacci emphasizes that the surgical indication is not dogmatic — the choice of procedure and reconstruction depends on stage, but also on age, sex, general condition and life expectancy, associated chemo- and radiotherapy, and the psychosocial context of the patient.

**AJCC TNM system.** A complementary framework used in general oncology practice that emphasizes tumor size (T1 ≤ 8 cm; T2 > 8 cm; T3 — multifocal in the same bone), regional lymph nodes (N), distant metastases (M), and histologic grade (G1–G3). For sarcomas the TNM system provides more granular prognostic comparison between centers.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The Enneking triad — grade, compartment, metastases — is sufficient to build the operative plan in 90% of cases; AJCC TNM adds granularity through size and number of foci.</li>
    <li>Skip metastases (typically proximal to the tumor in the same or adjacent bone through the joint) are by definition M1 and automatically classify the tumor as stage III, even with an otherwise contained primary.</li>
    <li>Enneking proposed a modified staging for Ewing sarcoma (EW I–IV), since its natural behavior does not follow the compartmental containment model on which the general system rests.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Surgical Margins
<details id="margins">
<summary>Surgical Margins</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The quality of surgical margins is the principal predictor of local recurrence and survival in primary sarcoma. Enneking defined four categories that remain the universal vocabulary of orthopedic oncology:

**Intralesional margin (I).** The tumor is removed through its substance — curettage, enzymatic lysis, partial debulking. Microscopic tumor remains. Appropriate only for benign lesions (stage 1–2) and in palliative scenarios, not for sarcomas.

**Marginal margin (M).** En-bloc resection through the reactive zone or pseudocapsule of the tumor, without a normal-tissue cuff outside it. Microscopic satellite foci frequently remain. Acceptable for benign active tumors with local adjuvants and in rare palliative situations with sarcomas.

**Wide margin (W).** En-bloc resection through a continuous layer of normal tissue around the tumor, with the reactive zone intact. The standard for stage I and II sarcomas; the typical recommendation is at least 1–2 cm of normal bone and a cuff of muscle, with fascial barriers preserved where possible.

**Radical margin (R).** En-bloc resection of the entire anatomical compartment(s) of origin, bounded by their natural barriers (e.g., whole bone, whole muscle group from origin to insertion). Used for stage IIB when safe wide excision is not feasible, for contaminated pathological fracture, and at selected anatomic sites.

Beyond quantity, the **quality** of the margin matters: an intact fascial barrier 1 mm thick is more valuable than a wide layer of fat 2 cm thick. Intraoperatively the margin is judged by surgeon and pathologist together — inking of the specimen surface and histology cut perpendicular to the inked face. A "contaminated wide resection" (tumor inadvertently breached and then a wider resection done) is classified as wide but contaminated, and carries an increased local-recurrence risk.

</div>
</details>


### Biopsy — Mankin's Principles
<details id="biopsy">
<summary>Biopsy — Mankin's Principles</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The biopsy is the most consequential single decision in the management of a suspected bone malignancy. The principles articulated by Henry Mankin and colleagues (1982, confirmed in 1996) are non-negotiable and are embedded in every modern orthopedic oncology text. They rest on empirical observation: a biopsy error changes the treatment plan in roughly 18% of cases, mandates more extensive resection or amputation in 8%, and contributes to mortality in about 3% — all from a procedure performed before definitive treatment.

**The biopsy should be performed by the surgeon who will undertake the definitive resection, or after consultation with that surgeon.** The biopsy tract is contaminated by tumor and must be excised en bloc with the definitive specimen. A biopsy performed at a local hospital without prior coordination with the oncologic center often compromises future limb-salvage surgery.

**The tract must lie in the line of the future incision.** A misplaced tract (transverse to the future incision, into an adjacent compartment, through a joint capsule) contaminates tissues that would otherwise lie outside the resection. For distal-femur lesions, biopsy through vastus lateralis or vastus medialis along the line of the future anterolateral or anteromedial approach; for proximal tibia, through the planned route of resection, not through the tibialis anterior compartment.

**Image-guided core needle biopsy.** Preferred modern technique for most accessible lesions. Diagnostic accuracy 80–95%, with substantially lower contamination and complication rates than open biopsy. At least four cores — for histology, immunohistochemistry, cytogenetics/molecular biology, and microbiological culture.

**Open (incisional) biopsy** is reserved for: accessible lesions in which core biopsy is non-diagnostic; spinal and pelvic lesions in which an open approach is needed regardless; and lesions in which the entire diagnostic-and-therapeutic procedure can be completed in one stage (when radicality can be guaranteed).

**Frozen section** serves only to confirm tissue adequacy, not for definitive diagnosis. Definitive resection of a sarcoma based on frozen section without confirmed paraffin histology is an error.

**Biopsy must not be performed before complete imaging** — plain radiographs in two planes, MRI of the affected bone (without and with contrast), CT chest for pulmonary metastasis staging, bone scan or PET-CT. Post-biopsy edema and hematoma obscure the pre-biopsy imaging and can mandate repeat and unnecessary work-up.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Biopsy before complete imaging compromises surgical planning — post-biopsy MRI shows edema and hematoma exceeding the true tumor boundary.</li>
    <li>A biopsy tract placed transverse to the future definitive incision contaminates multiple compartments and often converts limb-salvage to amputation.</li>
    <li>Frozen section is never a definitive diagnosis — only tissue-adequacy confirmation. Ewing sarcoma vs. osteomyelitis, osteosarcoma vs. aneurysmal bone cyst, lymphoma vs. carcinoma — all of these require full histology with immunohistochemistry and molecular markers.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>

### Imaging and Staging Workup
<details id="imaging">
<summary>Imaging and Staging Workup</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Plain radiography.** First and often most informative study. The Enneking triad (1980) applies here too: pattern of bone destruction (geographic, moth-eaten, permeative, per Lodwick), periosteal reaction (dense and continuous — indolent; lamellated/onion-skin — subacute; spiculated/sunburst/Codman triangle — aggressive), and presence of a soft-tissue mass (defining for malignancy).

**Magnetic resonance imaging.** Indispensable for local staging. Provides the most accurate assessment of intraosseous extent, skip metastases within the affected bone, joint involvement, and the relationship of tumor to the adjacent neurovascular bundle. T1-weighted images best show marrow extent; T2 with fat suppression (STIR) — the soft-tissue mass and peritumoral edema. Contrast enhancement identifies viable tumor regions and helps select the representative biopsy area.

**Computed tomography.** Superior to MRI for assessing matrix mineralization (chondroid, osteoid matrix), cortical destruction, and for CT-guided core needle biopsy. CT chest is the standard for pulmonary metastasis staging, the dominant site of distant disease for most primary sarcomas.

**Bone scintigraphy.** Highly sensitive for detection of metastases and skip lesions, but notoriously falsely negative in multiple myeloma and in highly aggressive renal cell carcinoma — their lesions are purely lytic without enough osteoblastic response to produce uptake.

**18F-FDG PET-CT.** Increasingly the choice for high-grade sarcoma and for metastatic disease of unknown primary. SUV correlates with tumor aggressiveness and supports assessment of response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

**Bone marrow biopsy.** Mandatory for Ewing sarcoma and for multiple myeloma to assess systemic involvement.

### Osteosarcoma
<details id="osteosarcoma">
<summary>Osteosarcoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Epidemiology and subtypes.** Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignancy of bone in children and young adults, with a bimodal age distribution: a major peak in the second decade corresponding to the adolescent growth spurt, and a smaller peak in older adults corresponding to secondary osteosarcoma arising in Paget bone, post-radiation bone, or pre-existing fibrous dysplasia. Overall incidence is approximately 3–5 per million per year, with a slight male predominance.

The conventional intramedullary osteosarcoma (about 80% of all cases) is a high-grade tumor with three histological variants — osteoblastic (50%), chondroblastic (25%), and fibroblastic (25%); prognosis and treatment are identical. Surface osteosarcomas form an important separate group: **parosteal osteosarcoma** — a low-grade surface tumor of the posterior distal femur in young adults, with an excellent prognosis after wide resection alone; **periosteal osteosarcoma** — an intermediate-grade surface tumor of the diaphysis with a moderately good prognosis; **high-grade surface osteosarcoma** — behaves like a conventional intramedullary tumor. **Telangiectatic osteosarcoma** is a high-grade variant with fluid-fluid levels on MRI and a radiographic appearance that can mimic aneurysmal bone cyst (a critical differential); histologically it is dominated by dilated blood-filled spaces with scattered high-grade cells and scant osteoid. **Small-cell osteosarcoma** resembles Ewing sarcoma microscopically but is distinguished by the presence of osteoid.

Secondary osteosarcomas arise in Paget bone (risk in advanced Paget disease 0.5–1%; in polyostotic disease above 10%) or after radiation (latency 10–20 years after a dose above 30 Gy). They have a markedly worse prognosis than primary tumors.

The molecular biology includes mutations in TP53 (Li-Fraumeni syndrome — familial predisposition), in the retinoblastoma gene RB1 (bilateral retinoblastoma carries a high risk of secondary osteosarcoma, particularly in irradiated fields), and complex genomic instability with frequent chromothripsis and chromoplexy events.

**Clinical presentation.** Presentation is typically with pain and swelling at the site of the lesion — most commonly the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus (the metaphyses around the knee and shoulder). Pain is initially intermittent, becomes constant, and progresses over weeks; nocturnal pain is characteristic but is not as reliable, nor as aspirin-responsive, as in osteoid osteoma. Recent trauma frequently draws clinical attention to an underlying lesion that had been previously asymptomatic. Palpable warm tender swelling, overlying erythema, and dilated veins are common.

Pathological fracture occurs in 10–15% of cases at presentation. Historically it was considered an absolute indication for amputation. Modern practice, supported by Campanacci and Malawer-Sugarbaker, permits limb salvage in selected pathological fracture cases provided that adequate oncological margins can still be achieved and the soft-tissue contamination has not violated multiple compartments. Serum alkaline phosphatase and lactate dehydrogenase are commonly elevated and serve as prognostic markers — high LDH at presentation is an independent adverse prognostic factor.

**Imaging.** Plain radiographs show a destructive lesion with a wide zone of transition, often with mixed lytic and sclerotic areas reflecting tumor osteoid production. Periosteal reaction is characteristic and may take several forms: the **Codman triangle** (a focus of periosteal new bone at the proximal and distal margins of the lesion, formed where the tumor lifts the periosteum and bone formation continues at the edges); the **"sunburst"** or **"hair-on-end"** spiculated reaction (radial spicules of bone laid down along perpendicular tumor vessels as the tumor expands); and the **"onion-skin"** pattern (multiple laminations of periosteal new bone, more typical of Ewing sarcoma but seen here too). Soft-tissue extension is often substantial.

MRI is essential for local staging. CT chest is the staging investigation of choice for pulmonary metastases. PET-CT or bone scintigraphy is used to detect skip metastases and additional sites of bone involvement.

**Treatment — modern multidisciplinary model.** Osteosarcoma treatment has transformed survival from about 20% in the era of amputation alone to 60–70% five-year survival in patients without metastatic disease at presentation. The three-step model remains standard:

1. **Neoadjuvant chemotherapy.** The standard regimen is "MAP" — high-dose methotrexate (with folinic acid rescue), doxorubicin (Adriamycin), and cisplatin — administered over about 10–12 weeks preoperatively. Ifosfamide is added in high-risk patients and in poor responders. The aim is reduction of tumor volume, formation of a discrete pseudocapsule, and treatment of the occult micrometastases that, per Campanacci, are present in 80–90% of patients at presentation (based on the historical metastatic mortality after amputation alone).

2. **Wide surgical resection.** Achieves oncological margins (at least 1–2 cm of normal bone and a cuff of muscle, with intact fascial barriers preserved). Intralesional or marginal margins are associated with markedly higher local recurrence (10–20% vs. 2–5%) and worse survival. Limb-salvage reconstruction options include endoprosthetic replacement (the most common modern technique, with rotating-hinge knee or shoulder megaprostheses), structural allograft, allograft-prosthetic composite, vascularized fibular autograft, and in the immature skeleton, expandable endoprostheses (expandable implants extended noninvasively with an external magnet) or rotationplasty (the Van Nes procedure for distal femoral tumors, in which the foot is rotated 180° and the ankle joint functions as a knee — functionally excellent, psychologically demanding reconstruction).

3. **Adjuvant chemotherapy.** Continues for 16–20 weeks postoperatively. The Huvos system grades response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy by the percentage of tumor necrosis on the surgical specimen: greater than 90% necrosis (good responders) is associated with improved survival; less than 90% (poor responders) prompts a regimen change postoperatively.

Amputation is reserved for cases in which adequate margins cannot be achieved by limb salvage, for major neurovascular involvement that cannot be reconstructed, for extensive pathological fracture with multi-compartment contamination, or by the patient's informed preference.

**Prognosis.** Five-year survival in non-metastatic high-grade osteosarcoma — 60–70%; in metastatic disease at presentation — 20–30%; in isolated, surgically resectable pulmonary metastases (metastasectomy) — 30–50%. Local recurrence is 2–3% after amputation, 5–7% after limb-salvage surgery with a good neoadjuvant response. Local recurrence is almost always accompanied or followed by metastatic spread and substantially worsens survival. Axial sites (pelvis, spine) have a worse prognosis than appendicular sites.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Bimodal age curve: adolescent growth-spurt peak around the knee and shoulder metaphyses, and a smaller adult peak in Paget bone, irradiated bone, or fibrous dysplasia. Pre-treatment elevated LDH is an independent adverse prognostic factor.</li>
    <li>Modern treatment is neoadjuvant MAP (high-dose methotrexate, doxorubicin, cisplatin), wide resection with 1–2 cm of normal bone, then adjuvant MAP. Huvos necrosis > 90% defines a good responder and tracks with survival; < 90% prompts regimen change postoperatively.</li>
    <li>Pathological fracture in osteosarcoma is no longer an automatic indication for amputation — limb salvage is possible in selected cases if wide margins remain achievable and contamination has not breached compartmental boundaries.</li>

### Ewing Sarcoma
<details id="ewing">
<summary>Ewing Sarcoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Pathology and molecular biology.** Ewing sarcoma is a small round blue cell tumor classified within the Ewing family of tumors, which includes peripheral primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PNET) and Askin tumor of the chest wall. The molecular signature is the t(11;22)(q24;q12) translocation, generating the EWS-FLI1 fusion gene in approximately 85% of cases; less common variants include EWS-ERG, EWS-ETV1, and others. The fusion oncoprotein acts as an aberrant transcription factor driving the malignant phenotype, and its presence in a core biopsy specimen (FISH or RT-PCR) is practically definitive for the diagnosis.

The histology is sheets of small uniform round blue cells with scant cytoplasm, PAS-positive cytoplasmic glycogen, and characteristic membranous CD99 (MIC2) staining. The reactive reticulin fibers are sparse — a feature that distinguishes the tumor from lymphoma. The differential diagnosis includes other small round blue cell tumors of childhood (lymphoma, neuroblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, small-cell osteosarcoma) — immunohistochemistry and molecular studies are essential.

**Clinical presentation.** Ewing sarcoma typically affects the second decade, with a slight male predominance and a striking predilection for children of European ancestry; it is rare in African and East Asian populations. The lesion arises in the diaphysis of long bones (femur, tibia, humerus, fibula) and also in the flat bones of the pelvis, scapula, and ribs. Presentation is with pain, swelling, and constitutional symptoms — fever, weight loss, fatigue, elevated ESR — that are frequently confused with osteomyelitis, particularly in younger children. Campanacci emphasizes that patients are often treated for months with antibiotics before the diagnosis is made, because fever and leukocytosis suggest an infectious process.

**Imaging.** Plain radiographs classically show a permeative ("moth-eaten") pattern of bone destruction in the diaphysis, with the characteristic "onion-skin" laminated periosteal reaction reflecting episodic tumor expansion and periosteal new bone formation, and a substantial soft-tissue mass that often exceeds the size of the bone abnormality. MRI is essential — per Campanacci, marrow-space extension is frequently far more extensive than the radiographic image suggests, and defines the true margin of resection. CT chest and bone marrow biopsy are essential staging investigations, since pulmonary and bone-marrow metastases are common; bone-marrow involvement is an independent adverse prognostic factor.

**Modified Enneking staging.** Because the natural behavior of the tumor does not follow the compartmental containment model, Enneking proposed a separate staging system: EW I — solitary intraosseous; EW II — solitary with extraosseous extension; EW III — multicentric in the skeleton; EW IV — distant metastases. EW II is the commonest stage at presentation (about 70%); metastases at presentation — about 20%.

**Treatment.** The treatment of Ewing sarcoma is intensive multimodality combination chemotherapy plus local control (surgery, radiotherapy, or both). The standard chemotherapy regimen in North America is VDC/IE — vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide alternating with ifosfamide and etoposide — administered in compressed (every-two-week) cycles for 14–17 cycles in total. European protocols (EWING-2008 / EuroEwing) use similar agents in different sequences.

Local control is achieved by surgery, radiotherapy, or both, depending on the resectability of the primary lesion: surgically resectable lesions in expendable bones (fibula, rib, scapular spine) are treated by surgery alone; surgically resectable but functionally demanding lesions (pelvis, spine) are increasingly treated by surgery plus adjuvant radiotherapy; surgically unresectable lesions (large pelvic primaries, central spine) are treated by definitive radiotherapy with chemotherapy.

**Prognosis.** Five-year survival is approximately 65–75% for non-metastatic disease, dropping to 25–30% with metastases at presentation. Bone-marrow metastases carry a particularly poor prognosis. Prognostic factors: site (appendicular better than pelvic), tumor volume, histological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (necrosis > 90%), LDH at presentation.

### Chondrosarcoma
<details id="chondrosarcoma">
<summary>Chondrosarcoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-3/chondrosarcoma.jpg" data-figure-alt="Chondrosarcoma" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/ortho-3/chondrosarcoma.jpg" alt="Chondrosarcoma" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Chondrosarcoma: lytic lesion with "popcorn" chondroid mineralisation and endosteal scalloping of the cortex (typically over two-thirds of cortical thickness).</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · KGH, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chondrosarcoma_(1).jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Chondrosarcoma is the second most common malignant primary bone tumor after osteosarcoma, accounting for approximately 25% of primary bone sarcomas. Unlike osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma, chondrosarcoma is predominantly a tumor of older adults, with peak incidence in the fifth and sixth decades. The pathological spectrum spans several variants:

**Conventional (central) chondrosarcoma.** Arises within the central medullary cavity. Histological grading from I (low) to III (high) is the principal determinant of behavior: low-grade rarely metastasizes but recurs locally; high-grade behaves aggressively with metastatic spread to the lungs.

**Peripheral chondrosarcoma.** Arises from the cartilage cap of an osteochondroma (osteocartilaginous exostosis). Risk of malignant transformation in a solitary osteochondroma is below 1%; in multiple hereditary exostoses — 2–5%. Cartilage cap thickness above 2 cm in an adult is a warning sign and warrants biopsy.

**Dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma.** A particularly aggressive variant in which a low-grade cartilaginous tumor harbors a high-grade non-cartilaginous component (often osteosarcomatous, fibrosarcomatous, or with features of undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma) — a finding that markedly worsens prognosis.

**Mesenchymal chondrosarcoma.** A rare high-grade variant with bimodal histology (small round cell foci and zones of mature cartilage). More common in the axial skeleton and in younger patients; responds to Ewing-like chemotherapy.

**Clear-cell chondrosarcoma.** A low-grade epiphyseal variant with predilection for the proximal femur, with clear vacuolated cells in the matrix. Mimics chondroblastoma, but in older patients.

**Periosteal chondrosarcoma.** A surface tumor, usually of the distal femur; low-grade and resectable.

Anatomical sites include the pelvis (the most common single location, 25–30% of cases), the proximal femur, the proximal humerus, the scapula, and the ribs. Imaging shows a lytic lesion with characteristic chondroid matrix (popcorn, rings, or arcs of mineralization) with cortical destruction, soft-tissue extension, and endosteal scalloping. Scalloping greater than two-thirds of cortical thickness is practically always chondrosarcoma rather than enchondroma. MRI demonstrates the characteristic lobulated, high-T2-signal architecture of cartilage tissue with peripheral enhancement after contrast; high-grade regions show diffuse enhancement and mandate wide resection.

**Treatment.** Principally surgical: wide en-bloc resection with negative margins is curative for low- and intermediate-grade tumors. Conventional chondrosarcoma is notoriously chemoresistant and radioresistant. The clear principle, inherited from Campanacci and Malawer and adopted by modern consensus guidelines: "the only adjuvant for chondrosarcoma is the surgeon's scalpel." Dedifferentiated and mesenchymal chondrosarcoma, by contrast, are treated as high-grade sarcomas with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (mesenchymal — with a Ewing-like regimen) and aggressive surgery.

**Prognosis.** Five-year survival — excellent (85–90%) for low-grade lesions; falls to 30–50% for high-grade and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma. Pelvic location, size above 8 cm, and age over 60 are independent adverse prognostic factors.

### Chordoma
<details id="chordoma">
<summary>Chordoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-3/chordoma-sacrum.jpg" data-figure-alt="Sacral chordoma" aria-label="Enlarge">

Chordoma is a rare, low- to intermediate-grade malignant tumor arising from notochordal remnants (the so-called ecchondrosis physaliphora — small gelatinous remnants described by early anatomists along the midline of the axial skeleton, most common in the sacrococcygeal region and the spheno-occipital synchondrosis). Characteristic locations along the midline of the axial skeleton: the **sacrococcygeal** region (50% of cases), the **spheno-occipital** region (35%), and the **mobile spine** (15%, most often cervical). Peak incidence is in the fifth and sixth decades, with a slight male predominance.

The tumor grows slowly, infiltrates locally, and recurs persistently after incomplete resection; distant metastases (lung, liver, bone) occur late but have been described in about 30% of patients on long follow-up. Histologically the tumor is composed of large vacuolated "physaliphorous" cells (with intracytoplasmic vacuoles containing mucin and glycogen — PAS-positive, mucicarmine-positive) in a myxoid stroma. Chondroid chordoma — a rare variant with cartilage-like areas, more common in cranial sites. Dedifferentiated chordoma (especially after radiotherapy) — a rare high-grade variant. The transcription factor **brachyury (T)** is the immunohistochemical hallmark and is highly specific; the diagnostic standard in modern practice. The tumor is also positive for S-100 protein, cytokeratins, and epithelial membrane antigen — the dual epithelial-notochordal phenotype.

**Clinical presentation.** Sacrococcygeal chordomas present with long-standing (sometimes years) low back pain, a sacrococcygeal mass (palpable on rectal examination), urinary or bowel dysfunction, and posterior pelvic neurological symptoms. Because the tumor grows slowly, it is often large at presentation. Symptoms develop when the tumor compresses the sacral nerve roots or breaches the anterior sacral wall into the presacral space.

**Surgical treatment.** Wide en-bloc resection with negative margins is the only treatment offering durable disease control. Sacrectomy is technically demanding and is typically a combined anterior-posterior procedure: an anterior abdominal approach for vascular control of the middle sacral vessels and careful separation of the rectum from the anterior sacrum, followed by a posterior approach for the sacrectomy itself. Per Karakousis and Sugarbaker (Malawer-Sugarbaker *Musculoskeletal Cancer Surgery*), the level of sacral transection is critical for functional outcome:

- Transection below the lower border of S3 — preserved sphincter function, urinary and fecal continence.
- Transection through S3 (with bilateral sacrifice of S4–S5 roots) — mild to moderate urinary and fecal dysfunction, usually recoverable with rehabilitation.
- Bilateral sacrifice of S2–S4 roots — complete urinary and fecal incontinence; impotence in men.
- Unilateral preservation of an S2 root — preserves anorectal continence and largely preserves bladder function.

Resection above S3 inevitably compromises continence. The patient must be clearly informed preoperatively, since a clean surgical margin is essential for long disease-free survival. Tumor pull at higher sacrectomies requires bicortical reconstruction of the pelvic ring with pedicle-to-iliac (S-plus-iliac) screw constructs and/or fibular or iliac bone graft — at high sacrectomies (above S1) the pelvic ring is disrupted and reconstruction is complex.

**Adjuvant radiotherapy.** Conventional photon radiotherapy is of limited benefit (chordoma is relatively radioresistant), but **proton-beam therapy** and **carbon-ion therapy** have an increasing role for surgical residual disease, for residual disease after suboptimal resection, and for unresectable spheno-occipital chordomas, where dose constraints to the brainstem and optic apparatus limit conventional radiotherapy. In unresectable cases, definitive proton therapy can provide local control in about 60–70% of patients at 5 years.

### Adamantinoma
<details id="adamantinoma">
<summary>Adamantinoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Adamantinoma is a rare low-grade malignant tumor with an epithelial component histologically reminiscent of the ameloblastoma of the jaw bones — hence the name. It affects predominantly the age range 20–40 years, with a slight male predominance, and is exceptional below 11 years. About 80–90% of cases are located in the **diaphysis of the tibia** (typically in the central or middle-distal third, with characteristic involvement of the anterior cortex); the remaining cases are distributed in the fibula, femur, humerus, and forearm bones. Adamantinomas of the soft tissues of the leg are exceptional.

Per Campanacci, the tumor develops slowly and indolently — symptoms (pain and swelling) are long-standing, with a frequent history of antecedent trauma. Imaging shows an eccentric osteolysis along the anterior cortex of the tibia, with expansion, thinning, or breakthrough of the cortex, sclerotic borders, and a soft-tissue mass. Frequently — multifocal foci with intervening sclerosis, sometimes with anterior tibial bowing. CT and MRI show solid fibrous tissue with low T1 signal and moderately high T2 signal.

**Relationship with osteofibrous dysplasia (Campanacci).** Osteofibrous dysplasia (Campanacci disease, 1976) — described as a distinct nosological entity in the chapter on tumor-like lesions — typically presents in the first decade and often regresses spontaneously. The relationship between the two is debated: one hypothesis holds that osteofibrous dysplasia is a precursor or incomplete form of adamantinoma (so-called "differentiated adamantinoma"); another that the two are separate entities with overlapping histology. The practical message: a pediatric tibial diaphyseal lesion with zonal architecture and fibrous stroma — observation and serial imaging; an enlarging lesion in an adult — biopsy to exclude adamantinoma.

**Treatment.** Wide en-bloc resection with negative margins. Adamantinoma is essentially insensitive to chemotherapy and radiotherapy; the clean surgical margin determines prognosis. With inadequate margins, local recurrence is common and may lead to dedifferentiation. Reconstruction of the tibial diaphysis after resection includes vascularized fibular autograft, intercalary allograft, or a combined approach. Metastases are late (5–15 years after primary treatment) to the lungs and regional lymph nodes. Five-year survival — about 85–90%; 10-year — 60–70%.

### Fibrosarcoma and Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma
<details id="fibrosarcoma">
<summary>Fibrosarcoma and Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Fibrosarcoma of bone.** A rare malignancy of fibroblastic origin, with a characteristic microscopic "herringbone" pattern of long bundles of spindle cells. It occurs in adults aged 30–60, with predilection for the femur and tibia. Presentation is with pain and an enlarging swelling; the radiographic appearance is of a permeative or geographic lytic lesion without matrix mineralization. As a nosological entity fibrosarcoma is graded I–IV (Broders); the high-grade tumor histologically overlaps with malignant fibrous histiocytoma, and the modern WHO classification has reassigned many of these cases to the new entity of undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma. Treatment is wide surgical resection; in high-grade tumors adjuvant (sometimes neoadjuvant) chemotherapy is added on an osteosarcoma model. Five-year survival — 30–50% for high-grade, 70–80% for low-grade.

**Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma of bone (formerly malignant fibrous histiocytoma).** The WHO classification (2013) renamed this entity, retiring the term "malignant fibrous histiocytoma" because of the lack of a demonstrated histiocytic origin. It is a high-grade pleomorphic spindle-cell sarcoma without specific differentiation (a diagnosis of exclusion after osteosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, and others have been ruled out). The tumor accounts for less than 5% of primary bone sarcomas, occurs predominantly in adults aged 40–70, and arises most commonly in the metaphysis of long bones around the knee. It often arises secondarily in Paget bone, in irradiated bone, or in a bone-infarct zone. The radiographic appearance is of a destructive lytic lesion with poorly defined margins, typically without matrix mineralization. Treatment principles parallel those of osteosarcoma: neoadjuvant chemotherapy (MAP or an ifosfamide-based regimen), wide resection, adjuvant chemotherapy. Five-year survival — 40–50%, modestly worse than osteosarcoma.

</div>
</details>


### Multiple Myeloma and Solitary Plasmacytoma
<details id="myeloma">
<summary>Multiple Myeloma and Solitary Plasmacytoma</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

Multiple myeloma is the most common primary malignant tumor of bone in adults overall — accounting for nearly half of all primary bone malignancies, though it is more accurately characterized as a hematological malignancy with prominent bone manifestations. The disease is a clonal proliferation of plasma cells producing monoclonal immunoglobulin (paraprotein). Presentation is typically in the sixth to eighth decade with bone pain, pathological fracture (vertebral compression fractures are particularly common), anemia, renal impairment, hypercalcemia, and recurrent infections.

**CRAB criteria.** The classical International Myeloma Working Group criteria define symptomatic disease: **C**alcium (hypercalcemia > 2.75 mmol/L), **R**enal insufficiency (creatinine > 177 μmol/L), **A**nemia (Hb < 100 g/L), **B**one lesions (one or more osteolytic lesion on plain radiographs, CT, or PET-CT). One or more CRAB criteria plus ≥ 10% clonal plasma cells on bone marrow — diagnosis of symptomatic myeloma.

**Imaging.** Plain radiographs show characteristic "punched-out", geographic, lytic lesions — particularly in the skull, vertebrae, pelvis, and proximal long bones — without surrounding sclerosis or periosteal reaction. This feature distinguishes myeloma from most other lytic processes. Bone scintigraphy is famously falsely negative because the lesions are purely lytic without sufficient osteoblastic reaction to produce uptake. Whole-body low-dose CT, whole-body MRI, and increasingly PET-CT have replaced the traditional radiographic skeletal survey. MRI is particularly valuable for assessment of spinal involvement and for detection of diffuse marrow infiltration without visible lytic foci.

**Laboratory workup.** Monoclonal paraprotein on serum or urine electrophoresis (with immunofixation), with characteristic kappa or lambda light-chain restriction (M-protein in serum and/or Bence Jones protein in urine). Serum free light chains — a sensitive marker. Bone marrow biopsy — clonal plasma cell infiltration ≥ 10%. β2-microglobulin and albumin define the International Staging System (ISS): stage I — favorable; stage III — adverse.

**Treatment.** Medical: induction chemotherapy with bortezomib, lenalidomide, daratumumab, and dexamethasone (VRd, KRd, or Dara-VRd combinations, depending on the risk profile), followed by autologous stem-cell transplantation in eligible patients under 70 years. Maintenance lenalidomide prolongs remission. Bisphosphonates (monthly zoledronate or pamidronate) or the RANKL inhibitor denosumab reduce skeletal-related events and are standard.

**Surgical management.** Reserved for pathological fracture (most commonly femoral neck and intertrochanteric, requiring fixation with a cephalomedullary nail or hemiarthroplasty), for spinal cord compression (decompression and stabilization — often minimally invasive percutaneous pedicle screw fixation with kyphoplasty), and for impending fracture meeting Mirels criteria. Radiotherapy provides excellent palliation of focal bone pain (20–30 Gy).

**Solitary plasmacytoma of bone.** A single bone lesion of monoclonal plasma cells without systemic myeloma. Definition: a single bone focus, a bone marrow biopsy without myeloma infiltration, a skeletal survey without other lesions, and no CRAB criteria. Treatment: definitive radiotherapy (40–50 Gy) with excellent local control. Progression to multiple myeloma — in 50–70% of patients over the subsequent decade; lifelong follow-up with serum electrophoresis and imaging is required.

### Lymphoma of Bone
<details id="lymphoma">
<summary>Lymphoma of Bone</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Primary lymphoma of bone is rare and is almost always a diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. The "primary" definition requires absence of systemic lymphomatous disease for at least six months after the diagnosis of the bone lesion. Presentation is with bone pain and a permeative or lytic lesion, often with substantial soft-tissue extension. The differential diagnosis includes osteomyelitis, Ewing sarcoma, and metastatic carcinoma. Diagnosis is by core biopsy with appropriate immunohistochemistry (CD20, CD3, CD45, BCL-6) and flow cytometry.

Treatment is chemotherapy (typically R-CHOP — rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) and radiotherapy; surgery is rarely required and is generally limited to pathological fracture stabilization or biopsy. Five-year survival exceeds 70% with modern combined-modality therapy.

Bone involvement is also common in chronic and acute leukemias, particularly in children, where leukemic infiltration of the metaphyses produces characteristic radiolucent "leukemic lines" (bands of decreased density adjacent to the physis), and presentation with bone pain that mimics juvenile inflammatory arthritis is well described. Hodgkin lymphoma involves bone in about 15% of disseminated disease — sclerotic, "ivory vertebra" lesions are characteristic.

</div>
</details>


### Other Rare Tumors
<details id="other">
<summary>Other Rare Tumors</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Angiosarcoma of bone.** Rare, high-grade vascular sarcoma. Can be solitary or multifocal; the multifocal form is curiously regional, with multiple lesions typically confined to a single anatomical region (for instance, an entire lower limb). Treatment is multimodal, with wide surgical resection where feasible, and radiotherapy and chemotherapy for unresectable or recurrent disease. Prognosis is poor — five-year survival 20–40%.

**Leiomyosarcoma of bone.** An extremely rare malignancy of smooth-muscle origin. Arises predominantly in adults in the distal femur and proximal tibia. Treatment follows the high-grade soft-tissue sarcoma protocol — wide resection plus adjuvant radiotherapy; chemotherapy with doxorubicin and ifosfamide in younger patients.

**Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST) secondarily involving bone.** Arises from a large peripheral nerve (sciatic, femoral) and invades adjacent bone. About 50% develop in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1; in those patients the prognosis is worse. Treatment is wide resection with potential amputation; the role of adjuvant therapy is debated.

**Carcinoma and sarcoma in chronic osteomyelitis.** Arise in chronic osteomyelitis with draining sinuses, usually of more than 20 years duration. 90% of cases are squamous cell carcinoma developing in the epithelial proliferation of the drainage sinus; the remainder are sarcomas (fibrosarcoma, MFH/UPS, angiosarcoma). Clinical features — increasing pain, increased malodorous discharge, bleeding, an enlarging fungating mass. Treatment is wide resection or amputation; prognosis depends on early recognition.

</div>
</details>

### Epidemiology and Pathophysiology
<details id="epidemiology">
<summary>Epidemiology and Pathophysiology</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Metastatic disease is by far the most common malignant lesion of bone in adults, accounting for the vast majority of malignant bone tumors over the age of 40. Bickels and Malawer emphasize that the skeleton is the third most common site of metastatic disease after the lungs and liver; in the United States approximately 350,000 patients die annually with bone metastases.

**Primary cancers.** The five primary cancers responsible for about 80% of bone metastases (the mnemonic "BLT KP") are **B**reast, **L**ung, **T**hyroid, **K**idney, and **P**rostate. In children primary sources are neuroblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, retinoblastoma, and thyroid carcinoma.

**Anatomical distribution.** The most commonly affected bones are the spine (particularly the thoracic and lumbar vertebrae, about 60% of bone metastases), the pelvis, the proximal femur, the ribs, and the proximal humerus. Distal metastases (below the knee, below the elbow) are uncommon (under 10%) and often originate from lung carcinoma. The mechanism of preferential spread to these axial and proximal-limb sites involves the slow flow through the marrow sinusoids of red marrow, the rich vascular supply, and the receptive paracrine microenvironment of bone.

**Batson's plexus.** Vertebral metastases reach the spine particularly through Batson's plexus (Oscar V. Batson, 1940) — the valveless venous plexus that connects the pelvic and abdominal venous return to the vertebral venous system. When intra-abdominal pressure rises (cough, Valsalva), flow in the Batson plexus reverses, which explains the frequency of vertebral metastases in prostate and breast carcinoma.

**Patterns of bone metastasis.** Predominantly **osteolytic** — most carcinomas, particularly renal cell (characteristically highly vascular), thyroid, lung, multiple myeloma. Predominantly **osteoblastic** — prostate, occasionally breast, small-cell lung carcinoma. **Mixed** — most metastatic breast and gastrointestinal carcinomas. Lytic lesions are at higher risk for pathological fracture; blastic lesions rarely fracture.

Multiple myeloma (although a hematological rather than a truly metastatic disease) shares many features of the surgical management of metastatic carcinoma and is often included in this section.

</div>
</details>


### Clinical Evaluation and the Unknown-Primary Workup
<details id="workup">
<summary>Clinical Evaluation and the Unknown-Primary Workup</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical presentation is of a patient with known cancer presenting with bone pain or pathological fracture. However, in a significant minority of patients (about 15–25%), the bone metastasis is the presenting feature of an undiagnosed primary cancer — a situation in which thorough oncological staging is essential before any biopsy or surgical intervention.

**Rougraff workup.** The standardized protocol for suspected metastasis of unknown primary:

1. Careful history and physical examination (skin, lymphadenopathy, breasts, thyroid, prostate, rectal examination).
2. Plain radiographs of the affected bone and the chest.
3. CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis.
4. Bone scintigraphy (or PET-CT in high index of suspicion).
5. Serum and urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation (to exclude myeloma).
6. Tumor markers — PSA in men over 50, CEA, thyroglobulin if ultrasound suggests a thyroid lesion.
7. Complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, ESR, calcium.

This evaluation identifies the primary in about 85% of cases. The remaining 15% undergo biopsy of the bone lesion before any operative intervention; biopsy results then guide definitive treatment, which can differ substantially depending on the primary diagnosis.

**Surgical philosophy by primary.** Bickels and Malawer emphasize that knowledge of the primary tumor changes the surgical approach:

- **Renal cell carcinoma** — highly vascular, lytic, chemo- and radioresistant metastasis. Often a single bone lesion. Candidate for preoperative selective arterial embolization and en-bloc resection with curative intent if the metastasis is solitary.
- **Thyroid carcinoma** — also highly vascular, lytic. Preoperative embolization mandatory.
- **Breast carcinoma** — often multiple osteolytic or mixed lesions. Strong response to systemic and local therapy; surgery is palliative.
- **Lung carcinoma** — poor overall prognosis, limited life expectancy; surgery is strictly palliative stabilization.
- **Prostate carcinoma** — often osteoblastic lesions that rarely fracture; surgical treatment less often needed.
- **Multiple myeloma** — frequently multiple lytic foci with pathological fractures; routine use of bisphosphonates/denosumab; surgery for stabilization and decompression.

</div>
</details>


### Mirels' Scoring System
<details id="mirels">
<summary>Mirels' Scoring System</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The decision to undertake prophylactic surgical fixation of a metastatic lesion in a long bone (that is, fixation before a pathological fracture occurs) is supported by Mirels' scoring system (Hilton Mirels, 1989), which remains the most validated tool for fracture risk stratification. The system assigns **1–3 points for each of four parameters**:

| Parameter | 1 point | 2 points | 3 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site | Upper limb | Lower limb | Peritrochanteric |
| Pain | Mild | Moderate | Functional (on loading) |
| Lesion type | Blastic | Mixed | Lytic |
| Size (of bone diameter) | < 1/3 | 1/3 – 2/3 | > 2/3 |

**Interpretation.** Total score 7 or less — low fracture risk; radiotherapy alone. Score 8 — intermediate risk; case-by-case discussion (the impending-fracture rate at this score is about 15%). Score 9 or higher — high probability of fracture within months; supports prophylactic fixation (fracture risk on sequential observation is about 33% or higher).

Bickels and Malawer note that the alternative, earlier Harrington (1986) definition — lesion > 2.5 cm with destruction of ≥ 50% of the cortical diameter, associated with progressive load-related pain unresponsive to radiotherapy — remains widely used and leads to similar conclusions. It is a practical framework that does not require a point calculation and is particularly applicable to complex acetabular metastases, where the concept of "size relative to bone diameter" is not really applicable. Size above 2.5 cm, destruction of more than half the cortical circumference, and progressive load-related pain are the three equivalent criteria for impending fracture.

While Mirels' system is the most validated tool, **clinical judgment remains crucial**. Pain on loading — an independent indicator of instability, regardless of the Mirels score. Renal cell and thyroid metastases, even with a relatively low Mirels score, often warrant prophylactic fixation because of their radioresistance and the risk of progression in the radiotherapy field.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Mirels score ≥ 9 for a long-bone metastasis predicts high fracture risk within months and supports prophylactic fixation; ≤ 7 supports radiotherapy alone; 8 is the discussion zone.</li>
    <li>The four axes are site, pain character, lesion type (lytic worst), and size relative to bone diameter (> 2/3 worst).</li>
    <li>The alternative Harrington rule — lesion > 2.5 cm with > 50% cortical destruction and progressive functional pain — is a practical equivalent.</li>

### Surgical Principles
<details id="surgical-principles">
<summary>Surgical Principles</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The surgical approach to metastatic bone disease is fundamentally palliative: the goals are pain relief, restoration of function, and maintenance of independent ambulation for the patient's remaining life expectancy. Bickels and Malawer articulate six key principles that distinguish the surgery of metastases from that of primary bone tumors:

**1. The entire bone is protected by the implant.** Long intramedullary nails that traverse the entire bone, rather than short screws or limited plates, because second lesions in the same bone are common. A long cephalomedullary nail in the femur protects both the peritrochanteric region and any potential additional foci in the diaphysis and condyles.

**2. Immediate weight-bearing.** Fixation must permit full loading from the first postoperative day — prolonged convalescent rehabilitation of any kind is not realistic for patients with limited life expectancy. This imposes biomechanical demands that often exceed those of trauma fixation.

**3. Tumor removal before reconstruction.** Bickels emphasizes that the attempt to treat a pathological fracture as if it were a traumatic fracture (closed nailing without tumor removal) is an error — the tumor progresses in the radiotherapy field and compromises the fixation, leading to hardware failure. First step — tumor removal by intralesional curettage or resection; second step — reconstruction.

**4. Polymethylmethacrylate cement (PMMA).** Used freely to reinforce internal fixation, fill bone defects, and provide immediate structural support. The cement increases the diameter of the "intramedullary" construct — stiffness in bending increases with the fourth power of diameter, strength with the third. Cement-augmented intramedullary fixation provides stability that pure metallic fixation cannot achieve in pathological bone. Biological reconstruction (bone graft) is inappropriate — patients have no time to heal biologically and ongoing chemotherapy impairs healing.

**5. Joint replacement for peri-articular lesions.** Endoprosthetic reconstruction is preferred over osteosynthesis for peri-articular lesions, particularly femoral head and neck lesions, where the implant does not depend on healing of pathological bone. Hemiarthroplasty or total hip arthroplasty for a metastatic neck or head fracture; shoulder hemiarthroplasty for a proximal humerus metastasis with head destruction.

**6. Preoperative selective arterial embolization.** Mandatory for renal cell, thyroid, and other highly vascular metastases, where blood loss can otherwise be catastrophic (loss of 2–3 liters is not exceptional). Embolization is performed 24–48 hours before surgery; combined hemostatic balloon occlusion of the internal iliac artery for large pelvic metastases is an option.

**Patient selection.** Bickels and Malawer emphasize that patients with an expected life span of less than 3 months are not candidates for major reconstructive procedures — they are managed with smaller palliative measures (immobilization, radiotherapy, protected loading). An expected life span over 3 months justifies surgical intervention.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Closed nailing of a pathological fracture without tumor removal almost always fails — tumor progression in the postoperative radiotherapy field leads to hardware failure.</li>
    <li>Renal cell, thyroid, and other hypervascular metastases require preoperative selective embolization — otherwise blood loss can be life-threatening.</li>
    <li>Biological reconstruction (bone graft, cementless implant) is inappropriate for metastatic surgery — patients have no time or biological environment in which to heal.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Spinal Metastases and the SINS Score
<details id="spine-mets">
<summary>Spinal Metastases and the SINS Score</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Spinal metastases require special consideration because of the proximity of the spinal cord and the high frequency of involvement (about 60% of all bone metastases). The classical surgical indications are progressive neurological deficit, mechanical instability, intractable pain not controlled by other measures, and the need for tissue diagnosis.

**SINS — Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score.** Developed by the Spine Oncology Study Group (Fisher et al., 2010), SINS assesses six features for classifying metastatic spinal instability:

1. **Location.** Junctional zones (occipito-C2, C7-T2, T11-L1, L5-S1) — 3 points; mobile segments (C3-C6, L2-L4) — 2; semi-rigid (T3-T10) — 1; rigid (S2 and below) — 0.
2. **Pain type.** Mechanical pain (on loading, relieved by rest) — 3; non-mechanical pain — 1; pain-free — 0.
3. **Lesion type.** Lytic — 2; mixed — 1; blastic — 0.
4. **Spinal alignment.** Subluxation or translation — 4; deformity without translation — 2; normal — 0.
5. **Vertebral body collapse.** Above 50% — 3; below 50% — 2; no collapse but > 50% involvement — 1; none — 0.
6. **Posterolateral element involvement.** Bilateral — 3; unilateral — 1; none — 0.

**Interpretation.** 0–6 — stable; 7–12 — potentially unstable (surgical consultation); 13–18 — unstable (surgical management typically indicated). SINS is not a staging system — it does not determine whether and when to operate, but contributes to risk stratification and supports communication between the radiation oncologist and the spinal surgeon.

**Patchell trial.** The seminal randomized clinical trial of Patchell et al. (2005) demonstrated that surgical decompression and stabilization plus radiotherapy is superior to radiotherapy alone in patients with metastatic spinal cord compression who can tolerate surgery. Maintained ambulation in the surgical arm — 84% vs. 57% in the radiotherapy arm; median maintained-ambulation duration — 122 vs. 13 days. This trial fundamentally changed the standard of care.

**Surgical techniques.**

- **Posterior decompression with instrumented fusion** — standard for a posterior compressive component and where stabilization is needed. Laminectomy and pedicle-screw instrumentation two or more segments above and below the lesion.
- **Anterior corpectomy** with reconstruction — typically with expandable titanium cages and an anterolateral plate. Used for an anterior compressive component (vertebral body) and when radical resection is needed.
- **360° (combined anterior-posterior) approach** — for extensive lesions involving all three columns.
- **Minimally invasive percutaneous pedicle-screw fixation** — increasing role for stabilization without decompression in selected cases.
- **Kyphoplasty and vertebroplasty** — for compression fractures without neurological deficit and without epidural extension. Cement-injected augmentation provides structural support and pain relief.

**Radiosurgery (stereotactic body radiotherapy, SBRT).** An increasingly important tool for oligometastases, for radioresistant tumors (renal cell, melanoma, sarcoma), and after surgical decompression for local control. Doses of 16–24 Gy in one or three fractions give substantially higher local control than conventional radiotherapy.

</div>
</details>

## Pathological Fractures
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="pathological-fractures">
<details>
<summary>Pathological Fractures</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Definition and Principles
<details id="pf-definition">
<summary>Definition and Principles</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

A pathological fracture, by definition, is a fracture that occurs through abnormal bone — bone weakened by tumor, metabolic disease, infection, or congenital anomaly. The management of a pathological fracture is governed by three considerations: **the nature of the underlying disease**; **the location of the fracture**; and **the patient's overall condition, prognosis, and life expectancy**.

**Metastatic disease.** The principles of the previous section apply: a long intramedullary device protecting the entire bone, cement augmentation, immediate weight-bearing where possible, joint replacement for peri-articular lesions, and preoperative embolization for hypervascular primaries. The aim is palliative — pain relief, restoration of function, maintenance of ambulation.

**Primary malignant bone tumors.** The approach is fundamentally different: the operative goal is curative resection of the tumor, and the fracture is treated as part of the oncological resection rather than as an independent fracture. Pathological fracture through a primary high-grade sarcoma was historically considered an indication for amputation; modern series show that limb salvage is feasible in selected cases provided that adequate margins can still be achieved and that the soft-tissue contamination has not violated multiple compartments. The decision requires multidisciplinary evaluation by orthopedic oncologist, pathologist, and medical/radiation oncologist.

**Benign lesions.** For pathological fractures through benign lesions (particularly simple bone cyst, fibrous dysplasia, and enchondroma) the fracture itself is often the precipitating event for diagnosis and treatment; in many cases the fracture is managed by closed reduction and immobilization, with the underlying lesion addressed once the fracture has healed. The simple bone cyst of the proximal humerus, for example, frequently shows partial or complete spontaneous healing after pathological fracture — the phenomenon known as "cyst healing fracture", which makes active treatment unnecessary in a substantial fraction of cases.

</div>
</details>


### Proximal Femur
<details id="proximal-femur">
<summary>Proximal Femur</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The proximal femur is the site at which most metastatic pathological fractures occur. The anatomic features dictate the surgical approach:

**Femoral neck fractures.** In metastatic disease are generally treated by **hemiarthroplasty or total hip arthroplasty**, not by internal fixation. Two reasons: a high rate of nonunion through diseased bone (healing is compromised by the tumor and by chemo/radiotherapy) and avascular necrosis of the head from compromised blood supply through the diseased femoral neck. A long-stem implant with cement is the standard; with good acetabular bone — hemiarthroplasty; with acetabular involvement or pre-existing osteoarthritis — total arthroplasty.

**Intertrochanteric fractures.** Generally treated by **long cephalomedullary nail** (long PFN, gamma nail, IMHS) with the option of additional cement augmentation. The long nail protects the entire femoral diaphysis from secondary foci.

**Subtrochanteric fractures.** Same principle — long cephalomedullary nail. Cement augmentation for large lytic defects. An alternative for extensive bone loss: proximal femoral replacement endoprosthesis.

**Head involvement without fracture.** Hemiarthroplasty or total arthroplasty for large lytic lesions (> 50% of head volume).

</div>
</details>


### Diaphyseal Pathological Fractures
<details id="diaphysis">
<summary>Diaphyseal Pathological Fractures</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Diaphyseal pathological fractures of the femur or humerus are treated by **long intramedullary nail**, with cement augmentation through a small window at the fracture site for large lytic defects. Principles:

- Nail length protecting the entire bone (femoral nail from trochanter to condyles; humeral nail from head to olecranon fossa).
- Static locking proximally and distally.
- Tumor curettage and cement augmentation for defects > 2 cm.
- Immediate weight-bearing (humerus — partial, owing to the need for rotational stability).

For renal cell carcinoma and other chemo/radioresistant tumors, **open resection-cement-nailing** is more reliable than closed nailing, as residual tumor in the defect progresses and leads to hardware failure.

</div>
</details>


### Peri-Articular and Other Sites
<details id="periarticular">
<summary>Peri-Articular and Other Sites</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Distal femur.** Distal femoral pathological fractures may require a **distal femoral replacement endoprosthesis** if cortical reconstitution is not possible. For smaller lesions — locked plate with cement augmentation.

**Proximal humerus.** Hemiarthroplasty for significant bone loss with destruction of the head; intramedullary nail for diaphyseal location with an intact head. Resection-prosthetic composite for large lesions with soft-tissue invasion.

**Acetabulum.** Complex anatomy — the simple rule "size from bone diameter" does not apply. Standard approach (Harrington, 1981): acetabular curettage, cement reconstruction with Steinmann pins driven from the acetabular dome rim into intact pelvic bone, total hip arthroplasty with a "native" acetabulum embedded in cement. For extensive lesions — prosthetic cones, augments, or specialized megaprostheses.

**Distal humerus and forearm.** Rare sites; decisions are individualized, with a tendency to plate-and-cement in younger patients and prosthetic reconstruction for substantial articular lesions.

**Spine.** See the spinal-metastasis section (SINS score, anterior/posterior approach, kyphoplasty/vertebroplasty).

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Metastatic femoral neck fractures should be replaced (hemi- or total hip arthroplasty), not internally fixed — nonunion through diseased bone and AVN of the compromised head are predictable.</li>
    <li>Renal cell, thyroid, and other hypervascular metastases require preoperative selective embolization before any surgery.</li>
    <li>Closed nailing without tumor removal in radioresistant primaries leads to tumor progression in the radiotherapy field and hardware failure — open resection with cement-nail reconstruction is the standard.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

</div>

## Limb-Salvage Surgery and Amputation
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="limb-salvage">
<details>
<summary>Limb-Salvage Surgery and Amputation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The emergence of limb-salvage surgery in the late 1970s transformed the therapeutic philosophy of orthopedic oncology. Before effective neoadjuvant chemotherapy, introduced by Rosen, Marcove, and Huvos at Memorial Sloan Kettering and by Jaffe at MD Anderson around 1971–1978, amputation was the only local treatment for osteosarcoma, with a five-year survival of about 20%. The small orthopedic-oncology units at Northwestern (Enneking), the Mayo Clinic (Sim, Pritchard), Rizzoli (Campanacci), and Memorial Sloan Kettering formulated the concept: with sufficient tumor control from chemotherapy, local treatment could be wide resection with limb preservation, without significant compromise of oncological outcome.

**Malawer and Sugarbaker** in their textbook *Musculoskeletal Cancer Surgery* (2004) emphasize that today approximately **90–95% of all bone and soft-tissue sarcomas can be treated by limb-salvage surgery**. Amputation is reserved for specific situations:

- Inability to achieve adequate surgical margins by a limb-salvage operation.
- Significant neurovascular involvement that cannot be reconstructed.
- Extensive pathological fracture with multi-compartment contamination.
- Informed patient preference (younger, highly active patients with distal tumors sometimes choose amputation because a modern prosthesis can provide better function).
- In cases of local recurrence not amenable to limb-salvage revision.

**Reconstructive options** include endoprosthetic replacement (the standard for most sites), structural allograft, allograft-prosthetic composite, vascularized fibular autograft (particularly applicable in diaphyseal and metaphyso-diaphyseal sites), and in the immature skeleton — expandable endoprostheses (minimally invasive magnetic-lengthening implants) or rotationplasty (Van Nes — functionally excellent, psychologically demanding reconstruction in which the foot is rotated 180° and the ankle functions as a knee).

**Hemipelvectomy** (internal or external) — reserved for extensive pelvic sarcomas (chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma) and for recurrent lesions after a first limb-salvage resection. Internal hemipelvectomy (with reconstruction via sacro-iliac arthrodesis, saddle prosthesis, or specialized megaprostheses) is technically demanding but functionally far superior to external (full hindquarter) amputation.

**Functional outcomes.** For most sites the functional outcomes of modern limb-salvage surgery (as measured on the MSTS scale) are significantly better than those of amputation, especially in the lower limb. For the distal femur and proximal humerus, endoprosthetic reconstruction gives 65–80% of normal function; for the proximal femur (a more critical zone for static balance) — 50–70%. Long-term complications include aseptic loosening, infection (particularly in patients on ongoing chemotherapy), and articular surface wear.

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Complications and Outcomes
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Complications and Outcomes</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Complications of orthopedic oncologic surgery group by site and procedure. The summary below outlines typical issues by intervention.

**Limb-salvage resection with endoprosthetic reconstruction.** Early complications — infection (5–10%, higher in patients on chemotherapy), neurovascular injury, skin necrosis with large flaps. Late complications — aseptic loosening (higher in cemented endoprostheses in an irradiated or remodeled bed), hardware failure, polyethylene wear, capsular loosening; the interval from primary oncologic resection to reoperation for aseptic loosening — about 50% at 15 years, particularly in younger patients. Deep prosthetic infection often mandates a two-stage revision (explantation, antibiotic spacer, reimplantation after 6 weeks) or, in the worst case, amputation.

**Allograft-prosthetic composite.** Complications shared with endoprosthetic reconstruction, plus allograft junction nonunion (10–20%), allograft fracture (10%), allograft resorption (5–10%).

**Vascularized fibular autograft.** Graft non-take (under 5% in experienced centers), stress fracture of the graft (5–10%), delayed hypertrophy in high-load reconstruction.

**Amputation.** Early — skin necrosis, infection, bleeding; late — phantom pain, neuroma, prosthesis-related problems. Psychological adaptation is substantial — multidisciplinary rehabilitation is standard.

**Metastatic surgery (palliative).** Hardware failure (5–15%, higher with tumor progression or with skipped tumor resection), local tumor progression (10–20% in the radiotherapy field), peri-prosthetic fracture, infection (higher with immunocompromise and continuing systemic therapy), pulmonary embolism (increased risk through the combination of tumor-induced hypercoagulability, immobilization, and megaprosthetic surgery).

**Sacrectomy.** Wound complications (15–25% — large surface area, poor blood supply, frequent prior radiotherapy), incontinence (predictable in high sacrectomies — see chordoma section), impotence in men, CSF fistula, thromboembolism. Pelvic-ring reconstruction is a stepping stone of severe complications.

**Long-term chemotherapy effects.** Cardiotoxicity of doxorubicin (cumulative-dose-dependent, threshold around 320 mg/m²), nephrotoxicity of cisplatin and methotrexate, ototoxicity of cisplatin, infertility, secondary malignancies (particularly acute myeloid leukemia after alkylating agents), delayed growth in children.

**Prognostic factors.** Common prognostic indicators for primary malignant bone tumors: histological grade, tumor size, site (axial vs. appendicular), Enneking stage, response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Huvos > 90% necrosis), age, pre-treatment LDH and alkaline phosphatase, specific molecular markers (P-glycoprotein in osteosarcoma — drug resistance).

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopedic school and the eponymous USBALO "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. In his three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) he devoted chapters to primary and metastatic bone tumors, pathological fractures, and the operative tactic for each. The Boychev chapters introduced into the Bulgarian curriculum the systematic distinction between traumatic and pathological fracture and the principles of biopsy before definitive treatment that to this day define the teaching standard.

**P. Tanchev and G. Kaymakchiev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** A modification of percutaneous vertebral biopsy with a thick needle and a wedge-cancellous core key for obtaining a solid bone core — particularly applicable to suspected chordoma and to vertebral bodies with dense cortical bone in the lumbar and sacral regions, the typical sites of chordoma. Used at USBALO "Boychev" as an alternative to CT-guided trocar biopsy in patients in whom full tomographic equipment is not accessible.

**V. Tanchev and G. Kaymakchiev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Principles of operative tactic for pathological fractures of the spine, including indications for instrumented posterior stabilization in metastatic disease and in pathological fracture through a primary sarcoma; these set the basis of modern Bulgarian practice in spinal tumor surgery.

**A. Chervenyakov et al. <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Chapters in the Bulgarian curriculum on orthopedic oncology covering the treatment of metastatic proximal-femur fractures with long cephalomedullary nails and cement augmentation; indications for hemiarthroplasty in metastatic femoral neck fractures consistent with the Bickels-Malawer principles.

From the classical Russian and Ukrainian school, embedded in the Bulgarian curriculum:

**Ya. L. Tsivyan <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Spinal oncologic surgery with anterior-posterior vertebral body resection for tumor involvement and instability; the conceptual basis for modern corpectomy with expandable cage and pedicle stabilization.

**A. A. Korzh <sup class="natflag">UA</sup>.** Reconstructive pelvic surgery for malignant tumors of the acetabulum and proximal femur; modifications of the classical internal hemipelvectomy with reconstruction through massive allografts and specialized constructs.

**G. A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Transosseous osteosynthesis and the technique of segmental bone transport for large bone defects found application in reconstructive orthopedic oncology — the Ilizarov principle of bone regeneration in the distraction gap is an alternative to allograft and vascularized fibular autograft in young patients with diaphyseal tumors.
