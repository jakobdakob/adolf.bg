## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Musculoskeletal infections span the full spectrum, from acute hematogenous osteomyelitis of childhood through implant-related infections of contemporary joint replacement to persistent chronic osteomyelitis after open fracture and the indolent osteoarticular tuberculosis still endemic in much of the world. Successful management depends on rapid recognition, accurate microbiological diagnosis, antibiotic therapy guided by culture and sensitivity, and (in many cases) definitive surgical debridement. This chapter presents the pathogenesis and pathologic anatomy of pyogenic and tuberculous bone infection, the principal classifications (Cierny-Mader for chronic osteomyelitis, Tuli for osteoarticular tuberculosis, Kocher for pediatric hip septic arthritis), the principles of imaging and microbiological sampling, the disease-by-disease treatment (acute hematogenous osteomyelitis, subacute osteomyelitis and Brodie's abscess, chronic osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, Pott's disease, hip and knee tuberculosis, peripheral tuberculous arthritis, cold abscess, necrotizing fasciitis), and the classical Bulgarian and Russian school that remains part of the orthopaedic curriculum in Bulgaria.

### Pyogenic Infection — Sequence of Destruction
<details id="pyogenic-pathology">
<summary>Pyogenic Infection — Sequence of Destruction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Acute hematogenous osteomyelitis is predominantly a childhood disease, with the peak incidence in the first decade and a male predominance of approximately 2:1. The metaphysis of the long bones (especially the distal femur, proximal tibia, distal tibia, and proximal humerus) is the characteristic site. The pathogenetic basis of this metaphyseal predilection is the anatomy of the metaphyseal vascular supply in the immature skeleton: metaphyseal arteries terminate in slow, hairpin loops adjacent to the growth plate, with limited collateral flow and a paucity of reticuloendothelial elements; circulating bacteria readily lodge in this stagnant capillary bed, where local hypoxia and the absence of phagocytic clearance permit rapid proliferation.

Once established, the infection produces inflammation, edema, and the release of cytokines and prostaglandins; rising intramedullary pressure compromises the marrow vasculature, and bacteria can spread through the cortex into the subperiosteal space, where elevation of the periosteum produces the characteristic clinical periosteal pain and tenderness. From the subperiosteal abscess, infection can extend further into the surrounding soft tissues or, in young children whose metaphysis lies within the joint capsule (notably the hip, to a lesser extent the shoulder, elbow, and ankle), directly into the joint to produce a concurrent septic arthritis.

The pattern of bone destruction in established acute osteomyelitis follows a well-described sequence. Within the first week the infection is purely cellulitic; by 7–10 days bony destruction becomes visible, with focal trabecular resorption and patchy radiographic lucency. As the inflammatory exudate compresses the local arteries, segments of bone become avascular; this dead bone is termed the sequestrum and remains embedded within the infected tissue. The periosteum, elevated by the subperiosteal abscess, lays down new bone (the involucrum) which surrounds the sequestrum like a sleeve. Sinus tracks (cloacae) connect the inner cavity with the skin, permitting drainage of pus and small bony fragments. Without effective treatment the disease becomes chronic, with persistent infection within the dead bone of the sequestrum that no systemic antibiotic can reach.

    <li>Intracapsular metaphysis of the hip → hematogenous osteomyelitis can rupture directly into the joint with secondary septic arthritis (severe in neonates).</li>
    <li>Plain radiographs remain normal in the first 7–10 days; destruction becomes visible only after day 10–14.</li>
  </ul>

### Tuberculous Infection — Tubercle, Caseation, Cold Abscess
<details id="tb-pathology">
<summary>Tuberculous Infection — Tubercle, Caseation, Cold Abscess</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Osteoarticular tuberculosis is always the result of hematogenous dissemination of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* from a primary visceral focus (lung, mediastinal or mesenteric lymph nodes, kidneys). Skeletal involvement typically occurs 2–3 years after the primary infection. Spread is through the arterial circulation or, for the axial skeleton, through Batson's paravertebral venous plexus — the latter explaining the simultaneous involvement of two adjacent paradiscal vertebral halves in classical Pott's disease (Tuli et al., 1974). About 7% of cases of spinal tuberculosis show "skip lesions" along the vertebral column, and about 12% have concomitant lesions in other bones and joints.

**The tubercle.** After infection is inoculated, polymorphonuclear cells accumulate and are rapidly replaced by macrophages and monocytes (mononuclears). Bacilli are phagocytosed; their lipids disperse in the cytoplasm of mononuclears, transforming them into epithelioid cells — the characteristic morphological hallmark of the tuberculous reaction. Langhans giant cells form by fusion of epithelioid cells and often contain bacilli; their function is to digest and remove necrotic tissue. After about a week, lymphocytes form a ring around the periphery of the lesion. This cellular mass forms the nodule known as the tubercle. Tubercles grow by expansion and coalescence; during the second week central caseous necrosis develops, caused by the protein fraction of the bacilli.

**Cold abscess.** Pronounced exudative reaction is characteristic of tuberculous skeletal infection. The cold abscess is a collection of liquefaction products and reactive exudate — serum, leukocytes, caseous material, bone debris, and tubercle bacilli. The abscess penetrates ligaments in joint disease, bone and periosteum in osseous disease, and migrates along fascial planes and along vessels and nerves. The "cold abscess" is not truly cold — it feels warm on palpation, but lacks the temperature surge of pyogenic infection. A superficial abscess may secondarily infect and behave as pyogenic; it may rupture, leaving a sinus or ulcer whose walls are lined with tuberculous granulations.

**Tuberculous sequestrum.** Hyperemia and severe osteoporosis accompany tuberculous infection. Bone destruction is by lysis of the bone, which softens and yields under gravity and muscle action, leading to compression, collapse, or deformation. Additional ischemic necrosis arises from thromboembolic phenomena, endarteritis, and periarteritis. Radiographically, tuberculous sequestra are finer, described as "coarse sand" or "feathery sequestra" — distinct from the dense, well-defined pyogenic sequestra (Cleveland and Bosworth, 1942; Girdlestone, 1950).

**Sparing of cartilage.** Tuberculous granulation tissue does not form proteolytic enzymes within the joint cavity. Articular cartilage destruction begins peripherally — where the pannus grows in the subchondral zone and undermines the margins of the articular surface. The central weight-bearing zones are preserved for many months, providing the potential for good functional recovery with effective treatment. This is directly opposite to pyogenic arthritis, where bacterial proteases and host matrix metalloproteinases begin central cartilage destruction within 8 hours.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Tuli (1965–67, Banaras Hindu University): analysis of 1,074 lesions in 980 patients showed distribution — spine 440 (41%), hip 81 (8%), knee 89 (8%), sacroiliac 69, elbow 51, ankle 43, tarsals 39, calcaneus 32, metatarsals/phalanges 44, greater trochanter/bursa 21, shoulder 17; vertebral localisation ≈50%, extraspinal ≈50%.</li>
    <li>The "paradiscal" pattern in the spine (simultaneous involvement of the upper and lower halves of two adjacent vertebrae about the disc) is pathognomonic of Pott's disease, reflecting a shared arterial blood supply.</li>
    <li>Central weight-bearing cartilage is preserved for a long time in tuberculous arthritis — an opportunity for functional recovery. Pyogenic arthritis destroys cartilage within hours.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Biofilm and Implant-Related Infection
<details id="biofilm">
<summary>Biofilm and Implant-Related Infection</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is the most consequential complication of total joint arthroplasty. The microbiological pattern reflects skin flora (coagulase-negative staphylococci, *Staphylococcus aureus* including MRSA, *Cutibacterium acnes* for shoulder arthroplasty) more than the hematogenous pattern of native osteomyelitis. Biofilm on the implant surface is the central pathogenic feature: bacteria encased in extracellular polysaccharide matrix become tolerant to antibiotics at concentrations many orders of magnitude above their planktonic MIC. Biofilm matures over 2–6 weeks — the clinical basis for the "acute" (≤4 weeks) versus "chronic" (>4 weeks) distinction in implant-related infection. Established biofilm infection typically requires implant removal; single-stage exchange, two-stage exchange (the standard for chronic infection in most centers), DAIR (debridement, antibiotic therapy, and implant retention — appropriate for early postoperative or acute hematogenous infection with a well-fixed implant and a treatable organism), and chronic suppressive therapy are the principal therapeutic options.

The Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS 2018) criteria, validated by Parvizi et al., provide a diagnostic framework with major criteria (two positive cultures of the same organism; sinus tract communicating with the joint) and minor criteria (synovial fluid leukocyte count, neutrophil leukocyte esterase, alpha-defensin, serum CRP and ESR, intraoperative purulence and histology).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Biofilm matures in 2–6 weeks; the "acute" vs. "chronic" distinction in implant-related infection reflects this biology, not just chronology.</li>
    <li>Sonication of removed components increases microbiological yield over conventional cultures (Trampuz et al., NEJM 2007).</li>
    <li>Synovial α-defensin — high specificity, particularly useful in patients on antibiotics.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Microbiology and Diagnosis
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="microbiology">
<details>
<summary>Microbiology and Diagnosis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

*Staphylococcus aureus* is the causative organism in approximately 70–90% of pediatric acute hematogenous osteomyelitis, with methicillin-resistant strains (MRSA) increasingly prominent in the developed world over the past two decades. In neonates, group B streptococcus and enteric Gram-negative organisms must also be considered. *Kingella kingae* has emerged as a leading pathogen of bone and joint infection in children aged 6 months to 4 years, often producing subtle clinical presentations with modestly elevated inflammatory markers; molecular detection by PCR of joint fluid and oropharyngeal swabs has substantially improved diagnostic yield. Group A streptococcus, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (now rare since universal vaccination) are less common pathogens. In patients with sickle cell disease, *Salmonella* species predominate alongside *Staphylococcus aureus* — one of the classical exam-relevant pairings in pediatric orthopedics. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is the classical organism of puncture wounds of the foot through a rubber-soled shoe (osteochondritis of the metatarsal heads and tarsal bones). Anaerobic infection should be considered in chronic, foul-smelling, polymicrobial infections — particularly diabetic foot infections and pelvic infections after open pelvic trauma.

Laboratory investigation reveals leukocytosis with neutrophilia, elevated ESR (typically peaking at 3–5 days), and elevated CRP (peaking earlier, at 1–2 days, and falling more rapidly with successful treatment). CRP is the most useful single marker for assessing response to therapy. Blood cultures should be obtained before antibiotic administration in every case and yield a causative organism in 30–60% of cases. Aspiration of any subperiosteal pus or adjacent joint effusion under ultrasound guidance where appropriate is essential for definitive microbiology. In suspected osteoarticular tuberculosis the diagnosis combines clinical suspicion in the appropriate epidemiological setting, IGRA (interferon-gamma release assay) or tuberculin skin testing, and definitive microbiological or histological diagnosis from CT-guided biopsy. Acid-fast staining is positive in only a minority of cases (15% in a Taiwanese 1989–1997 series); Löwenstein-Jensen culture (4–8 weeks) and PCR-based molecular diagnostics (GeneXpert MTB/RIF) are more sensitive and provide drug-resistance information.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Pediatric acute hematogenous osteomyelitis is *S. aureus* in 70–90% (MRSA increasingly), *Kingella kingae* aged 6 months to 4 years, *Salmonella* plus *S. aureus* in sickle cell disease — empirical regimens must reflect this triage.</li>
    <li>Blood cultures before the first antibiotic dose — yield falls steeply after even a single dose.</li>
    <li>CRP peaks at 1–2 days and falls fastest with effective therapy → the best single marker for tracking response.</li>
    <li>For TB: GeneXpert MTB/RIF (PCR + rifampicin resistance) is first-line; AFB smear positive in ≤15%.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Cierny-Mader (Chronic Osteomyelitis)
<details id="cierny-mader">
<summary>Cierny-Mader (Chronic Osteomyelitis)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Cierny-Mader system, developed in the 1980s, classifies chronic osteomyelitis along two axes: the anatomical type of bone involvement and the physiological status of the host. The four anatomical types are: **Type 1** — medullary osteomyelitis (intramedullary infection, e.g., after intramedullary nail infection); **Type 2** — superficial osteomyelitis (contiguous infection from an overlying soft-tissue defect, e.g., decubitus ulcer or burn); **Type 3** — localized osteomyelitis (full-thickness cortical involvement but with stable bone — typical of open fracture or adjacent implant infection); **Type 4** — diffuse osteomyelitis (segmental bony destruction with mechanical instability, "through-and-through" disease of hard and soft tissue). Posttraumatic osteomyelitis is almost always type 3 or type 4.

The host classes are: **A** — normal physiological status (no significant systemic or local compromise); **B** — compromised host, stratified as B-systemic (B<sub>S</sub>) for systemic factors (malnutrition, diabetes, immunosuppression, tobacco use), B-local (B<sub>L</sub>) for local factors (previous radiation, lymphedema, chronic venous insufficiency, major vascular disease), or combined (B<sub>S,L</sub>); and **C** — treatment more morbid than the disease (surgical intervention poses a greater risk than the infection itself; palliation or amputation is preferable). The combination of anatomical type and host class (for example Type 4-B<sub>L</sub>) drives treatment decisions: simple medullary infection in an A host responds to limited debridement and antibiotics, whereas diffuse infection in a B host may require staged limb reconstruction or amputation. Cierny (2003) reported limb salvage attempts in 91% of his patients with an overall success rate (infection-free, functional reconstruction at 2-year follow-up) of 84%, including 63% of patients classified as B hosts.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Cierny-Mader has two axes: anatomical type (1 medullary, 2 superficial, 3 localized full-thickness with stable bone, 4 diffuse with instability) and host class (A normal, B compromised systemic/local/combined, C treatment more morbid than disease). The combined label drives the decision between limited debridement, staged reconstruction, and amputation.</li>
    <li>Posttraumatic osteomyelitis ≈ always type 3 or 4 — the standard is radical debridement back to bleeding bone (Paprika sign) with dead-space management and stability restoration.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Tuli (Osteoarticular Tuberculosis)
<details id="tuli-stages">
<summary>Tuli (Osteoarticular Tuberculosis)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

S. M. Tuli (Banaras Hindu University, 1973) proposed and validated a clinico-radiologic stage classification of joint tuberculosis, applied equally to hip, knee, shoulder, elbow, and ankle. Each stage has specific therapeutic implications.

**Stage I — synovitis.** Range of motion preserved >75% of normal; limitation only in terminal degrees or in selected directions. Clinically: soft-tissue swelling and synovial effusion. Radiographically: only soft-tissue shadow and juxta-articular osteoporosis, with no destruction or erosion and no joint-space narrowing. *Treatment:* chemotherapy plus splintage — rarely synovectomy. *Expected outcome:* preservation of nearly full mobility.

**Stage II — early arthritis.** Range 50–75%; limitation in all directions. Radiographically, in addition to synovitis, slight joint-space narrowing and marginal bony erosions. *Treatment:* chemotherapy plus (occasionally) synovectomy or debridement. *Expected outcome:* recovery of 50–75% of range.

**Stage III — advanced arthritis.** Range <25% (≈75% loss) in all directions. Radiographically, in addition to early arthritis, marked joint-space narrowing and gross destruction of the articular surfaces and bone ends. *Treatment:* chemotherapy and usually arthrodesis in lower-extremity joints (arthroplasty at elbow or shoulder may be considered after skeletal maturity). *Expected outcome:* ankylosis in a position of function.

**Stage IV — advanced arthritis with subluxation/dislocation.** Stage III plus joint deformity from subluxation or dislocation — for example "wandering acetabulum" or pathological hip dislocation, the "triple deformity of the knee" (flexion + external rotation + posterior tibial subluxation), anterior subluxation/dislocation of the wrist, anterior or posterior subluxation of the ankle. *Treatment:* chemotherapy plus surgery, usually arthrodesis with grossly disorganized ankylosis.

**Stage V — terminal or aftermath arthritis.** Joint ankylosis with adapted articular margins, subchondral eburnation (in fibrous ankylosis), degenerative changes, marginal bony buttressing. *Treatment:* on assessment — corrective osteotomy for functional position, arthrodesis, or, if a mobile joint is desired (especially elbow or hip), excisional arthroplasty; formal arthroplasty deferred to skeletal maturity.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Joint positions in active disease (Tuli, "position of ease" vs. "position of function"):
      <ul>
        <li>Hip — ease: flexion, abduction, external rotation. Function (for ankylosis): flexion 10–30°, neutral abduction, neutral rotation.</li>
        <li>Knee — ease: flexion. Function: flexion 5–10° (lets the foot clear the ground in gait).</li>
        <li>Ankle — ease: slight flexion + inversion. Function: 90° (plantigrade neutral).</li>
        <li>Wrist — ease: slight palmar flexion. Function: ≈10° dorsiflexion (for a firm grip).</li>
        <li>Elbow — ease: flexion 90° + semi-pronation. Function: 90° flexion + semi-pronation; in bilateral disease left 45°, right 105° (hygiene and feeding).</li>
        <li>Shoulder — ease: abduction. Function: abduction 50°, forward flexion 30°, internal rotation 30° ("salute position").</li>
      </ul>
    </li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Kocher (Pediatric Hip Septic Arthritis)
<details id="kocher">
<summary>Kocher (Pediatric Hip Septic Arthritis)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Kocher criteria (Kocher, Zurakowski, Kasser, *J Bone Joint Surg Am*, 1999; validated by Caird et al., 2006), developed for distinguishing septic arthritis of the hip from transient synovitis in children, comprise four clinical findings: **refusal to bear weight**, **fever >38.5 °C**, **ESR >40 mm/h**, and **WBC >12,000/mm³**. The probability of true septic arthritis is <0.2% with zero criteria, 3% with one, 40% with two, 93% with three, and 99% with four. CRP >2 mg/dL has been added in subsequent refinements as a powerful fifth predictor. With three or more criteria, urgent joint aspiration under ultrasound guidance with analysis of the synovial fluid (cell count, Gram stain, culture, PCR for *Kingella* in children aged 6 months to 4 years) is mandatory.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Kocher criteria: refusal to bear weight, fever >38.5 °C, ESR >40 mm/h, WBC >12,000/mm³. Probabilities 3%, 40%, 93%, 99% with 1–4 criteria. CRP >2 mg/dL — validated fifth predictor.</li>
    <li>≥3 criteria → urgent ultrasound-guided joint aspiration; do not delay for routine MRI.</li>
    <li>Articular cartilage destruction begins within 8 hours of bacterial colonization — septic arthritis is a surgical emergency.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Imaging
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="imaging">
<details>
<summary>Imaging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Plain radiographs are typically normal in the first 7–10 days of acute osteomyelitis; bony changes (focal lucency, periosteal new bone) appear later. MRI is the imaging modality of choice for early diagnosis: it identifies marrow edema (T2-hyperintense, T1-hypointense with gadolinium enhancement) within 1–3 days of symptom onset, accurately delineates the extent of bone involvement, identifies adjacent soft-tissue abscess, and distinguishes osteomyelitis from cellulitis. Bone scintigraphy (Tc-99m MDP triple phase) is sensitive but less specific than MRI; it retains a role in multifocal suspicion or when MRI is unavailable. 18F-FDG PET/CT is useful in chronic osteomyelitis and implant-related infection because it does not suffer from the metal artefact that limits MRI.

In tuberculous spondylitis MRI shows anterior vertebral destruction with subligamentous spread, a paravertebral abscess with smooth thick-walled rim, and disc preservation early in disease followed by disc destruction later — the exact opposite of pyogenic spondylitis, which affects the disc early and often simultaneously with the adjacent endplates. The classical radiographic findings in tuberculous arthritis are Phemister's triad: juxta-articular osteopenia, peripheral osseous erosions, and gradual joint-space narrowing. In advanced hip tuberculosis, irregular destruction of the femoral head and acetabulum with reactive osteolysis and minimal sclerosis — the "wandering acetabulum" — is characteristic. In Brodie's abscess, MRI demonstrates a fluid-containing cavity with surrounding marrow edema and the "double-line" or "penumbra" signs that point toward infection rather than tumor.

### Acute Hematogenous Osteomyelitis
<details id="aho">
<summary>Acute Hematogenous Osteomyelitis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The cornerstone of treatment is prompt antibiotic therapy guided by the most likely organisms, modified according to culture results. Empirical regimens vary by age and local resistance patterns: in regions of low MRSA prevalence, intravenous β-lactamase-resistant penicillin (flucloxacillin/nafcillin) or a first-generation cephalosporin (cefazolin) is standard; in regions of high MRSA prevalence, vancomycin or clindamycin is added or substituted; in neonates, broader coverage including a third-generation cephalosporin is appropriate; in children with sickle cell disease, coverage for *Salmonella* with a third-generation cephalosporin is added. Total duration is typically 3–6 weeks, with conversion from intravenous to oral therapy once clinical response is established and CRP is trending toward normal.

Surgical drainage is indicated for subperiosteal abscess demonstrated by imaging or aspiration; for concurrent septic arthritis; for inadequate clinical response to appropriate antibiotics at 48–72 hours; and for established chronic osteomyelitis with sequestrum or involucrum. The principle is straightforward: drain pus, debride necrotic tissue, obtain microbiological samples, and irrigate copiously. Open drainage of an intraosseous abscess or subperiosteal collection is typically performed through a small cortical window; the bone is left open for healing by secondary intention or filled with antibiotic-loaded bone substitute.

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Surgical drainage is mandatory for: subperiosteal abscess; concurrent septic arthritis; inadequate clinical response to appropriate antibiotics at 48–72 hours; chronic osteomyelitis with sequestrum or involucrum.</li>
    <li>The neonatal hip is a special case — its intracapsular metaphysis allows hematogenous osteomyelitis to rupture directly into the joint with severe consequences for the femoral head.</li>
    <li>OVIVA trial (Li et al., *NEJM* 2019): early conversion to oral therapy is non-inferior to prolonged intravenous therapy in many bone and joint infections — modern practice has progressively shortened intravenous courses.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>

### Subacute Osteomyelitis and Brodie's Abscess
<details id="subacute">
<summary>Subacute Osteomyelitis and Brodie's Abscess</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Subacute osteomyelitis is an indolent, localized infection — most commonly with *Staphylococcus aureus*, with a significant share of *Kingella* in the relevant age group — in which the host's immune response has partly contained the infection, producing a walled-off cavity rather than an aggressive spreading process. Brodie's abscess, described by Sir Benjamin Brodie in 1832, is the classical form: a circumscribed lytic lesion of the metaphysis of a long bone (typically the proximal or distal tibia in a child or adolescent) surrounded by a halo of sclerotic bone, with minimal systemic features and a long history of intermittent localized pain. The differential diagnosis includes osteoid osteoma (smaller, more sclerotic, with a radial pain pattern and night pain responsive to NSAIDs), eosinophilic granuloma, and other lytic lesions of childhood. Treatment combines targeted antibiotic therapy and surgical debridement: the abscess is drained, cultured, and curetted; antibiotic therapy is continued for 4–6 weeks. Healing is usually uneventful but recurrence has been described.

</div>
</details>


### Chronic Osteomyelitis
<details id="chronic-osteo">
<summary>Chronic Osteomyelitis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Chronic osteomyelitis is the persistent or recurrent infection of bone in which dead bone (sequestrum) maintains a focus of bacterial colonization that is largely inaccessible to systemic antibiotics and that periodically produces clinical flares of pain, drainage, or systemic illness. The conversion from acute to chronic osteomyelitis follows inadequate treatment of the acute episode. In the modern era, chronic osteomyelitis is largely a complication of open fractures, internal fixation hardware, and other trauma rather than a primary hematogenous condition; implant-related infection is the most common contemporary form.

The fundamental principle is that chronic osteomyelitis cannot be cured by antibiotics alone; surgical debridement is necessary to remove the dead bone that harbors the persistent infection. The standard sequence is: thorough surgical debridement to excise all dead and infected tissue back to bleeding bone (the "Paprika sign" of punctate bleeding from cortical bone is the surgical end-point); multiple intraoperative samples for microbiology (at least 5 representative samples); management of the resulting dead space with bone-graft substitutes, antibiotic-loaded cement spacers (PMMA with gentamicin/vancomycin), or vascularized tissue transfer; restoration of skeletal stability with internal or external fixation as appropriate; and administration of organism-directed parenteral antibiotic therapy for typically 4–6 weeks, followed by oral therapy for an additional period as clinical course dictates.

**Saucerization (Orr, Trueta).** The classical surgical principle — removal of the overlying bone above an intramedullary abscess ("saucer-like" unroofing of the cavity), resection of rigid walls, and free drainage of pus — described by H. Winnett Orr (Lincoln, Nebraska, 1929) and refined by Jose Trueta during the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) with cast immobilisation without dressing change. The "blood, time, antibiotics, debridement" (BTAD) concept is the modern equivalent.

**Reconstruction of segmental defects.** The Masquelet induced-membrane technique (Masquelet and Begue, 1986) and the Ilizarov bone transport (Kurgan school, 1950s–1970s) address segmental defects after debridement of diffuse osteomyelitis. Modern adjuncts include vacuum-assisted closure (VAC), antibiotic-loaded bioabsorbable calcium sulphate, and free muscle or fasciocutaneous flap reconstruction (latissimus dorsi, gracilis, ALT) for skeletal coverage.

Amputation remains a legitimate option for patients with extensive limb destruction, poor functional potential, or compromised hosts (Cierny C) in whom limb salvage is unrealistic.

  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Debride "by the centimetre" — 0.5 cm above and below the border of visible destruction, not exactly to it; tuberculous and pyogenic foci extend microscopically beyond their macroscopic limits.</li>
    <li>Do not rely on superficial swab from a sinus tract — it fails to reflect the deep flora in >50% of cases. Always intraoperative deep tissue samples.</li>
    <li>Implant-related infection: established biofilm infection (>4 weeks) on a chronic implant almost always requires implant removal. DAIR does not work against mature biofilm.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Septic Arthritis
<details id="septic-arthritis">
<summary>Septic Arthritis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Ruiz Santiago et al., <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:X-ray_of_septic_arthritis_of_the_hip.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY 4.0</a></span>

Septic arthritis is a surgical emergency. Cartilage destruction begins within 8 hours of bacterial colonization, driven by bacterial proteases and the host inflammatory cascade (matrix metalloproteinases, cathepsins); delay in treatment is the single greatest determinant of joint destruction. The hip, knee, and ankle are the most commonly affected joints in children; the pathogenic route is hematogenous in the majority of pediatric cases, with the occasional direct extension from adjacent osteomyelitis — particularly in the hip and shoulder, where the metaphysis is intracapsular.

Presentation is with acute joint pain, fever, refusal to bear weight or move the affected limb, and a held-fixed antalgic position (flexion-abduction-external rotation of the hip; flexion of the knee). Examination reveals warmth, swelling, restricted range of motion, and exquisite tenderness on attempted joint motion. Aspiration of the joint under anesthesia and sterile conditions (under ultrasound guidance in the hip) is the diagnostic gold standard: synovial fluid examined for cell count (>50,000 WBC/mm³ with >75% neutrophils), Gram stain, culture, and PCR for *Kingella* in the relevant age group establishes the diagnosis.

Treatment is urgent surgical drainage — open, arthroscopic, or, in selected cases of accessible joints with prompt presentation, repeated aspiration — combined with empirical antibiotic therapy adjusted by culture results. The pediatric hip USUALLY requires open arthrotomy (medial Ludloff approach in an infant, or anterior Smith-Petersen in an older child); the knee may be arthroscopically lavaged with excellent results. The total duration of antibiotic therapy is typically 3–4 weeks, with intravenous administration for the first 5–10 days followed by oral therapy once clinical response is established and CRP is falling.

In adults septic arthritis arises by hematogenous spread, direct inoculation (intra-articular injection, surgery), or extension from adjacent infection. The classical organisms are *Staphylococcus aureus* (50–60% overall, including MRSA in increasing proportion) and the streptococci. Gonococcal arthritis remains an important cause of polyarticular oligoarthritis in sexually active young adults — migratory arthralgia, tenosynovitis, and dermatitis; synovial cultures are positive in <50%, and urethral, cervical, and pharyngeal swabs are usually more useful for diagnosis.

  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Cartilage destruction begins within 8 hours — septic arthritis is a surgical emergency with minimal tolerance for delay.</li>
    <li>Pediatric hip septic arthritis ≈ open arthrotomy (Ludloff or Smith-Petersen). Arthroscopic drainage is acceptable for the knee and shoulder; reliance on repeated aspiration in the hip is associated with higher rates of return-to-theatre and lasting joint damage.</li>
    <li>Cryptic gonococcal disease in polyarthritis in a young adult — sample urethra, cervix, pharynx, not just the joint.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Spinal Tuberculosis (Pott's Disease)
<details id="spine-tb">
<summary>Spinal Tuberculosis (Pott's Disease)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-4/pott-disease.jpg" data-figure-alt="Pott's disease, tuberculous spondylitis with gibbus deformity" aria-label="Enlarge">

  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Pott's disease: tuberculous spondylitis with the classical gibbus deformity (acute angular kyphosis) from anterior wedge collapse of the affected vertebral bodies — historical clinical illustration from the Wellcome Collection.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Wellcome Collection, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tuberculosis%3B_spine_of_boy_under_care_of_Mr_Pott_Wellcome_L0006118.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY 4.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Spinal tuberculosis, originally described by Sir Percival Pott in a letter to the London Royal Surgical Society in 1779 ("Remarks on that kind of palsy of the lower limbs"), is the most common form of skeletal tuberculosis — about 50% of osteoarticular tuberculosis (Tuli, BHU 1965–67; Martini, 1988; Jain et al., 2007). The thoracolumbar junction is the most frequently affected region.

**Pathologic topography.** The infection typically begins paradiscally in the anterior aspect of the vertebral body — the two adjacent paradiscal segments (upper and lower half) are involved simultaneously, reflecting a shared arterial blood supply. The infection also spreads beneath the anterior longitudinal ligament to involve adjacent vertebrae (contrasting with pyogenic spondylitis, which spreads through the disc to the adjacent vertebra), and progresses to destruction of the disc and adjacent endplates with collapse of the vertebral body. The characteristic gibbus deformity (an acute angular kyphosis) results from anterior wedge collapse of the affected vertebrae. Besides the paradiscal pattern, central type (mid-body involvement with "concentric collapse"; often via Batson's plexus), anterior type (beneath the anterior longitudinal ligament), and posterior type (rare, involving the posterior elements) are described.

**Cold abscess.** Typical formation in the prevertebral, paraspinal, psoas, or retropharyngeal spaces. A psoas abscess from a lower thoracic or lumbar focus may present as a flank or groin mass remote from the spinal level of disease; in late stages it may track down the iliopsoas sheath into the subsartorial canal or even the posterior compartment of the thigh (CT-documented cases, Tuli). A retropharyngeal abscess from cervical Pott may cause dysphagia and threaten the airway.

**Pott's paraplegia — Tuli classification.** Neurological involvement occurs in 10–20% of cases through direct compression from the abscess and necrotic debris ("early-onset paraplegia", generally reversible with treatment), through progressive bony deformity ("late-onset paraplegia"), or through intrinsic cord injury (thrombosis of end arteries, granulomatous myelitis). Tuli (1969, 1973) classified tuberculous paraplegia into four stages based on motor deficit:

- **Stage I — negligible.** Patient unaware of neural deficit; physician detects plantar extensor response and/or ankle clonus.
- **Stage II — mild.** Patient aware of deficit but manages to walk with support.
- **Stage III — moderate.** Non-ambulatory because of paralysis (in extension), sensory deficit <50%.
- **Stage IV — severe.** Stage III plus flexor spasms / paralysis in flexion / flaccid paralysis / sensory deficit >50% / sphincter involvement.

The classification applies to cord compression, not cauda equina. The higher the stage, the more severe the cord compression and the poorer the prognosis for neurological recovery. Flaccid paralysis is the terminal stage of progressive compressive myelopathy, often with associated arachnoiditis.

**Treatment.**

*Medical (primary).* Four-drug antituberculous chemotherapy (RIPE: rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) for 2 months, followed by rifampicin and isoniazid for a further 7–10 months (total 9–12 months). Extension for MDR/XDR-TB to 18–24 months with newer agents (bedaquiline, delamanid). Thoracic brace for 4–6 months for pain control and prevention of further deformity.

*Tuli's "middle path regimen".* Tuli (Banaras, 1975) championed a middle path between purely conservative (ambulatory chemotherapy) and purely operative care — operative indications limited to: progressive neurological deficit despite adequate chemotherapy; large paravertebral or intraspinal abscess with mass effect; instability with severe progressive deformity; failure of medical therapy; and persistent constitutional symptoms. In remaining cases, 95% success with ambulatory chemotherapy is documented in long series (Dutt et al., 1986).

*Surgical — the Hong Kong operation (Hodgson and Stock, 1956–1960).* Radical anterior debridement, removal of caseation and sequestra, anterior structural strut grafting (autograft from rib or iliac crest; alternatively a fibular strut), and combined posterior instrumentation for multilevel destruction or severe deformity. Approaches: for thoracic and upper lumbar levels — transthoracic; for a large paravertebral abscess without pleural opening — posterior costotransversectomy; for psoas abscess with distal thigh collection — anterior thigh incision along the medial border of m. sartorius at the inguinal crease; for lumbar and sacrolumbar TB without large collection — anterior extraperitoneal approach (Tenev or classical left-sided).

  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Pott begins paradiscally, spreads beneath the anterior longitudinal ligament, spares the disc until late. Pyogenic spondylitis starts from the disc and affects adjacent endplates early — the exact opposite.</li>
    <li>Standard antituberculous therapy: 2 months RIPE + 7–10 months R+I; total 9–12 months. MDR/XDR requires specialised regimens with newer-generation drugs.</li>
    <li>Surgical indications in Pott: progressive neurological deficit, large abscess with mass effect, instability/deformity >60°, failure of medical therapy. NOT — routine anterior radical operation in every case.</li>
    <li>Expected correction of the gibbus deformity after posterior correction and anterior stabilisation by Hong Kong technique: 30–50%, depending on rigidity and level.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Late-onset Pott's paraplegia by Tuli (decades after primary disease, from progressive deformity and cord stretching over the gibbus) carries a worse prognosis than early-onset; may require vertebral column resection and multilevel posterior instrumentation.</li>
    <li>Haemoptysis or sudden bleeding during anterior surgery for upper thoracic Pott — possible thoracic aortic erosion from prolonged destruction. Preoperative CT angiography is mandatory.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Tuberculosis of the Hip
<details id="hip-tb">
<summary>Tuberculosis of the Hip</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Tuberculosis of the hip accounts for about 15% of osteoarticular tuberculosis — the second most common site after the spine. The initial focus may be in the acetabular roof (most common), in the femoral head epiphysis or metaphysis (Babcock's triangle), or in the greater trochanter. Because the proximal femur is entirely intracapsular, any intracapsular bony focus rapidly involves the joint as "osteoarticular" disease with destruction of the femoral head and acetabulum. A cold abscess typically forms within the joint; the weaker inferior part of the capsule or (rarely) the acetabular floor may perforate and the abscess may track around the joint — to the femoral triangle, the medial, lateral, or posterior aspect of the thigh, the ischiorectal fossa, or the pelvis.

**Clinical presentation.** Limp is the earliest and most common symptom. Antalgic gait with shortest stance phase. Pain in the femoral triangle, medial to the greater trochanter, or indirectly elicited by bitrochanteric pressure or thumping. Spasm in the lower abdominal muscles and adductors of the thigh on sudden abduction-external rotation.

**Tuli stages.**
- **I — synovitis:** flexion + abduction + external rotation (apparent lengthening); preserved motion in terminal degrees.
- **II — early arthritis:** flexion + adduction + internal rotation (apparent shortening); restricted motion in all directions; marginal erosion and slight joint-space narrowing.
- **III — advanced arthritis:** flexion + adduction + internal rotation + true shortening; destruction of articular surfaces.
- **IV — advanced arthritis with subluxation/dislocation:** gross shortening; "wandering acetabulum" or pathological posterior dislocation.

**Treatment.** Standard antituberculous chemotherapy for 9–12 months. Stages I–II: chemotherapy plus bed rest, traction to correct deformity, controlled mobilisation; synovectomy is rarely required. Stage III: chemotherapy plus, in the lower limb, arthrodesis; in an adult with controlled active disease, a total hip arthroplasty becomes possible after at least 12 months from completion of chemotherapy, with continuation of dual antituberculous therapy through the perioperative period (risk of reactivation around the implant). Stage IV: chemotherapy plus surgery — typically arthrodesis in a young patient; excisional arthroplasty (Girdlestone) or arthrodesis in the elderly; arthroplasty deferred. Result: preservation of functional mobility in stages I–II; stable ankylosis in a position of function (flexion 10–30°, neutral abduction and rotation) in more advanced disease.

</div>
</details>


### Tuberculosis of the Knee
<details id="knee-tb">
<summary>Tuberculosis of the Knee</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Tuberculosis of the knee accounts for about 8–10% of osteoarticular tuberculosis. Presentation is with progressive monoarticular pain, synovial swelling, restricted motion, and often a positional flexion deformity. Radiographs show Phemister's triad: juxta-articular osteopenia, peripheral osseous erosions, and gradual joint-space narrowing. The "triple deformity of the knee" in advanced disease (Tuli stage IV) comprises flexion + external rotation + posterior tibial subluxation — pathognomonic of chronic tuberculous disease.

Treatment combines antituberculous chemotherapy for 9–12 months, synovectomy for active disease in stages I–II, and in advanced disease arthrodesis in a position of function (flexion 5–10°, neutral rotation) or, where active disease has been controlled and the patient is an adult, total knee arthroplasty with continued chemotherapy. Arthrodesis gives a durable, pain-free but rigid joint and remains the working choice in young patients with physically demanding work.

</div>
</details>


### Peripheral Tuberculous Arthritis and Tenosynovitis
<details id="peripheral-tb">
<summary>Peripheral Tuberculous Arthritis and Tenosynovitis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Tuberculous involvement of the sacroiliac joint (6% by Tuli), ankle and foot (tarsal bones, calcaneus; 14% combined), elbow (5%), wrist (2%; carpal bones and tendons), shoulder (1.5%), and short tubular bones of hand and foot (metacarpals, metatarsals, phalanges; 7% combined — *spina ventosa* in childhood) is less common but well described in the monographs of Tuli and of Kornev. The classical "compound palmar ganglion" — tuberculous tenosynovitis of the wrist flexors with a dumbbell-shaped swelling on either side of the flexor retinaculum — is a historical description still occasionally encountered. Tenosynovectomy combined with antituberculous chemotherapy is curative when applied at an early stage. *Spina ventosa* (tuberculous dactylitis) in a child produces fusiform swelling of the phalanx with ballooning involucrum — typically benign with adequate treatment but requiring differential diagnosis with other paediatric lesions (eosinophilic granuloma, enchondroma, osteoid osteoma).

</div>
</details>


### Cold Abscess — Drainage and Follow-up
<details id="cold-abscess">
<summary>Cold Abscess — Drainage and Follow-up</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

A cold abscess from osteoarticular tuberculosis is not managed by simple incision and drainage as for a pyogenic abscess. The principles are: **(1) aspiration through healthy skin** — needle or small trocar through intact, distant skin, never through a previously cracked tract; **(2) aspiration, not incision** — to avoid a chronic sinus tract; **(3) parallel broad-spectrum antituberculous therapy** — without chemotherapy the abscess will refill; **(4) indications for open drainage** — large abscess with mass effect (cord compression, airway obstruction from retropharyngeal abscess, obstruction of the cauda equina canal), persistent recurrence despite adequate chemotherapy, added pyogenic superinfection. Standard approaches: anterior thigh incision along the medial border of m. sartorius for psoas abscess with distal collection; posterior costotransversectomy for paraspinal abscess without pleural opening; retropharyngeal approach for cervical Pott with airway compression.

</div>
</details>


### Necrotizing Soft-Tissue Infections
<details id="necrotizing">
<summary>Necrotizing Soft-Tissue Infections</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Although technically not a bone or joint infection, necrotizing fasciitis must be addressed as a true orthopedic emergency in any review of musculoskeletal infection. The disease — group A streptococcal in about half of cases, polymicrobial in much of the remainder, and clostridial gas gangrene in a small but historically important fraction — produces rapidly progressive necrosis of the subcutaneous fat and fascia with relative sparing of muscle and skin. Presentation is often deceptively benign: localized erythema and tenderness, with **pain disproportionate to the apparent clinical findings**, progressing within hours to systemic toxicity, hypotension, and multi-organ failure. The LRINEC score (Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis), based on serum CRP, WBC, hemoglobin, sodium, creatinine, and glucose, supports clinical suspicion; LRINEC ≥6 is moderate suspicion, ≥8 high.

Treatment is immediate aggressive surgical debridement of all involved tissue (often requiring serial "second-look" debridements over several days) combined with broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy: clindamycin (suppresses streptococcal toxin production), penicillin G, vancomycin or linezolid for MRSA, and an aminoglycoside or quinolone for Gram-negative coverage. Mortality remains 20–30% even with optimal management; the single most important determinant of survival is the time from presentation to first surgical debridement — delay >24 hours doubles mortality.

  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Pain disproportionate to apparent skin findings, with rapid systemic toxicity = necrotizing fasciitis until proven otherwise. LRINEC supports suspicion but does not delay surgery.</li>
    <li>Immediate aggressive debridement + clindamycin (toxin suppression) + broad cover (penicillin, MRSA agent, Gram-negative) is the only intervention that reliably influences mortality.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Outcomes and Complications
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Acute hematogenous osteomyelitis in a child treated promptly with appropriate antibiotic therapy and selective drainage carries a favourable prognosis with complete cure in over 90% of patients. Complications — progression to chronic osteomyelitis, iatrogenic growth-plate injury during drainage of an intra-articular metaphysis, pathological fracture in extensive destruction — are limited by early diagnosis and adequate debridement when indicated. Chronic osteomyelitis, even with thorough debridement and targeted therapy, carries a recurrence rate of 5–20% on long-term follow-up; Cierny C hosts have significantly higher amputation rates.

Septic arthritis in a child treated within the first 4 days has an excellent prognosis with full functional recovery; delay >4 days correlates with permanent joint damage in a rising proportion (Gillick et al., 2006). The pediatric hip is particularly vulnerable — delayed drainage leads to septic necrosis of the femoral head with lasting deformity (Tönnis I–IV). Adult septic arthritis carries less catastrophic outcomes overall because of mature cartilage, but in immunocompromised and elderly patients mortality reaches 7–15%.

Pott's disease treated medically in an early stage (before major gibbus collapse) has an excellent result with remineralisation and spontaneous bone block formation at 18–24 months (documented by Tuli in lumbar Pott after ambulatory chemotherapy). Early-onset Pott's paraplegia recovers in 70–90% with early decompression and chemotherapy; late-onset paraplegia (from progressive deformity) has a worse prognosis with persistent weakness in a significant proportion even after correction and instrumentation. Tuberculosis of the hip and knee at stages I–II with modern chemotherapy gives functional recovery in >80%; stages III–IV — ankylosis or arthroplasty with less certain results and a risk of reactivation around the implant.

Implant-related infections remain a leading cause of revision arthroplasty in the developed world. Two-stage revision arthroplasty for chronic PJI carries 80–90% success in specialised centres, but higher mortality in elderly and compromised hosts. Chronic suppressive antibiotic therapy is an acceptable option in patients for whom further surgery is not feasible.

</div>

**P. Tanchev and G. Kaymakchiev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1983).** Modification of percutaneous vertebral biopsy with a thick needle and a cancellous-bitten wedge key to obtain a solid bone core — particularly applicable in suspected chordoma, but widely used for the diagnosis of tuberculous and pyogenic spondylitis in Bulgarian practice before the broad adoption of CT-guided biopsy.

From the classical Russian and Soviet school of osteoarticular tuberculosis, tightly woven into the Bulgarian curriculum:

  <strong>Classical lineage — tuberculous bone surgery</strong>
  <p>Spinal tuberculosis as a clinico-pathological entity was defined by Sir Percival Pott in his letter to the London Royal Surgical Society in 1779. Laennec described the microscopic tubercle in the early nineteenth century, and Koch (1882) identified *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. The pre-antituberculous era developed sanatorium regime and heliotherapy (Rollier in Switzerland; Krasnobaev in Moscow) as the basis of treatment. Antituberculous drugs (streptomycin 1944, isoniazid 1952, rifampicin 1968, pyrazinamide and ethambutol in the 1960s) transformed the prognosis. Hodgson and Stock described radical anterior debridement and spondylodesis (the "Hong Kong operation", 1956–1960). Tuli (Banaras Hindu University) synthesised the "middle path regimen" and validated the clinico-radiologic stage classification (1969–1973).</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Pott 1779</strong> — *Remarks on that kind of palsy of the lower limbs* (London).</li>
