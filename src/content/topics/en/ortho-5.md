## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The term "infantile osteoarthritis" denotes in classical orthopaedic teaching a specific clinical entity — the destructive, often multifocal pyogenic osteoarthritis of the neonate and young infant, with its characteristic pattern of insidious presentation, rapid bony and cartilaginous destruction, and high rates of permanent joint damage when diagnosis is delayed. Together with the biologically distinct entity of transient (toxic) synovitis of the hip in the older child, these two conditions form the central differential diagnosis of the limping or non-weight-bearing child. The clinical importance of distinguishing them is one of the cardinal teaching points of pediatric orthopaedics: septic arthritis is a surgical emergency that destroys the joint within hours, while transient synovitis is a self-limiting inflammatory condition that resolves over days.

## Anatomical Features of the Immature Joint
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="anatomy">
<details>
<summary>Anatomical Features of the Immature Joint</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Intracapsular Proximal Femoral Metaphysis
<details id="intracapsular-metaphysis">
<summary>Intracapsular Proximal Femoral Metaphysis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The first of two cardinal anatomical features that define the catastrophic potential of joint sepsis in the immature hip is the intracapsular position of the proximal femoral metaphysis. In the adult and the older child, the metaphysis of the long bones is extracapsular — metaphyseal osteomyelitis presents as a subperiosteal abscess and does not communicate directly with the joint cavity. In the neonatal and infantile hip, however, the joint capsule encloses not only the femoral head and neck but also the upper metaphysis, extending almost to the vasti origins on the proximal femur. Any infection developing in the metaphysis may rupture directly into the joint through the thin medial cortex of the femoral neck, producing a concurrent septic arthritis.

The principle applies to other joints with similar anatomy, although with smaller catastrophic potential: the proximal humerus lies within the shoulder joint, the radial head within the elbow joint, and the proximal fibula intracapsularly within the superior tibiofibular joint. In neonatal shoulder involvement, progression to complete destruction of the humeral head follows an analogous pathogenetic path.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The proximal femoral metaphysis, proximal humerus, radial neck, and distal fibula are intracapsular metaphyses — characteristic sites of osteomyelitis that breaches directly into the joint.</li>
    <li>About one-third of pediatric long-bone osteomyelitis cases have concurrent septic arthritis of the adjacent joint — mandatory adjacent-joint evaluation whenever metaphyseal osteomyelitis is confirmed.</li>
    <li>Multifocal involvement is common in the neonate: 30–50% of cases have simultaneous involvement of multiple joints and adjacent metaphyseal bone.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Vascular Supply of the Femoral Head
<details id="vascular-supply">
<summary>Vascular Supply of the Femoral Head</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The second cardinal anatomical feature is the vulnerability of the immature femoral head's vascular supply. The developing ossific nucleus of the head (which appears between months 4 and 7 after birth) is surrounded by an entirely cartilaginous epiphysis. Vascular supply derives from two main sources: the artery of the ligamentum teres (the foveolar branch of the obturator artery, which supplies mainly the ossific nucleus and the cartilage around the fovea capitis femoris) and the lateral epiphyseal group of retinacular branches from the medial femoral circumflex artery. The lateral epiphyseal vessels run on the posterosuperior surface of the femoral neck, penetrate the capsule at the retinaculum of Weitbrecht and enter the cartilaginous epiphysis.

These vessels are particularly vulnerable by two independent mechanisms in septic arthritis. First, raised intra-articular pressure from a purulent effusion thromboses the extraosseous retinacular vessels by direct compression — the clinical basis for the principle that "a joint under pressure is a joint losing the head's blood supply." Second, the septic inflammatory process induces thrombosis in the intracartilaginous vessels traversing the unossified epiphysis to reach the developing ossific nucleus. The combination of these two mechanisms explains why neonatal hip sepsis can produce, within days, complete avascular necrosis of the femoral head.

### Transphyseal Vessels and Age-Related Vulnerability
<details id="transphyseal">
<summary>Transphyseal Vessels and Age-Related Vulnerability</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In the neonate and during the first 12–18 months of life, before the formation of the physeal barrier, transphyseal vessels cross the growth plate and communicate between the metaphysis and the developing ossific nucleus in the epiphysis. This vascular communication serves as a two-way conduit through which infection from the metaphysis may enter the epiphysis and thence the joint. After about 18 months of age the physeal cartilage matures fully and blocks transphyseal flow — this anatomical transition explains why isolated metaphyseal osteomyelitis without concurrent joint sepsis becomes more common in the older child.

Age-stratified risk is therefore: the neonate (under 4 weeks) and the young infant (up to about 6 months) are at highest risk of multifocal osteoarthritis because the intracapsular metaphysis, open transphyseal vessels, and immature immune system coexist. The infant from 6 to 24 months retains the intracapsular metaphysis, but the physeal barrier is more mature; infant series still show a high proportion of combined osteoarthritis. Beyond 24 months, isolated septic arthritis without concurrent osteomyelitis predominates.

<aside class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>Note</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Transphyseal vessels are present until about 18 months of age — vascular communication between metaphysis and epiphysis that explains the frequency of multifocal osteoarticular involvement in the neonate.</li>
    <li>After 18 months the mature physeal barrier blocks transphyseal flow; the typical presentation shifts to isolated osteomyelitis or isolated septic arthritis.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Other Joints with Intracapsular Metaphyses
<details id="other-joints">
<summary>Other Joints with Intracapsular Metaphyses</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The principle of the intracapsular metaphysis is not limited to the hip. The shoulder joint encloses the proximal humerus, the elbow joint includes the radial head and proximal ulna, and the superior tibiofibular joint encloses the proximal fibula. In each of these locations metaphyseal osteomyelitis may rupture directly into the adjacent joint, producing a concurrent septic arthritis with the potential to destroy the epiphysis.

Bos and colleagues (1998) described late consequences of neonatal shoulder sepsis with complete resorption of the humeral head, analogous to the hip equivalent. The shoulder equivalent of "Tom Smith arthritis" is rarer but clinically equally devastating — adult patients present with a "flail shoulder" and severe functional impairment. The knee, the most commonly affected joint in the post-neonatal period (about 41% of cases by Jackson and Nelson, 1982), does not have an intracapsular metaphysis but is easily accessible to direct inoculation (for example, the infant kneeling on the tip of a hidden needle in the carpet — the classical picture from Mercer Rang).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Hip (23%), shoulder (4%), elbow (12%) and superior tibiofibular joint have intracapsular metaphyses — the topography that determines which joints are particularly susceptible to multifocal osteoarthritis in the neonate.</li>
    <li>The knee is the most commonly affected joint in the post-neonatal period (about 41%), but has no intracapsular metaphysis — typical presentation is isolated septic arthritis, not combined osteoarticular infection.</li>
    <li>Unrecognised neonatal shoulder sepsis produces complete resorption of the humeral head and a "flail shoulder", the shoulder equivalent of Tom Smith arthritis.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Hematogenous Seeding and Course of Joint Sepsis
<details id="pathogenesis-mech">
<summary>Hematogenous Seeding and Course of Joint Sepsis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Septic arthritis in the child arises by three distinct mechanisms. Hematogenous seeding is the most common route — bacteria from transient bacteraemia (upper respiratory infection, skin lesions, otitis, neonatal omphalitis, invasive procedures) enter the synovium through the low-resistance postcapillary venous plexus. The synovium has no basement membrane, making it particularly vulnerable to haematogenous implantation. The second mechanism is spread from adjacent metaphyseal osteomyelitis — where an intracapsular metaphysis is present (hip, shoulder, elbow, superior tibiofibular), cortical breakthrough discharges pus directly into the joint. The third mechanism is direct inoculation — kneeling on a needle, penetrating trauma, or iatrogenic (arthrocentesis, arthroscopy, joint injection).

The course of joint sepsis is predictable and catastrophic if untreated. The initial inflammatory response is a serous effusion with high polymorph count; within 24–48 hours a fibrin-rich exudate is secreted into the joint and begins to clot. Lysosomal enzymes released by the polymorphs begin to degrade the glycosaminoglycans of articular cartilage — the catastrophic moment in the pathogenesis. Experimental studies show that visible cartilage destruction occurs within 8 hours of onset of purulent infection in an experimental rabbit joint (Curtiss and Klein, 1965), and is practically irreversible after 4–5 days. Untreated, the joint capsule eventually ruptures — in the hip this produces lateral dislocation of the head, caused by swelling of the ligamentum teres. The knee tends to ankylose; the hip and shoulder, by contrast, retain full range of motion despite the destroyed epiphysis and shortened limb.

### Microbiological Spectrum by Age
<details id="microbiology">
<summary>Microbiological Spectrum by Age</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The microbiological spectrum of septic arthritis is distributed by age group, which determines empirical antibiotic cover. **Neonate (under 4 weeks):** *Staphylococcus aureus* remains the most common pathogen, but a smaller proportion of cases than in the older child; *Streptococcus agalactiae* (group B streptococcus) is the leading neonatal pathogen, vertically transmitted from the maternal genital tract — it accounts for about 21% of neonatal cases in the classical series of Jackson and Nelson (1982); *Escherichia coli* and other Gram-negative enteric organisms (about 14%) — particularly in neonates with omphalitis, urinary tract infection, or maternal chorioamnionitis; *Candida* species — in premature infants on broad-spectrum antibiotics or with central venous catheters; *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* — vertically transmitted from the maternal genital tract, with a characteristic presentation of polyarticular arthritis (bilateral knee or wrist involvement) and overlying skin pustules, a sentinel sign of unrecognised maternal infection.

**Child from 1 month to 5 years:** *Staphylococcus aureus* (including an increasing share of MRSA), historically followed by *Haemophilus influenzae* type B — the latter has essentially disappeared since the introduction of Hib vaccination in the late 1980s and early 1990s in developed countries. *Kingella kingae* has emerged as the leading pathogen in this age group over the past two decades — a Gram-negative coccobacillus that is fastidious in culture (requires enriched media and BACTEC bottles, classical cultures are often sterile) and for which PCR against its rtxA gene is the gold standard. Lundy and Kehl (1998) were among the first to describe the rising proportion of *Kingella kingae* in pediatric series.

**Child over 5 years:** *Staphylococcus aureus* dominates; *Streptococcus pyogenes* and *Streptococcus pneumoniae* are less common. **Sexually active adolescents:** *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* — typically with monoarticular (sometimes polyarticular) arthritis, associated tenosynovitis and skin lesions. Additional factors: sickle cell anaemia — *Salmonella* (a characteristic pathogen seeded into infarcted marrow); penetrating trauma through a sneaker — *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* (colonising the moist sneaker lining).

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Empirical cover in the neonate: ampicillin (for group B streptococcus and listeria) + third-generation cephalosporin or gentamicin (for Gram-negatives); vancomycin added if MRSA prevalence is high.</li>
    <li>Empirical cover in the older child: cefazolin or clindamycin if MRSA prevalence is low; vancomycin or linezolid if MRSA prevalence is high — modified by culture.</li>
    <li><em>Kingella kingae</em> is the leading pathogen from 6 months to 4 years; classical cultures are often sterile but synovial-fluid PCR is positive; third-generation cephalosporins are active.</li>
    <li>Sickle cell disease → <em>Salmonella</em>; penetrating trauma through a sneaker → <em>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</em>; vertical transmission → <em>Neisseria gonorrhoeae</em> and group B streptococcus.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Neonatal Form (Pseudoparalysis)
<details id="neonatal">
<summary>Neonatal Form (Pseudoparalysis)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical presentation of neonatal septic arthritis is deceptively subtle — Mercer Rang called it the "sleeper" form of pediatric joint sepsis. Systemic signs of sepsis — irritability, poor feeding, lethargy, temperature instability (hypothermia is more common than hyperthermia in the neonate), jaundice — may be the only initial clinical clues, without localising signs, and the child may be transferred from neonatology to orthopaedics only after several days of treatment for "neonatal sepsis" with no anatomical focus identified.

Local signs, when present, are subtle. **Pseudoparalysis** of the affected limb is the critical local sign: the child holds the limb still and resists passive motion, but does not cry on direct palpation of the diaphysis of the long bones. This is an important differentiating feature from true neurological paralysis (traumatic plexopathy, spinal trauma) — pseudoparalysis disappears with adequate analgesia, while true paralysis does not. A second key sign in the neonate is the *cri à la mobilisation* — crying when the limb is handled, typically at nappy change, which separates a joint pathology from a viral syndrome.

Inflammatory signs over the affected joint (warmth, swelling, erythema) may be subtle or absent because of the neonate's blunted inflammatory response to infection. The hip is held in the classical position of flexion-abduction-external rotation — the position that maximises capsular volume and reduces intra-articular pressure. The shoulder — flexion and adduction. The elbow — flexion and pronation. The knee — flexion. The ankle — equinovarus position.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <strong>Pitfall</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Neonatal septic arthritis hides behind nonspecific sepsis (temperature instability, irritability, poor feeding, lethargy, jaundice) — mandatory orthopaedic evaluation in every neonatal sepsis without a clear focus.</li>
    <li>Pseudoparalysis (the infant holds the limb still and resists passive motion but does not cry on direct diaphyseal palpation) is the critical local sign — distinguishes from true neurological paralysis and demands immediate joint evaluation.</li>
    <li><em>Cri à la mobilisation</em> — crying when the limb is handled during nappy change — is an early and often the only localising sign in young infants.</li>
    <li>The classical hip position (flexion-abduction-external rotation) maximises capsular volume and lowers intra-articular pressure; forced positioning into other postures during the examination causes sharp pain.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Infant and Early Childhood Form
<details id="infant">
<summary>Infant and Early Childhood Form</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In the older infant (3–24 months) and in the older child the presentation is closer to the typical pediatric septic arthritis — the clinical picture described by Smith (1874) and subsequently codified by Lloyd-Roberts. High fever (typically above 38.5 °C), refusal to bear weight or move the limb, fixed antalgic position, joint warmth and tenderness are constant features. The child looks systemically unwell (Staheli emphasises the child's "ill appearance" as the most reliable global clinical signal — lethargy, dehydration, reduced responsiveness to the environment).

Examination reveals joint swelling (subtle in the hip because of its depth, obvious in the knee or ankle), erythema, and warmth on palpation. Active motion is fully or almost fully restricted; passive motion produces sharp pain in all directions (in contrast to transient synovitis, in which only internal rotation is restricted). The "log roll" test — gentle axial rotation of the limb without flexion at the hip or knee — produces pain in septic arthritis of the hip but is usually tolerated by other causes of limb pain.

In multifocal involvement (about 20% of infant cases) systemic features may be surprisingly mild relative to the local picture, and several joints may be affected simultaneously, reflecting the common haematogenous origin of the infection.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The "log roll" test — axial rotation of the limb without flexion at the joints — is a gentle screen for hip joint pathology; painful in septic arthritis, rarely in other causes of limb pain.</li>
    <li>Systemic ill appearance (lethargy, dehydration, reduced responsiveness) is the most reliable global clinical signal in the child; the well-looking but limping patient is more likely to have transient synovitis or early Perthes disease.</li>
    <li>Referred pain from hip to knee is characteristic in the child; always examine the hip in a complaint of knee pain.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Investigations and Imaging
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="investigations">
<details>
<summary>Investigations and Imaging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Laboratory investigations parallel those for septic arthritis at any age. **Complete blood count** shows leukocytosis with neutrophilia in most cases but not all — a normal white cell count does not exclude septic arthritis. **CRP** rises rapidly (peaks at about 48 hours from onset) and falls equally rapidly with adequate treatment, making it the most useful serial marker of clinical response. A CRP value below 1 mg/dL has a high negative predictive value for septic arthritis. **ESR** rises more slowly (peaks at 3–5 days) and falls more slowly (remains elevated 2–3 weeks after clinical resolution) — useful for monitoring the overall response, but less responsive in the acute phase. **Blood culture** is positive in 30–50% of cases and should be obtained in every case before starting antibiotic treatment.

**Aspiration of the affected joint** is the definitive diagnostic test. When neonatal hip involvement is suspected, aspiration is performed under ultrasound guidance with appropriate sedation. Synovial fluid is sent for: cell count and differential (septic arthritis: typically over 50,000 WBC/mm³, often over 100,000, with over 75% polymorphs; transient synovitis: typically under 25,000/mm³ with a mixed pattern), Gram stain, aerobic and anaerobic culture, PCR for *Kingella kingae* (where available), and glucose (decreased in septic arthritis). Septic synovial fluid is often thick and fibrinous — Wenger described "cast-like" sediments that cannot pass through the needle, and emphasised that repeated aspiration is a poor method of treatment compared with open arthrotomy.

**Plain radiographs** are often normal in the early phase. A reduction in bone density of about 30% or more is required before radiographic changes appear, which typically takes 10–14 days. Early possible findings: soft-tissue swelling, obliteration of normal fat planes around the joint, lateralisation or subluxation of the proximal femur with a widened joint space (the classical "Tom Smith arthritis" sign of a high-riding, laterally displaced proximal femur), adjacent metaphyseal bony changes in concurrent osteomyelitis. **Ultrasound** is the imaging modality of choice for early detection of hip joint effusion, especially in the neonate — portable, radiation-free, fast and accessible. Bilateral ultrasound for comparison improves sensitivity. Beyond the effusion, ultrasound can guide aspiration in real time.

**MRI** is the most sensitive imaging study for early diagnosis and identification of associated osteomyelitis, soft-tissue abscess, or pyomyositis. In the neonate it requires general anaesthesia and can be logistically hard to obtain urgently. **Technetium-99m bone scintigraphy** shows increased uptake ("warm" or "hot" scan) in septic arthritis — particularly useful when multifocal osteoarthritis is suspected, since it identifies multiple foci with a single study. Aspiration does not disturb the scintigraphic finding.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Ultrasound is the imaging modality of choice for the neonatal hip: portable, radiation-free, reliably demonstrates the effusion and guides aspiration in real time.</li>
    <li>Plain radiographs may be normal for up to 10–14 days from onset; early signs include lateralisation/subluxation of the proximal femur (Tom Smith sign) and soft-tissue swelling.</li>
    <li>CRP is more responsive than ESR in the acute phase: peaks at 48 hours, falls rapidly with adequate treatment; a value below 1 mg/dL has a high negative predictive value for septic arthritis.</li>
    <li>Blood culture is positive in 30–50%; mandatory before starting antibiotic treatment, even in suspected neonatal sepsis without a localising sign.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Differential Diagnosis and the Kocher Criteria
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="kocher">
<details>
<summary>Differential Diagnosis and the Kocher Criteria</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The pivotal diagnostic challenge in acute non-traumatic hip pain in the 3–10-year-old child is to distinguish transient synovitis from septic arthritis of the hip. The former is a self-limiting inflammatory process that resolves in 7–10 days with rest and NSAIDs; the latter is a surgical emergency that destroys the joint within hours. The **Kocher criteria**, derived from a study by Kocher, Zurakowski, and Kasser (Boston Children's Hospital, 1999) of 168 children with acute non-traumatic hip pain, identified four independent predictors of true septic arthritis:

1. **Non-weight-bearing** on the affected limb
2. **Fever** greater than 38.5 °C
3. **ESR** greater than 40 mm/h
4. **White cell count** greater than 12,000/mm³

The predictive probability of septic arthritis according to the number of criteria met in the original study was approximately: 0.2% with zero, 3% with one, 40% with two, 93% with three, and 99.6% with four. This predictive value is exceptional for any clinical instrument and explains the broad use of the tool. The validation study by Kocher, Mandiga and colleagues (2004) confirmed the criteria but with more conservative predictive values (40% at two, 73% at three, 93% at four criteria), particularly at a lower baseline prevalence of septic arthritis in the cohort.

**Modifications.** The Caird and colleagues (2006) modification adds CRP greater than 2 mg/dL as a fifth criterion — by itself it is a particularly powerful predictor. Luhmann and colleagues (2004) proposed an alternative algorithm with only three criteria (non-weight-bearing, fever, ESR), validated in St Louis, and emphasised that the prevalence of septic arthritis at different institutions affects predictive value. Jung and colleagues (2003) and Sultan and Hughes (2010) include CRP as the most powerful single predictor; a CRP value below 1 mg/dL has a negative predictive value over 87% for septic arthritis and often allows aspiration to be deferred at lower clinical suspicion.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Kocher criteria (1999): non-weight-bearing, fever > 38.5 °C, ESR > 40 mm/h, WBC > 12,000/mm³ — original predictive probabilities for septic arthritis: 3%, 40%, 93%, 99.6% for one to four criteria met.</li>
    <li>Validation study (Kocher et al., 2004) gave more conservative values (40%, 73%, 93% at two, three, four criteria); local prevalence of septic arthritis affects these figures.</li>

  </ul>
</aside>

When the diagnosis is uncertain — particularly when two or three Kocher criteria are met — joint aspiration is the definitive diagnostic test. Synovial fluid examination demonstrates the differentiation: in septic arthritis the white cell count is typically over 50,000/mm³ (often over 100,000) with over 75% polymorphs, glucose is decreased, and Gram stain or culture identifies the organism; in transient synovitis the cell count is typically under 25,000/mm³ with a more mixed pattern, and culture is sterile.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Never defer aspiration on the grounds of absent fever — atypical presentation (afebrile septic arthritis, particularly in the immunocompromised patient or after partial antibiotic treatment) is a real clinical pitfall.</li>
    <li>Antibiotics given before aspiration may render culture sterile without substantially changing the cell count — clinical decisions must rest on the overall picture, not on a sterile culture alone.</li>
    <li>In children under 3 years of age the Kocher criteria are not validated — the diagnosis must rest on clinical examination, ultrasound, and aspiration with a low threshold.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Transient (Toxic) Synovitis of the Hip
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="synovitis">
<details>
<summary>Transient (Toxic) Synovitis of the Hip</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-5/transient-synovitis-us.jpg" data-figure-alt="Ultrasound of joint effusion in transient synovitis" aria-label="Enlarge">

</figure>

**Definition and epidemiology.** Transient synovitis (synonyms: toxic synovitis, "irritable hip", *observation hip*) is a self-limiting inflammatory synovitis of the hip joint in children, characterised by acute onset of hip pain, antalgic gait, and joint effusion in the absence of true infection. It is the most common cause of acute hip pain in children aged 3–10 years, with a peak incidence at 5–6 years and a male predominance of about 2:1. Annual incidence is approximately 0.2% in children, and lifetime cumulative incidence around 3%.

**Aetiology.** Not definitively established. The most enduring hypothesis is that it represents a post-viral or immune-mediated synovitis — about half of cases have a history of preceding (1–2 weeks earlier) upper respiratory infection, gastroenteritis or other viral illness. Alternative theories: allergic synovial reaction, microtrauma, serum-sickness-like reaction. The classical Lovell and Winter series notes that synovial biopsy is non-specific — a mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate without etiologically distinctive findings.

**Clinical features.** Acute onset of unilateral hip or referred groin or knee pain, antalgic gait or refusal to bear weight, and mild restriction of hip motion, most consistently of internal rotation (Staheli's test for hip pathology). The child is generally well-appearing, afebrile or with low-grade fever (typically less than 38.5 °C), and without the systemic features of acute infection. Examination reveals tenderness over the anterior hip joint and restriction of range of motion at the extremes, with the limb often held in mild flexion and external rotation. In contrast to septic arthritis, the child can usually tolerate some hip motion and some weight-bearing, and the inflammatory signs are mild.

**Imaging.** Plain radiographs are typically normal, although they may show capsular distension or (in cases evolving over days) slight lateral displacement of the proximal femur from the pelvis as a sign of joint distension. Ultrasound is the imaging modality of choice and reliably demonstrates the hip joint effusion that is the cardinal feature; bilateral ultrasound for comparison is useful in equivocal cases, and ultrasound-guided aspiration of the affected joint can be performed in the same sitting when needed. MRI is rarely required but can be helpful when other diagnoses (particularly early Perthes disease) are considered (with characteristic hypointense epiphyseal signal on T1).

**Treatment and natural history.** Treatment is supportive: rest, restricted weight-bearing, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory analgesics (typically ibuprofen 10 mg/kg three times daily), and reassurance. The natural history is one of spontaneous resolution over 7–10 days. Recurrence in the same hip or the opposite hip occurs in approximately 5–15% of cases. Aspiration is not a therapeutic necessity in pure transient synovitis but can offer rapid pain relief (through capsular decompression) and simultaneously rule out septic arthritis with a definitive negative culture.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>

    <li>The internal rotation test is the most sensitive single sign for hip pathology in the child; asymmetric restriction of internal rotation mandates further evaluation.</li>
    <li>Joint aspiration in pure transient synovitis is not a therapeutic necessity, but can offer rapid relief through capsular decompression.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <ul>

    <li>Transient synovitis below 3 years of age is unusual — a lower threshold for septic arthritis and for neonatal infectious pathogens mandates joint aspiration.</li>
    <li>The classical "inverted" clinical pitfall: thrice-recurring "transient synovitis" turns out to be early Perthes disease — mandatory follow-up pelvic radiographs at 6 weeks.</li>

## Differential Diagnosis of the Limping Child
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="ddx-limping">
<details>
<summary>Differential Diagnosis of the Limping Child</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The differential diagnosis of acute hip or limb pain in the child is wide and varies substantially with age. The classical age-based differential, presented in Staheli and Wenger-Rang as the foundation of pediatric orthopaedic evaluation, is summarised below.

**Birth to 4 years.** Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) — missed neonatally or untreated; septic arthritis or osteomyelitis of the hip or femur (with a high index of suspicion because of the low systemic manifestations); transient synovitis (uncommon below 3 years); toddler's fracture (a spiral oblique fracture of the tibial diaphysis in the first walking year, typically from unwitnessed low-energy trauma); non-accidental injury (the orthopaedic surgeon must always consider this possibility, particularly with metaphyseal corner fractures, spiral fractures of long bones in a non-walking infant, and fractures inconsistent with the offered history); the neuromuscular disorders that present in infancy with limp (cerebral palsy, spinal muscular atrophy, hereditary myopathies); and discitis — typically at L4–L5 with a history of poor feeding and marked spinal stiffness on flexion.

**4 to 10 years.** Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease (a chronic ischaemic disorder of the femoral head, presenting with antalgic gait and groin or knee pain, typically in boys aged 4–8); septic arthritis or osteomyelitis; transient synovitis (peak range); juvenile idiopathic arthritis (monoarticular form); rheumatic fever (migratory polyarticular arthritis); reactive arthritis (post-streptococcal, post-dysenteric); lower-extremity overuse syndromes (typically sports-related); tarsal coalitions (typically with symptom peak at 9–11 years when the cartilaginous coalition ossifies); discoid meniscus; bone tumours (osteoid osteoma — night pain relieved by NSAIDs; eosinophilic granuloma — monostotic lymphadenitis-like lytic defect; Ewing sarcoma — diaphyseal with onion-skin periosteal reaction); leukaemia, in which bone or joint pain may be the presenting feature in 20–30% of childhood cases (characteristic — night pain, often non-progressive with mild to moderate systemic symptoms).

**10 to 15 years.** Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE — a critical diagnosis that must not be missed, since delayed presentation produces avascular necrosis and chondrolysis; typically in boys with higher BMI or endocrine conditions — hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency); juvenile idiopathic arthritis; sports-related overuse syndromes (Osgood-Schlatter at the tibial tubercle, Sinding-Larsen-Johansson at the inferior patellar pole, Sever's at the calcaneal apophysis, jumper's knee at the proximal patellar pole); reactive arthritis; bone tumours (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma); leukaemia. Spondylolisthesis, spondylolysis, juvenile discitis — typically with lower-back or lumbar pain.

This age-based stratification is the practical foundation of every consultation in pediatric orthopaedics. Every child with a limp or limb pain should be assessed in the context of the diagnoses likely at that age, with particular attention to "red flags" — night pain (tumour), systemic symptoms (infection, leukaemia, JIA), ill general appearance (sepsis, leukaemia) and a family history of bleeding or joint disorders.

    <li>Referred pain from hip to knee is characteristic in the child; always examine the hip in a complaint of knee pain.</li>

## Treatment
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The treatment of confirmed or strongly suspected neonatal or infantile septic arthritis is urgent surgical drainage combined with broad-spectrum parenteral antibiotic therapy. Aspiration alone is generally insufficient for the deep joints (hip, shoulder) in the infant because of the thick adherent synovium of the immature joint and the difficulty of complete evacuation — the fibrin-rich exudate clots within the joint to form a "cast" that cannot pass through the needle (Wenger 1993). Open arthrotomy or arthroscopic drainage is standard.


### Antibiotic Therapy
<details id="antibiotics">
<summary>Antibiotic Therapy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Empirical cover in the neonate (under 4 weeks).** The standard is a combination of ampicillin (200 mg/kg/day, divided in four doses) for group B streptococcus and listeria, plus a third-generation cephalosporin (cefotaxime 150 mg/kg/day, divided in three doses) or an aminoglycoside (gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg/day, divided in three doses, with serum-level monitoring) for Gram-negative enteric organisms. Vancomycin (60 mg/kg/day, divided in four doses) is added in regions with high MRSA prevalence or in central-catheter-associated infections. Activity against *Candida* (amphotericin B or fluconazole) is added only with proven or strongly suspected fungal infection (premature infants with prolonged NICU stays, broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, or a positive culture).

**Empirical cover in the older infant (>3 months) and child.** Cefazolin (100 mg/kg/day, divided in three doses) or flucloxacillin in regions of low MRSA prevalence; clindamycin (40 mg/kg/day, divided in four doses) or vancomycin (60 mg/kg/day, divided in four doses) in regions of high MRSA prevalence. Cefuroxime or ceftriaxone when *Kingella kingae* is suspected.

**Dose and duration.** The classical recommendation was parenteral therapy for 4–6 weeks. Modern evidence (Peltola et al. 1997, Jaberi et al. 2002, Kim et al. 2000) supports a shortened parenteral course of 5–10 days (until clinical improvement, CRP normalisation, and tolerance of oral intake), followed by oral therapy for a total duration of 3–4 weeks in septic arthritis and 4–6 weeks in concurrent osteomyelitis. Transition to oral therapy requires clinical improvement, CRP reduction to below half of the peak value, tolerance of oral intake, and a reliable family context. Serial CRP monitoring is a more responsive indicator than ESR for clinical response.

**Adjunctive dexamethasone.** Odio and colleagues (2003), in a double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial, showed that short adjunctive dexamethasone in pediatric haematogenous septic arthritis reduces cartilage destruction and the duration of symptoms. The concept is analogous to adjunctive use in bacterial meningitis — suppression of cytokine storm and the inflammatory enzymes released from polymorphs. The use remains a matter of discussion; when given, the dose is 0.15 mg/kg every 6 hours for a total of 4 days, started simultaneously with the first dose of antibiotic.

    <li>Empirical cover in the older child: cefazolin (or clindamycin/vancomycin in high-MRSA regions) — modified by culture results.</li>
    <li>Shortened parenteral therapy (5–10 days) with transition to oral on documented clinical and laboratory response; total duration 3–4 weeks in isolated septic arthritis, 4–6 weeks in concurrent osteomyelitis.</li>

### Anterior Approach for Hip Drainage
<details id="drainage-anterior">
<summary>Anterior Approach for Hip Drainage</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The anterior Smith-Petersen approach (1917) through the internervous plane between the sartorius (femoral nerve) and tensor fasciae latae (superior gluteal nerve) is the standard approach for drainage of pediatric septic hip arthritis. It provides wide exposure of the joint capsule, safe distance from the medial femoral circumflex artery (the main blood supply to the femoral head), and direct visualisation of the joint cavity with full evacuation of pus.

**Positioning.** The patient is supine with a small bump under the ipsilateral buttock for slight elevation of the hip. The affected limb is freely draped to allow intra-operative manipulation. The classical position allows easy access to the anterior joint capsule and controlled examination of the joint volume by rotating the limb.

**Skin incision.** The classical Smith-Petersen incision begins 1 cm distal to the middle of the iliac crest, runs along the crest down to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), and then curves distally and medially along the line of the sartorius for about 8–10 cm. In the child the incision is shorter and often only the distal portion is required — a brief "mini-Smith-Petersen" of 4–6 cm beginning 1–2 cm distal and lateral to the ASIS.

**Dissection.** The subcutaneous tissue is divided to the level of the fascia. The **internervous plane** between the sartorius (medially, innervated by the femoral nerve) and the tensor fasciae latae (laterally, innervated by the superior gluteal nerve) is developed by blunt dissection. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve passes 2–3 cm medial and distal to the ASIS — mandatory caution and gentle retraction. Deeper, **between the rectus femoris** (medially, retracted medially or detached from its origin at the ASIS) **and the glutei** (laterally), the anterior joint capsule is reached. The iliopsoas passes medially and craniocaudally and can be retracted medially; in infants and small children it does not obstruct the approach.

**Capsulotomy.** The anterior joint capsule is opened with a transverse or T-shaped incision. Pus is evacuated; material is sent for Gram stain, culture (aerobic, anaerobic, PCR for *Kingella*), and histology. The joint is irrigated copiously with warm normal saline. The capsule is left open or closed loosely over a drain (a soft silicone drain with passive drainage or active suction drainage, left in place for 24–72 hours according to output). The skin is closed with absorbable sutures. Immobilisation: spica cast or soft traction apparatus for 7–10 days depending on age and need for positional stability.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Smith-Petersen internervous plane: sartorius (femoral nerve) — tensor fasciae latae (superior gluteal nerve); deeper between the rectus femoris and the glutei.</li>
    <li>The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve passes 2–3 cm medial and distal to the ASIS — mandatory caution and gentle retraction to avoid postoperative meralgia (anaesthesia on the anterolateral thigh).</li>
    <li>The anterior approach is safe with respect to the medial femoral circumflex artery (the main blood supply to the femoral head, which runs posterior to the neck).</li>
    <li>The capsule is left open or closed loosely over a drain — the goal is continued decompression and the option of repeat evacuation of exudate.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Posterior (Ober) Approach for Hip Drainage
<details id="drainage-posterior">
<summary>Posterior (Ober) Approach for Hip Drainage</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The posterior Ober approach (adapted from Kocher-Langenbeck) is an alternative in very young infants or when posterior drainage is preferred. It gives a more direct exposure of the posteroinferior quadrant of the joint capsule, where pus tends to accumulate in the limb's typical position of flexion-abduction-external rotation.

**Positioning.** The patient is in the lateral decubitus position with the affected hip up. The classical Kocher-Langenbeck incision begins at the posterior superior iliac spine, runs along the gluteus maximus toward the tip of the greater trochanter, and then distally along the line of the femur. Dissection splits the gluteus maximus along its fibres and exposes the posterior short external rotators — piriformis, gemelli, obturator internus, quadratus femoris.

**Danger.** The posterior approach exposes the medial femoral circumflex artery (which runs deep behind the femoral neck between the obturator externus and the quadratus femoris), and technical injury to this artery has catastrophic potential — iatrogenic avascular necrosis of the head. The posterior approach is therefore preferred only in experienced hands, when there is a clear anatomical indication (posteroinferior abscess inaccessible from the front), and with full preservation of the quadratus femoris and its vascular bed.

**Bulgarian school.** Boychev and his successors (Boev, Holevich) in the tradition of the Boychev orthopaedic hospital in Gorna Banya recommend an **anterolateral Watson-Jones-Boychev approach** for cases that are not purely capsular but have concurrent metaphyseal osteomyelitis — the incision allows both capsular decompression and matching metaphyseal drainage in one field. This classical adaptation remains part of the Bulgarian orthopaedic curriculum for neonatal hip sepsis.

### Arthroscopic Drainage
<details id="arthroscopy">
<summary>Arthroscopic Drainage</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Arthroscopic drainage of the hip in pediatric septic arthritis is an alternative to open arthrotomy in the older child and adolescent (Kalan, El-Hawary and colleagues 2008; Sankar and colleagues 2009). Standard portals — anterolateral and anterior — allow complete visualisation of the central and peripheral compartments, copious lavage and evacuation of pus, synovial debridement, and placement of a drain. The arthroscopic approach is substantially less invasive and allows earlier mobilisation, but requires specific equipment, an experienced arthroscopist, and pediatric-appropriate instrumentation (4 mm arthroscope or 2.7 mm pediatric arthroscope).

**Limitations in the neonatal infant.** In the neonate and young infant (under 6 months) arthroscopic drainage is technically challenging because of the small joint (joint volume under 5 mL) and problems with the traction position. In this age group open Smith-Petersen arthrotomy remains preferred. After 12–18 months the arthroscopic option is an acceptable alternative in experienced hands.

**Arthroscopy in the knee and shoulder.** Arthroscopic drainage of the knee in septic arthritis is the standard treatment since the late 1980s — easy access, complete visualisation, effective lavage and debridement. The shoulder is accessible arthroscopically, although in deep subacromial abscesses open drainage may be required. The ankle is also accessible arthroscopically.

    <li>Knee septic arthritis — arthroscopic drainage has been the standard since the 1980s; shoulder and ankle are also accessible arthroscopically in experienced hands.</li>

### Drainage of Other Joints
<details id="other-joints-drainage">
<summary>Drainage of Other Joints</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Knee.** Classical medial parapatellar arthrotomy for open drainage; in modern practice arthroscopic drainage is the standard for the older child. Needle aspiration is an acceptable option in an early case with a low-virulence pathogen and adequate antibiotic therapy — Herndon and colleagues (1986) reported 34 of 49 non-hip joints successfully treated with repeated aspiration and antibiotics; the remainder required drainage for lack of response.

**Shoulder.** Deltopectoral interval, entering between the deltoid and the pectoralis major along the plane of the cephalic vein. Coracobrachialis and biceps brachii are retracted medially; the subscapularis is divided or retracted to expose the anterior joint capsule.

**Elbow.** Classical posteromedial incision over the medial epicondyle or a combined medial and lateral incision for full evacuation — the ulnar nerve is at risk in the medial approach and must be identified and protected.

**Ankle.** Anterolateral incision between the tibialis anterior and the extensor digitorum longus, or anteromedial incision between the tibialis anterior and the extensor hallucis longus, depending on the site of maximum effusion.

## Outcomes and Complications — Tom Smith Arthritis
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Complications — Tom Smith Arthritis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The complications of neonatal and infantile septic arthritis are among the most devastating in pediatric orthopaedics and are the basis of the principle of urgent drainage. The classical late sequelae of inadequately treated neonatal hip sepsis are: avascular necrosis of the femoral head, with secondary collapse, deformity, premature physeal arrest, and lifelong shortening and arthritis; chronic dislocation or subluxation; chondrolysis with severe stiffness; and the residual geometric deformities coxa magna, coxa breva, coxa vara, or a combination of these. Premature physeal arrest in any affected long bone leads to shortening and angular deformity.

**Tom Smith arthritis.** Sir Thomas Smith (1833–1909), a surgeon at St Bartholomew's Hospital in London, described in 1874 in *St Bartholomew's Hospital Reports* the acute arthritis of infants with its typical features: sudden onset, rapid progression, high mortality, and devastating consequences for those who survived. Smith wrote: "It occurs, as far as my experience extends, within the first year of life, and is characterized by the suddenness of its onset and the rapidity of its progress and termination, whether the latter be of a fatal or a favourable kind. It is very dangerous to life, and intensely destructive to the articular ends of the bones, which, of course, at this period of life are largely cartilaginous. Lastly, I would mention as a feature of the disease, that it rarely produces ankylosis, but leaves a child with a limb shortened, by loss of part of the end of some bone, and with a weakened flail-like joint." Smith worked in the outpatient clinic at St Bartholomew's, where he also performed autopsies on neonates who had died of infantile arthritis — the anatomical basis that allowed him to understand the course of the disease.

Classification of outcome. **Hunka et al. (1982)** and **Forlin et al. (1992)** proposed classifications of the residual deformities after neonatal hip sepsis, based on radiographic morphology of the head and neck: type I — a normal head with minimal neck deformity; type II — the head is present, but the neck is short with coxa magna; type III — partial resorption of the head; type IV — complete loss of head and neck ("flail hip"); type V — fused head and neck remnants with the acetabulum in a non-articulated position. Choi and colleagues (1990) reported a series of reconstructive procedures for the destroyed pediatric hip.

**Late reconstruction.** The options are demanding and often staged. **Pelvic support osteotomy (Ilizarov, Schanz)** creates a new support point laterally and proximally on the residual head, engaging the ilium with a high valgus osteotomy — it compensates for shortening and provides gait stability, but does not restore joint anatomy. **Valgus or varus femoral osteotomy** — according to the morphology of the residuum and the need for centring in the acetabulum. **Arthrodesis** in a suitable position (flexion 25–30°, neutral rotation, 5° adduction) — reserved for a severely damaged unilateral case at skeletal maturity; great functional cost. **Arthroplasty** — reserved for adult age, with a high risk of revision because of the long expected life span.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <strong>Pitfall</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Tom Smith arthritis (1874) is the late consequence of unrecognised neonatal hip sepsis: avascular necrosis with collapse, chronic dislocation or resorption of the head, premature physeal arrest, coxa magna/breva/vara, limb shortening and a flail-like joint.</li>
    <li>Outcome is almost entirely set by the speed of initial recognition and drainage — the window is hours, not days.</li>
    <li>The Hunka/Forlin classification of residual deformities stratifies reconstructive options — from valgus osteotomy (type I–II) through Ilizarov pelvic support osteotomy (type III–IV) to arthrodesis/arthroplasty at skeletal maturity (type V).</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Long-term outcomes for timely treated neonatal septic arthritis are encouraging. Series with drainage in the first 4 days of symptom onset show preserved normal joint function in over 80% of cases at long-term follow-up (Choi 1990, Bos 1998 for the shoulder). Delay of one week or more gives low to very low chances of normal functional recovery. This time window — measured in hours for the neonate and a few days for the older child — is the central clinical principle around which the entire concept of pediatric joint sepsis is organised.

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Local Contributions

<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school; the eponymous "Prof. Boycho Boychev" Hospital for Orthopaedics and Traumatology in Gorna Banya. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a reference in the Bulgarian curriculum and contains detailed descriptions of the classical operative approaches for drainage of pediatric joint infections, including the modified anterolateral Watson-Jones approach for combined capsular and metaphyseal drainage in neonatal hip sepsis with concurrent osteomyelitis — a core operative tactic in the Bulgarian tradition.

**Boev and Holevich <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Followers in the classical Boychev orthopaedic school; their teaching emphasises early recognition of the "sleeper" clinical presentation of neonatal septic arthritis — irritability, poor feeding, temperature instability in a neonate in the NICU — as the basis for early orthopaedic evaluation and aspiration. The Bulgarian residency training standard emphasises the urgency of joint aspiration in any unlocalised neonatal sepsis, a philosophy that mirrors Sir Thomas Smith's classical warning.

**Bulgarian pediatric orthopaedic tradition <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** Empirical antibiotic cover for suspected neonatal osteoarthritis in Bulgarian practice combines penicillin or ampicillin (for group B streptococcus and listeria) + a third-generation cephalosporin or aminoglycoside (for Gram-negative enteric organisms) + vancomycin or clindamycin in high-MRSA regions — a modern standard based on international pediatric protocols (AAP Red Book) and adapted to the local epidemiological picture.

From the classical Russian and Ukrainian school, embedded in the Bulgarian curriculum:

**G. A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Pelvic support osteotomy for reconstruction of the destroyed pediatric hip after neonatal sepsis — a double osteotomy of the proximal femur (high valgus osteotomy at the level of the greater trochanter + a lower varising osteotomy at the diaphyseal level) with the Ilizarov external fixator to correct shortening and centre the limb. The classical procedure remains valuable in type III–IV residual deformities (Hunka/Forlin) in older children before skeletal maturity.

**Classical Russian orthopaedic school <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** Recommendation of a posterolateral approach in deep posteroinferior hip abscess after a failed anterior drainage; concept of staged reconstruction of the destroyed hip by Ilizarov with explicit avoidance of arthrodesis in childhood in favour of preserving residual mobility.

## Classical Sources
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">
<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical line — pediatric joint sepsis</strong>
  <p>The concept of "infantile arthritis" as a distinct clinical entity is a product of the late nineteenth century. The classical textbook source is Sir Thomas Smith's paper "On the acute arthritis of infants" (St Bartholomew's Hospital Reports, 1874), describing the sudden onset, rapid progression and devastating consequences of the disease — the basis for the term "Tom Smith arthritis" denoting the late residual deformities.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Smith 1874</strong> — <em>On the acute arthritis of infants</em>, St Bart's Hospital Reports — the first classical description of infantile septic arthritis and its sequelae.</li>
    <li><strong>Hoffa 1905</strong> — <em>Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie</em> — consolidates the late-nineteenth-century European experience with juvenile hip pathology, including the differentiation of septic arthritis from coxitis tuberculosa and what later became transient synovitis (<a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderortho00hoff">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Billroth 1882</strong> — <em>Allgemeine chirurgische Pathologie und Therapie</em> — joint infections (<a href="https://archive.org/details/dieallgemeinechi1882bill">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Smith-Petersen 1917</strong> — anterior approach to the hip; the standard for drainage of pediatric septic hip arthritis.</li>
    <li><strong>Lloyd-Roberts 1960</strong> — modern systematisation of neonatal hip sepsis and its complications.</li>
    <li><strong>Curtiss & Klein 1965</strong> — experimental data on cartilage destruction within 8 hours of onset of the purulent process — pathophysiological basis for the time window.</li>
    <li><strong>Jackson & Nelson 1982</strong> — classical epidemiological series of pediatric septic arthritis by age and pathogen.</li>
    <li><strong>Kocher, Zurakowski, Kasser 1999</strong> — the four criteria for differentiating septic arthritis from transient synovitis of the hip.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Additional classical descriptions: **Ober** (posterior approach to the hip); **Watson-Jones 1936** (anterolateral approach to the hip, the basis for the Bulgarian modification); **Salter Robert B.** *Textbook of Disorders and Injuries of the Musculoskeletal System* — standard pediatric orthopaedic teaching with the classical principles of pediatric joint sepsis. **Tachdjian's Pediatric Orthopaedics** (Herring, 6th edition 2020) — the modern reference text with a detailed section on neonatal septic arthritis and the criteria for drainage. **Lovell and Winter's Pediatric Orthopaedics** (Flynn, Weinstein, 8th edition 2020) — a detailed description of the pathogenesis of pediatric joint sepsis and concurrent osteomyelitis. **Wenger & Rang** *The Art and Practice of Children's Orthopaedics* (1993) — a classical clinically oriented teaching text with a historical emphasis on Sir Thomas Smith and the philosophy of early recognition. **Staheli** *Fundamentals of Pediatric Orthopedics* (3rd edition 2016) — outpatient evaluation of the limping child and algorithmic approach to the differential diagnosis.
