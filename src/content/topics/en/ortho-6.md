## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The congenital deficiencies of the lower limb constitute one of the most complex areas of paediatric orthopaedics. They range from minor isolated reductions of a single segment to extensive multisegmental absence requiring lifelong prosthetic or surgical management. The principal entities covered in this chapter are: proximal focal femoral deficiency (PFFD — increasingly called *congenital femoral deficiency*, CFD); fibular hemimelia (postaxial longitudinal deficiency of the fibula), the commonest long-bone deficiency in children; tibial hemimelia (preaxial longitudinal deficiency of the tibia); and congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia. Each is associated with a characteristic set of foot, ankle, knee, and pelvic anomalies, and each demands an individualised management plan that integrates family counselling, careful staging of surgery, prosthetic provision where indicated, and the management of associated upper-limb and visceral anomalies.

The classifications used here follow Aitken (PFFD, 1969) and Paley's modern functional framework (1998), Achterman and Kalamchi (fibular hemimelia, 1979), Jones (tibial hemimelia, 1978), and Crawford (congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia, 1986), as elaborated in Tachdjian's Pediatric Orthopaedics (Herring), Paley's *Principles of Deformity Correction* (Springer 2002), Lovell and Winter's Pediatric Orthopaedics, and Ilizarov's *Transosseous Osteosynthesis* (Springer 1992) — the biological foundation of distraction histogenesis that makes contemporary limb-lengthening reconstruction possible at all.

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Embryology and Classifications
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="embryo-classif">
<details>
<summary>Embryology and Classifications</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Lower-Limb Embryology
<details id="embryo">
<summary>Lower-Limb Embryology</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The lower-limb bud appears at about day 28 of gestation as a lateral protrusion of the lateral plate mesoderm at the level of segments L2–S2. By the 8th week the limb has passed from a mesenchymal to a chondrogenic phase with full outlines of the future femur, tibia, fibula, and tarsal bones. The primary ossification centres of the femur, tibia, and fibula appear at about 7–8 weeks; the acetabulum develops from the triradiate cartilage between ilium, ischium, and pubis, with final fusion at about 15–17 years.

Interruption of early lower-limb development between the 4th and 6th week — by teratogenic, vascular, or genetic cause — produces the characteristic congenital deficiencies. The classical example is the thalidomide epidemic of the 1960s, which produced phocomelia and a characteristic pattern of PFFD. The great majority of sporadic contemporary cases, however, occur without identifiable exposure and without an obvious genetic cause; tibial hemimelia is the notable exception, since it is often familial with autosomal dominant inheritance.

</div>
</details>


### Classification Systems
<details id="classif-overview">
<summary>Classification Systems</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Classifications of congenital lower-limb deficiencies have accumulated over three generations of authors. Aitken (1969) proposed a descriptive anatomical classification of PFFD into four classes (A–D) based on the presence of the femoral head and acetabulum. Pappas (1983) extended Aitken's framework into nine classes for finer description of the milder forms. **Paley** (1998) reformulated the classification on a functional basis — the potential for hip stability and reconstruction — which today is the standard in specialised limb-reconstruction centres. For fibular hemimelia, Achterman and Kalamchi (1979) proposed a three-type classification (IA, IB, II); for tibial hemimelia, Jones (1978) with four types; for congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia, Crawford (1986) with four types, replacing the older Boyd scheme.

<aside class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>Note</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Aitken (1969) — descriptive anatomical classification of PFFD; classes A–D by presence of femoral head and acetabulum.</li>
    <li>Paley (1998) — functional framework for PFFD, emphasising the reconstructive potential; standard in contemporary limb-lengthening and reconstruction centres.</li>
    <li>Achterman-Kalamchi (1979) — fibular hemimelia types IA, IB, II.</li>
    <li>Jones (1978) — tibial hemimelia, types I–IV.</li>
    <li>Crawford (1986) — congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia, types I–IV.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Epidemiology and Associated Anomalies
<details id="pffd-epi">
<summary>Epidemiology and Associated Anomalies</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Proximal focal femoral deficiency — renamed by Paley as *congenital femoral deficiency* (CFD) — is a sporadic congenital disorder with a spectrum of severity ranging from mild femoral shortening with a partially formed hip to complete absence of the proximal femur, the femoral head, and the acetabulum. The incidence is approximately 1 in 50,000 live births. The condition is unilateral in about 90% of cases and bilateral in 10%. The aetiology in the great majority of patients remains unknown; maternal exposure to thalidomide in the 1960s produced a characteristic teratogenic pattern of PFFD with phocomelia and confirmed the potential for an environmentally induced disorder, but does not explain sporadic contemporary cases.

Associated anomalies are the rule, not the exception: fibular hemimelia coexists in **60–80%** of PFFD cases, contralateral hip dysplasia or *coxa vara*, absent or short cruciate ligaments of the knee, and hypoplastic proximal tibial and femoral epiphyses. The length of the affected femur in a classically severe case approximates the length of the contralateral foot at the level of the contralateral knee.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>PFFD coexists with fibular hemimelia in 60–80% of cases; mandatory full assessment of the entire limb, not just the femur.</li>
    <li>Absence or hypoplasia of the anterior cruciate ligament is a common associated defect that becomes clinically important during lengthening and weight-bearing later in development.</li>
    <li>The contralateral hip is often dysplastic — routine ultrasound or radiography in infancy.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Aitken Classification
<details id="pffd-aitken">
<summary>Aitken Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Aitken classification (1969) — the historical standard — divides PFFD into four classes based on the presence and morphology of the femoral head and acetabulum.

Class A: the femoral head is present in a developed acetabulum; there is a varus deformity of the femoral neck or a *pseudarthrosis* at the subtrochanteric level; the femoral shaft is short but present.

Class B: the femoral head is present in a developed acetabulum, but there is no bony connection between the femoral head and the femoral shaft; the trochanteric region is connected to the shaft.

Class C: the femoral head is absent or rudimentary and the acetabulum is severely dysplastic; the femoral shaft is severely shortened.

Class D: both the femoral head and the acetabulum are absent; the proximal end of the femur tapers to a point.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Aitken A/B — preserved femoral head in an acetabulum — candidates for hip reconstruction.</li>
    <li>Aitken B — head present but no bony connection to the shaft; reconstruction requires arthrodesis of the pseudarthrosis (e.g. Sofield-type osteotomy).</li>
    <li>Aitken C/D — reconstruction of a stable hip is not feasible; the patient depends on a prosthesis or on Van Nes rotationplasty.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

The Pappas classification (1983) expanded Aitken's framework into nine classes for finer description of the milder forms of femoral hypoplasia, but in contemporary practice is less used than the Paley classification.

</div>
</details>


### Paley Classification
<details id="pffd-paley">
<summary>Paley Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Paley (1998, elaborated in *Principles of Deformity Correction*, Springer 2002) reformulated the PFFD classification on a functional basis, emphasising the potential for hip reconstruction and for limb reconstruction. The system divides CFD into three principal types, each with subtypes, and has become the reference framework in specialised reconstruction centres.

**Type 1** — femoral head and acetabulum present; the shaft is intact or subacutely deformed. Subtypes 1a (stable hip, ossified femoral neck, no mobile proximal-femoral segment) and 1b (mobile proximal-femoral segment — pseudarthrosis or delayed ossification).

**Type 2** — femoral head present but with no bony continuity to the shaft; the acetabulum is developed or mildly dysplastic. Subtypes 2a (mobile femoral head within the acetabulum) and 2b (immobile femoral head, hanging on soft tissues only).

**Type 3** — absent or rudimentary femoral head; the acetabulum is severely dysplastic or absent. Type 3 conceptually subsumes Aitken classes C and D and demands a prosthetic solution or Van Nes rotationplasty.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The Paley classification is functional, not merely descriptive — it directly directs the operative plan: type 1 is a candidate for SUPERhip; type 2 — for SUPERhip with reconstruction of proximal femoral continuity; type 3 — for rotationplasty or Syme amputation with prosthesis.</li>
    <li>Correspondence with Aitken: Paley 1 ≈ Aitken A; Paley 2a/2b ≈ Aitken B; Paley 3 ≈ Aitken C/D.</li>
    <li>Definitive classification requires MRI in later infancy, since ossification of the proximal femur at birth is incomplete.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Clinical Features and Imaging
<details id="pffd-clinical">
<summary>Clinical Features and Imaging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The presentation is at birth or in infancy with a striking limb-length discrepancy, often concealed initially by the apparent prominence of the contralateral hip as the child stands. Examination reveals a short thigh, an externally rotated and flexed limb position, the apparent "knee at hip level" appearance in severe cases, and limited hip range of motion with fixed deformity. The foot of the affected limb is often at the level of the contralateral knee. Associated fibular hemimelia produces additional shortening below the knee, *equinovalgus* foot deformity, and absence of the lateral foot rays.

Plain radiographs at birth may underestimate the severity of the deficiency because the femoral head is unossified; they still show the general extent of femoral shortening, the presence of trochanteric ossification, and the morphology of the acetabulum. Ultrasound of the hip is useful in the neonatal period for assessing the cartilaginous femoral head and the acetabular cup. **MRI** in later infancy (about 6–12 months) is the imaging modality of choice for definitive Paley classification, identification of subtrochanteric *pseudarthrosis*, and characterisation of the cruciate ligaments and other associated knee anomalies.

</div>
</details>


### Treatment
<details id="pffd-treatment">
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The fundamental treatment decision in PFFD rests on the predicted final limb-length discrepancy at skeletal maturity and the functional potential of the affected limb. A discrepancy of less than 20% (≤7–8 cm at maturity) is typically managed with limb-lengthening protocols combined with hip reconstruction; a discrepancy greater than 20% (sometimes quoted as 30–40% in severe Class D / Paley 3) typically commits the patient to Syme amputation and prosthetic reconstruction or to Van Nes rotationplasty.

**Limb lengthening.** Lengthening protocols in Paley type 1 and 2 (Aitken A and B) use the Ilizarov method with circular external fixator, the *Taylor Spatial Frame*, or a modern motorised intramedullary nail (PRECICE), with serial distraction-histogenesis lengthenings of the femur and sometimes the tibia. Multiple lengthenings over the growing years are usually required to address the cumulative discrepancy — typically three lengthenings (around ages 4–6, 9–11, and 13–15), each about 5–8 cm. The protocols are technically demanding, with complication rates including pin-track infection, premature consolidation, joint stiffness, refracture, and stretch neurological injury.

**Van Nes rotationplasty (1950).** For Paley type 3 (Aitken C/D), where limb lengthening is not feasible, Van Nes rotationplasty offers a functional alternative: the limb is shortened, the knee is fused, the foot is rotated 180° on the longitudinal axis of the tibia so that the ankle joint serves as the functional knee, and the patient is fitted with a below-knee prosthesis that articulates with the rotated ankle. The procedure preserves an active joint (the ankle, now-knee) with proprioception and active control by the patient's own muscles, and the functional outcomes are significantly better than those of an equivalent-level amputation.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Van Nes rotationplasty for Aitken C/D (Paley 3) fuses the knee and rotates the foot 180° so the ankle becomes the functional knee — preserves active proprioceptive control.</li>
    <li>The cosmetic appearance (foot pointing posteriorly at the level of the contralateral knee) is initially confronting for families — careful preoperative counselling is essential.</li>
    <li>Long-term studies show excellent functional outcomes, high participation in athletic and occupational activities, and reasonable cosmetic acceptance with appropriate prosthetic clothing.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Knee fusion with Syme amputation and prosthetic conversion** is a third option, in which the foot is removed and the ankle/leg complex is fused to the residual femur, producing a functional above-knee limb that fits an above-knee prosthesis. The outcome is good for activities of daily living but does not match the functional capability of the Van Nes procedure.

</div>
</details>


### SUPERhip and SUPERknee Reconstruction
<details id="pffd-superhip">
<summary>SUPERhip and SUPERknee Reconstruction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Paley developed two specific reconstructive procedures that, together with the lengthening protocols, form the contemporary standard for limb reconstruction in CFD: **SUPERhip** (Systematic Utilitarian Procedure for Extremity Reconstruction — hip) and **SUPERknee** (the same principle for the knee).

**SUPERhip.** A single-stage or staged procedure to correct the typical proximal-femoral deformities of CFD before beginning lengthening. Steps: arthrodesis of the subtrochanteric *pseudarthrosis* (in Paley 1b); valgus-extension osteotomy of the proximal femur to normalise the *mechanical lateral proximal femoral angle* (MPFA) and neck-shaft angle (NSA); acetabular reorientation by pelvic osteotomy (Dega or Salter type, where acetabular morphology permits); reconstruction of the abductors by transposition or lengthening. The goal is a stable, centred, abductor-functional femur before exposure to distraction forces.

**SUPERknee.** Reconstruction of knee-joint stability before or during lengthening: cruciate-ligament reconstruction (for absent anterior or posterior cruciate), corrective osteotomy of the distal femur or proximal tibia to normalise the mechanical axes, and, where indicated, fusion of a subluxated knee position. The goal is a stable knee that can tolerate distraction forces without subluxation during lengthening.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>SUPERhip is performed before the first lengthening (usually around 18–24 months of age for isolated hip reconstruction, around 3–4 years in PFFD with fibular hemimelia) — a stable, normally aligned femur is a precondition for safe lengthening.</li>
    <li>SUPERknee cruciate-ligament reconstruction most commonly uses autograft or allograft from the iliotibial band or *semitendinosus*; for an absent anterior cruciate, a double-bundle reconstruction with epiphyseal fixation in a suitable axis.</li>
    <li>Without SUPERhip / SUPERknee preceding lengthening, the risk of hip subluxation (Paley 1b with a mobile segment) and knee subluxation during distraction is significantly increased.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Hip reconstruction in PFFD addresses the residual proximal-femoral deformity: subtrochanteric osteotomy for varus correction, arthrodesis of the *pseudarthrosis* when present, and pelvic osteotomy in type 1 and 2 (Aitken A and B) when the acetabulum requires improvement. In type 3 (Aitken C and D), hip stability cannot be achieved by reconstruction and the patient depends on a prosthesis for stability.

</div>
</details>

</div>
</details>

## Fibular Hemimelia
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="fibular">
<details>
<summary>Fibular Hemimelia</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-6/fibular-hemimelia.jpg" data-figure-alt="Fibular hemimelia, right leg" aria-label="Enlarge">

  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Fibular hemimelia: partial or complete absence of the fibula with anteromedial bowing of the tibia, limb shortening, and ankle/foot valgus.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Mosh, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fibula_Hemimelia_on_right_leg_of_patient.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 4.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Fibular hemimelia — the postaxial longitudinal deficiency of the fibula — is the commonest long-bone deficiency in children, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 50,000 to 1 in 100,000 live births. The condition is characterised by partial or complete absence of the fibula with associated deformities of the tibia, foot, and ankle. The spectrum is best understood as a **whole-limb deficiency** rather than an isolated fibular defect.


### Clinical Features
<details id="fibular-clinical">
<summary>Clinical Features</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The presentation is at birth with the characteristic constellation: anteromedial bowing of the tibia with a skin dimple over the apex of the bow; foot deformity ranging from mild valgus to severe *equinovalgus*; absence of the lateral foot rays (most commonly the fifth and fourth, and in severe cases the third — *2-3-4 ray foot*). The fibula may be palpable as a partial bone, a fibrous band-like structure (the cartilaginous anlage), or entirely absent. The knee shows associated anomalies — cruciate-ligament absence is common, particularly the anterior cruciate, with consequent knee instability that becomes clinically important as the child grows. The hip is often associated with PFFD or simple femoral shortening. Limb-length discrepancy at maturity ranges from a few centimetres in the mildest cases to 20 cm or more in the most severe.

</div>
</details>


### Achterman-Kalamchi Classification
<details id="fibular-classif">
<summary>Achterman-Kalamchi Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Achterman and Kalamchi classification (1979) divides fibular hemimelia into three types based on the radiographic appearance of the fibula.

Type IA: a shortened but present fibula with its proximal end distal to the tibial epiphysis and its distal end proximal to the tibiotalar joint.

Type IB: a partial fibula with greater reduction; the fibular anlage terminates with a fibrous tether to the talus.

Type II: complete absence of the fibula.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Achterman-Kalamchi type II (complete fibular absence) is the most severe and is associated with the greatest tibial bowing, foot deformity, and PFFD coexistence.</li>
    <li>The classification describes the radiographic morphology of the fibula only — functional decisions rest on foot function, not on the type.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Paley later developed a functional classification of fibular hemimelia (FH) that includes assessment of ankle instability, ray count, and the presence of tarsal coalitions — the basis for contemporary centre decisions between reconstruction and amputation.

</div>
</details>


### Treatment
<details id="fibular-treatment">
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Management decisions in fibular hemimelia rest on the predicted limb-length discrepancy, the foot deformity, and the **foot function**. The classical teaching, formulated by Birch and colleagues at the *Texas Scottish Rite Hospital*, emphasises the function of the foot rather than its appearance: a foot that can be made stable, plantigrade, and adequately positioned in a normal shoe is worth preserving; a foot that cannot be made functionally useful is better amputated early in life.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl — Birch principle</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Fibular hemimelia is the commonest long-bone deficiency in children.</li>
    <li>Foot function ahead of foot appearance: three or more rays generally support salvage with lengthening and reconstruction; fewer than three rays generally support early Syme (or Boyd) amputation with prosthesis.</li>
    <li>Early amputation (before walking, typically around 8–12 months of age) produces excellent long-term functional outcomes.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Limb lengthening with foot reconstruction.** The treatment of choice for patients with adequate foot function and a discrepancy projected to be less than 20% at maturity. Serial tibial lengthenings using Ilizarov or *Taylor Spatial Frame*, combined with corrective osteotomies to address the anteromedial bowing, foot reconstruction (preserving rays where possible), and knee-ligament reconstruction achieve excellent functional outcomes in experienced hands.

**Syme amputation** (disarticulation at the ankle, preserving the heel pad), or its modification **Boyd** (incorporation of the calcaneus into the distal tibia), is the alternative for patients with a non-functional foot or a discrepancy too severe for lengthening. Early amputation in the first year of life is generally well accepted; the residual limb develops with the child, the prosthesis is upgraded as the child grows, and functional outcomes are excellent in long-term studies.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The decision between amputation and lengthening must be made jointly with the family, with realistic discussion of the multiple operations, prolonged frame wear, and unpredictability of complex reconstruction versus the predictable and rapid functional outcomes of a well-fitted prosthesis.</li>
    <li>Lengthening of a fixed *equinovalgus* foot without prior correct foot reconstruction produces uncontrollable ankle-joint subluxation — foot reconstruction precedes lengthening.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

</div>
</details>

## Tibial Hemimelia
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="tibial">
<details>
<summary>Tibial Hemimelia</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Tibial hemimelia (partial or complete absence of the tibia with a preserved fibula) is substantially less common than fibular hemimelia, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 1,000,000 live births. Unlike fibular hemimelia, tibial hemimelia is frequently **familial** — autosomal dominant inheritance has been described in many series — and is often associated with hand anomalies and other syndromic features (Werner syndrome, *tibial aplasia-ectrodactyly* syndrome).


### Jones Classification
<details id="tibial-jones">
<summary>Jones Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Jones classification (1978) — the standard scheme — divides tibial hemimelia into four types based on the radiographic appearance of the tibia.

Type I — the most severe — comprises two subtypes: IA (complete absence of the tibia, hypoplastic distal femur, unossified proximal tibial epiphysis) and IB (complete absence of the tibia on radiograph but a proximal tibial anlage demonstrable on ultrasound or MRI, with a normal distal femur).

Type II — partial absence of the distal tibia with preserved proximal tibia and a functional knee.

Type III — partial absence of the proximal tibia with preserved distal tibia and a functional ankle (rare).

Type IV — the diastasis variant: both tibia and fibula are present but the distal *tibiofibular* relationship is markedly abnormal with the foot displaced into varus.

</div>
</details>


### Treatment
<details id="tibial-treatment">
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Presentation at birth shows the characteristic short limb with the foot in varus, the absent or hypoplastic tibia (palpable as a short bony stump or no tibia at all), the fibula prominent laterally, and associated hand anomalies in many cases. The knee functions only when the proximal tibia is present (Types II and III); in Type I, no functional knee exists and reconstruction must address this deficit.

**Jones Type I** — knee disarticulation with prosthesis is the standard treatment, with reasonable functional outcomes. Attempts to build a knee from the fibula in IB (where a proximal anlage is present) are possible in experienced hands but rarely yield better function than a quality disarticulation prosthesis.

**Type II and Type III** — the historical approach has been the **Brown procedure** (fibular centralisation), in which the fibula is centralised under the femur to function as a tibia. Long-term functional outcomes, however, have been disappointing because the centralised fibula often fails to bear weight and a progressive valgus knee deformity often develops; in contemporary practice, **below-knee amputation with a functional ankle prosthesis** is often preferred.

**Type IV** — ankle reconstruction with re-establishment of the distal *tibiofibular* relationship and foot stabilisation.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Tibial hemimelia is rare and often familial (autosomal dominant) — careful family history and examination of the hands for associated defects.</li>
    <li>Jones Type I lacks a functional knee — standard treatment is knee disarticulation; attempts at fibular centralisation in Type I have a poor prognosis.</li>
    <li>Brown fibular centralisation in Types II/III has disappointing long-term results — below-knee amputation with prosthesis is often preferred.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>

</div>

## Congenital Pseudarthrosis of the Tibia
<!-- toc-h2 -->

Congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia (CPT) is a rare congenital condition characterised by anterolateral bowing of the tibia in infancy that progresses to spontaneous fracture, followed by failure of normal fracture healing — a true *pseudarthrosis*. The incidence is approximately 1 in 250,000 live births. Approximately **half** of cases are associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) and CPT is one of the classical orthopaedic manifestations of NF1 (the "von Recklinghausen association"); the remainder are sporadic or associated with fibrous dysplasia.


### Crawford Classification
<details id="cpt-crawford">
<summary>Crawford Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Crawford classification (1986) — the most widely used — divides CPT into four types based on the radiographic morphology of the affected tibia.

Type I: anterolateral bowing of the tibia with a thickened cortex and a normal or thickened medullary canal; the bowing has not yet progressed to fracture.

Type II: anterolateral bowing with a sclerotic, narrow, or absent medullary canal but no fracture.

Type III: anterolateral bowing with a cystic lesion of the tibial diaphysis.

Type IV: established *pseudarthrosis* with frank discontinuity of the tibia.

The Boyd classification (1982) — an earlier scheme — uses descriptive categories from "constricted" (Type I) through "non-progressive" (Type V) and remains in occasional use.

</div>
</details>


### Clinical Features and NF1 Association
<details id="cpt-clinical">
<summary>Clinical Features and NF1 Association</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Presentation is in infancy with an anterolateral bowing of the tibia, often noted in the first 6–12 months of life as the child begins to bear weight. The bowing progresses, the affected leg shortens, and a spontaneous fracture may occur without significant trauma, typically between 12 and 24 months of age. Once fracture has occurred, the *pseudarthrosis* fails to heal with standard fracture management (closed reduction, casting, or even intramedullary fixation); progressive shortening, deformity, and recurrent failed surgical attempts are the natural history.

The signs of NF1 must be sought: *café-au-lait* spots, axillary or inguinal freckling (*Crowe's sign*), neurofibromas, Lisch nodules of the iris, optic glioma, and family history. The diagnosis of NF1 (NIH criteria) carries prognostic weight — patients with NF1 have a lower chance of primary union and a higher risk of refracture.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <strong>Pearl</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Anterolateral bowing of the tibia in infancy is the pre-fracture stage of CPT, and roughly half of cases carry NF1.</li>
    <li>Brace the bowed but unfractured tibia (Crawford I–III) to prevent the index fracture — a well-supported principle.</li>
    <li>Look for NF1 signs: ≥6 *café-au-lait* spots >5 mm prepubertally or >15 mm postpubertally, axillary/inguinal freckling, neurofibromas, Lisch nodules — the diagnosis has prognostic significance.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Treatment
<details id="cpt-treatment">
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

CPT is one of the **most difficult problems** in paediatric orthopaedics. The natural history of untreated disease is progressive deformity, shortening, and disability; surgical management requires balancing the goal of union with the realities of multiple operations, prolonged immobilisation, and the high failure rate of every available technique. The current standard of care combines several principles.

**Protection of the pre-fracture tibia.** Bracing (clamshell brace or *patellar-tendon-bearing* orthosis) of the bowed but unfractured tibia to prevent the index fracture.

**Resection and reconstruction at fracture.** Aggressive resection of the pseudarthrotic segment back to healthy bone; restoration of length and alignment; reliable internal fixation. Classical variants: Williams rod (1965) — fixation by a rod passed across the heel, replaced with growth; Fassier-Duval telescoping rod — grows with the child by a telescoping mechanism; Ilizarov external fixation with resection-compression of the *pseudarthrosis* and lengthening of adjacent normal bone.

**Biological enhancement.** Autogenous bone graft from the iliac crest (*gold standard*); BMP-2 or BMP-7 infusion at the *pseudarthrosis* site (demonstrated benefit in several series); periosteal graft. The vascularised fibular graft from the contralateral leg, popularised by the work of Weiland and colleagues, is an option for refractory cases with a long bone defect (>4 cm).

**Protection after consolidation.** Continued bracing until skeletal maturity — refracture is common, especially in the first 2–3 years after consolidation.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Refracture is common even after radiographic union — continued bracing until skeletal maturity is mandatory.</li>
    <li>Amputation remains a legitimate option for patients with extensive failure after multiple operations and established disability — it should not be perceived as a surgical defeat but as a rational choice for functional prosthetic reconstruction.</li>
    <li>For an older child with advanced disability and multiple failed operations, the discussion of amputation should begin early in the treatment course.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Other Congenital Anomalies of the Lower Leg
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="other">
<details>
<summary>Other Congenital Anomalies of the Lower Leg</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Posteromedial bowing of the tibia.** A congenital deformity of the tibial diaphysis with the apex pointing posteromedially. In contrast to anterolateral bowing (which is associated with CPT), posteromedial bowing is **benign** and typically resolves spontaneously over the first few years of life, although it produces a predictable limb-length discrepancy that requires later management. The condition is typically isolated and not associated with neurofibromatosis. The natural history is progressive resolution of the bowing with growth, but with a predictable shortening of the affected tibia ranging from a few millimetres to several centimetres at skeletal maturity; epiphysiodesis of the contralateral distal femur or proximal tibia, planned by careful prediction of remaining growth, addresses the discrepancy.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <strong>Pitfall</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Do not confuse anterolateral with posteromedial bowing. Anterolateral is the pre-fracture stage of CPT and demands bracing and NF1 workup; posteromedial is benign, resolves spontaneously, has no NF1 association, and only leaves a predictable limb-length discrepancy.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

**Anterolateral bowing of the tibia.** The precursor of CPT and almost always associated with NF1 — discussed in the preceding section. The critical clinical importance is the prevention of fracture by bracing during infancy and early childhood.

**Congenital pseudarthrosis of the fibula.** Much less common than the tibial form and typically requires no specific treatment, since the fibula contributes only a small fraction of lower-limb load-bearing. Symptomatic cases can be treated by partial fibulectomy or pseudarthrosis revision.

**Congenital pseudarthrosis of the clavicle.** Included here for completeness, though it is an upper-limb condition. It is almost always right-sided (the rare left-sided cases are typically associated with *dextrocardia*), produces a painless or mildly painful prominence at the mid-clavicle in infancy or early childhood, and is treated by resection of the *pseudarthrosis* with bone graft and internal fixation when symptomatic; many cases require no treatment.

## Treatment Principles and Outcomes
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="principles">
<details>
<summary>Treatment Principles and Outcomes</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The management of congenital limb deficiencies follows several unifying principles.

First, **assessment must be comprehensive**: the entire affected limb, the contralateral limb, the pelvic and hip joints, the spine, and the upper limbs and viscera must be examined and imaged, since associated anomalies are the rule, not the exception.

Second, **projection of final limb-length discrepancy** at skeletal maturity — using the Moseley straight-line graph, the Paley-Bhave *multiplier method*, or contemporary Pritchett-Paley non-linear models — is the foundation of planning, since the discrepancy guides the choice between lengthening, amputation, or compensatory shortening of the contralateral limb.

Third, the **plan must be developed in close consultation with the family**, with realistic discussion of the multiple operations and prolonged rehabilitation that lengthening protocols entail versus the predictable and rapid recovery of a well-fitted prosthesis.

Fourth, **foot function** (its potential to bear weight, fit in a shoe, and contribute to gait) is often the single most important factor in deciding between salvage and amputation — the Birch principle.

Fifth, **timing of definitive procedures** must be coordinated with the child's growth and developmental milestones: early amputation in the first year of life allows prosthetic adaptation through development; lengthening protocols are typically initiated in early childhood and continued through adolescence; SUPERhip is performed before the first lengthening.

**Biological basis of lengthening — Ilizarov.** Distraction histogenesis, described by Ilizarov in *Transosseous Osteosynthesis* (Springer 1992) over a series of more than 25,000 patients, rests on the law of tension-stress: controlled graded tension on living tissues stimulates their proliferative and biosynthetic activation. The principles for safe lengthening are: a **latency period** of 5–7 days after corticotomy (sparing the medullary canal and periosteal blood supply) before beginning distraction; a **rate** of 1 mm/day divided into 4 equal steps of 0.25 mm — the rhythm is more important than the nominal rate; and a **consolidation phase** of approximately twice the duration of distraction, during which the fixator is retained to protect the regenerate.

**Outcomes.** Contemporary multidisciplinary management of PFFD produces good functional outcomes in most children. Van Nes rotationplasty has been the subject of long-term follow-up studies showing excellent functional outcomes, high participation in athletic and occupational activities, and reasonable cosmetic acceptance with appropriate prosthetic clothing. Lengthening protocols for Paley type 1 and 2 (Aitken A/B) disease produce equivalent functional outcomes when successful, but at the cost of multiple operations, prolonged frame wear, and significant complication rates. Outcomes in fibular hemimelia are excellent with early Syme amputation for a non-functional foot, and good with lengthening and reconstruction for a functional foot. Tibial hemimelia is less favourable, especially Jones type I, which typically ends with knee disarticulation. CPT remains the most challenging condition with high refracture rates and frequent need for multiple operations; amputation remains part of the armamentarium.

## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Gavriil A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1921–1992).** A surgeon from Kurgan (Siberia) who, over a series of more than 25,000 patients, demonstrated the **law of tension-stress** — controlled graded tension on living tissues stimulates their proliferative and biosynthetic activation — and created the eponymous circular external fixator. *Transosseous Osteosynthesis* (1971 Russian, Springer 1992 English) remains the reference work. Ilizarov's principles — gentle corticotomy with preservation of the medullary canal and periosteal blood supply, latency 5–7 days, rate 1 mm/day divided into 4 steps, consolidation ~2× the distraction phase — are the basis of all contemporary limb lengthening, including in PFFD, fibular hemimelia, and resection-compression schemes in CPT.

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school; the eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya. The three-volume *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curricular reference and contains descriptions of the classical approaches to lower-limb congenital deficiencies in the Bulgarian tradition: Syme amputation and prosthetic fitting, knee arthrodesis with *pseudarthrosis* resection, and classical techniques for correction of varus and valgus deformities before the era of distraction fixators.

**A. A. Korzh <sup class="natflag">UA</sup> and colleagues.** The Kharkiv school developed variants of resection-compression of the tibial *pseudarthrosis* with adjacent lengthening according to Ilizarov's principles; integrated Ilizarov distraction into a system of staged management of CPT with a high primary-union rate in published series.

**R. R. Vreden <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>, Ya. L. Tsivyan <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>, V. D. Chaklin <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** The classical Russian school of vertebrotomy and reconstructive osteotomy — although mainly devoted to the spine and hip — established principles of staged management and prolonged immobilisation that the Bulgarian curriculum applies in severe CPT cases and in lengthenings for PFFD.

</div>
</details>

## Classical Sources
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="classical">
<details>
<summary>Classical Sources</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical lineage — congenital lower-limb deficiencies</strong>
  <p>The description of congenital lower-limb deficiencies is a product of the classical orthopaedic literature of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Hoffa (1905) provides the first systematic orthopaedic-classical synthesis in *Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie*. Aitken (1969) formulated the modern classification of PFFD; Paley (1998) reformulated it on a functional basis.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Hoffa 1905</strong> — *Lehrbuch der orthopädischen Chirurgie* (Stuttgart: Enke); chapters on congenital limb deficiencies (<a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderortho00hoff">facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Aitken 1969</strong> — four-class classification of PFFD (A–D), the standard of the era.</li>
    <li><strong>Brown 1965</strong> — fibular centralisation in tibial hemimelia (today with disappointing long-term outcomes).</li>
    <li><strong>Boyd 1982 / Crawford 1986</strong> — classifications of congenital pseudarthrosis of the tibia.</li>
    <li><strong>Achterman, Kalamchi 1979</strong> — classification of fibular hemimelia.</li>
    <li><strong>Jones 1978</strong> — classification of tibial hemimelia.</li>
    <li><strong>Ilizarov 1971 / Springer 1992</strong> — *Transosseous Osteosynthesis*; biological foundation of distraction histogenesis, which makes contemporary reconstructive lengthening in PFFD possible at all.</li>
    <li><strong>Paley 1998 / 2002</strong> — functional classification of CFD and the SUPERhip and SUPERknee operations.</li>
    <li><strong>Van Nes 1950</strong> — rotationplasty; *Rotation-plasty for congenital defects of the femur* (J Bone Joint Surg Br).</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

Classical descriptions by condition: Aitken 1969 (PFFD classification); Brown 1965 (fibular centralisation); Pappas 1983 (nine-class extended classification of PFFD); Birch 1990s (the principle "foot function ahead of foot appearance"); Williams 1965 (intramedullary rod in CPT); Fassier-Duval 2000s (telescoping rod); Weiland 1980s (vascularised fibular graft in CPT).
