## Overview and Terminology
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview and Terminology</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is a spectrum of disorders of the developing hip ranging from mild acetabular dysplasia in a stable joint to frank dislocation of the femoral head from the acetabulum. The term "developmental" (preferred since the 1990s over the historical "congenital") emphasizes that the abnormality is not exclusively present at birth: many cases develop in the days, weeks, or months following birth as the immature hip is exposed to environmental forces, and some cases declare themselves only as the child begins to walk.

The spectrum encompasses: the unstable but reduced hip; the subluxable hip (in which gentle stress produces partial displacement); the dislocatable hip (in which gentle stress produces complete displacement); the dislocated but reducible hip (Ortolani-positive); and the dislocated and irreducible hip (in which secondary acetabular changes prevent reduction without surgery). The term "teratological dislocation" is reserved for the rare neonatal hip dislocation associated with arthrogryposis, neuromuscular conditions, and chromosomal disorders, in which the dislocation is fixed at birth and behaves differently from typical DDH.

## Epidemiology and Risk Factors
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="epidemiology">
<details>
<summary>Epidemiology and Risk Factors</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The incidence of DDH varies substantially with population, screening method, and case definition. Frank dislocation in the newborn is reported at **1–2 per 1,000** live births; instability of any degree (including subluxable and dislocatable hips that subsequently stabilize) at **5–20 per 1,000**; and acetabular dysplasia on later imaging at higher rates still. Geographic and ethnic variation is striking: very high prevalence has been reported in some Native American populations (swaddling with the hips in extension and adduction) and in Eastern Europe, while low prevalence is reported in African populations (carrying infants on the mother's back with the hips in abduction).

The classical risk factors form the mnemonic accepted in most pediatric texts: **female sex** (sixfold increased risk), **breech presentation** (the single most powerful environmental factor, with the highest risk for late frank breech rather than complete or footling), **positive family history** (first-degree relative), **firstborn** child (uterine packing), **oligohydramnios**, and other "packaging" disorders (congenital muscular torticollis and *metatarsus adductus*), each of which should prompt screening of the hip. The left hip is more commonly involved than the right (approximately **60% left, 20% right, 20% bilateral**), and this asymmetry is attributed to the typical fetal position with the left hip in adduction against the maternal sacrum.

  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Risk-factor stack for DDH: female sex, breech presentation (especially late frank breech), positive family history, firstborn, oligohydramnios, and other packaging disorders (torticollis, *metatarsus adductus*).</li>
    <li>The left hip is involved in roughly 60% of cases; bilateral involvement in about 20%.</li>

### The Five Obstacles to Reduction
<details id="five-obstacles">
<summary>The Five Obstacles to Reduction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">

    <span class="md-figure-caption">Spectrum of developmental hip dysplasia: from mild acetabular dysplasia through subluxation to complete dislocation of the femoral head out of the acetabulum.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Londenp, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hip_dysplasia_-_schematic.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY-SA 3.0</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The dysplastic hip exhibits a constellation of bony and soft-tissue abnormalities that progress with continued dislocation and that determine the difficulty of reduction and the long-term outcome. The acetabulum is shallow, sloped, and faces posterosuperiorly rather than laterally; the labrum is thickened, inverted, and forms an inverted-V *limbus* obstructing reduction; the *pulvinar* (fibro-fatty tissue at the base of the acetabulum) is hypertrophied and fills the empty socket; the *transverse acetabular ligament* hypertrophies and contracts; the *iliopsoas* tendon crosses the joint anteriorly and develops an hourglass constriction over the joint capsule, producing the classical "hourglass" or "dumbbell" appearance of the capsule on dye arthrography.

Five obstacles to closed reduction are classically described and must be understood at open reduction:

1. Inverted *limbus* (labrum).
2. Hypertrophied *pulvinar* (fibro-fatty floor).
3. Contracted *iliopsoas* tendon — capsular hourglass.
4. Contracted *transverse acetabular ligament*.
5. Capsular contracture.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Memorize the five classical obstacles to closed reduction of DDH: inverted *limbus*, hypertrophied *pulvinar*, contracted *iliopsoas* tendon (producing the capsular hourglass), contracted transverse acetabular ligament, and capsular contracture. Open reduction must release every one.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### The Proximal Femoral Complex
<details id="proximal-femur">
<summary>The Proximal Femoral Complex</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The femoral head is small and incompletely ossified, sometimes deformed by pressure against the false acetabulum that develops on the lateral aspect of the iliac wing. The femoral neck is short, anteverted, and progressively becomes valgus with continued dislocation. The hip adductor muscles, particularly *adductor longus*, become contracted in the dislocated position.

Per Tönnis: in untreated severe acetabular dysplasia with subluxation of the femoral head, there is mediocaudal flattening of the head, a very small EY angle, and a large CCD angle; the line of the prevailing compressive stress approaches the vertical. Excessive femoral anteversion (>50°) and valgus (CCD angle >150°) are common findings that justify a varus-derotation osteotomy at a later stage.

</div>

### Ortolani and Barlow Maneuvers
<details id="ortolani-barlow">
<summary>Ortolani and Barlow Maneuvers</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The **Ortolani maneuver**, described by Marino Ortolani in 1937, is a test for the dislocated but reducible hip. With the infant supine and relaxed, both hips are flexed to 90°, the thigh is gently abducted, and the examiner's middle finger placed over the greater trochanter exerts gentle anterior pressure. A positive test is the palpable **clunk** (not "click") as the dislocated femoral head reduces over the posterior acetabular rim into the socket. The maneuver is sensitive in the first weeks of life; it becomes negative once secondary changes prevent reduction.

The **Barlow maneuver**, described by Thomas Barlow in 1962, is a test for the dislocatable hip. With the infant supine and the hip flexed and adducted, the examiner applies gentle posterior pressure on the knee. A positive test is the palpable clunk as the femoral head dislocates posteriorly over the acetabular rim. The maneuver identifies the unstable hip that is currently reduced but can be dislocated by stress.

The examiner must distinguish the high-pitched soft **click** produced by ligamentous or tendinous snap (common, soft, innocuous) from the deeper **clunk** of true hip displacement (specific).

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Ortolani relocates a dislocated hip (abduct + lift); Barlow dislocates a reducible one (adduct + push).</li>
    <li>A clunk is the diagnostic sign; clicks are common and innocuous.</li>
    <li>Both signs become negative after about 3 months as secondary changes develop.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Signs in the Older Infant
<details id="secondary-signs">
<summary>Signs in the Older Infant</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In infants beyond 3 months, the Ortolani and Barlow signs become negative; the following signs take over:

- **Limited hip abduction** — less than 70° in unilateral disease, with asymmetry between the affected and normal sides being the most reliable sign; in bilateral disease, the symmetric limitation is often missed. Per Tönnis: with frank dislocation, abduction may be limited to 30–50°; with isolated dysplasia, only by 10–30°.
- **Apparent shortening** of the affected limb (Galeazzi sign): with both hips flexed to 90°, the knees are at different levels.
- **Asymmetric thigh and gluteal skin folds** — a non-specific sign that is overemphasized in some teaching.
- **Klisic test** — the line connecting the greater trochanter to the anterior superior iliac spine passes below the umbilicus in a dislocated hip (rather than above in a normal hip).
- **Ludloff sign** — passive knee extension with the hip flexed to 90° is limited in dislocation.

In the walking child with an undetected dislocation, the gait is characteristic: **Trendelenburg gait** (lateral lurch of the trunk over the affected hip during stance phase, with drop of the contralateral pelvis), apparent leg-length discrepancy with toe-walking on the affected side, and increased lumbar lordosis with bilateral disease.

### Graf Hip Sonography
<details id="graf">
<summary>Graf Hip Sonography</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

<figure class="md-figure">
  <button class="md-figure-zoom" type="button" data-figure-src="/img/ortho-7/graf-us.jpg" data-figure-alt="Graf-type hip ultrasound, normal Type Ia" aria-label="Enlarge">
    <img src="/img/ortho-7/graf-us.jpg" alt="Graf-type hip ultrasound, normal Type Ia" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </button>
  <figcaption>
    <span class="md-figure-caption">Newborn hip ultrasound: normal joint (Graf Ia) with α-angle &gt;60° and full bony coverage of the femoral head.</span>
    <span class="md-figure-attribution"> · Christaras A, <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ultrasound_-_normal_newborn_left_hip_(Graf_type_Ia).jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CC BY 2.5</a></span>
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The classification of **Reinhard Graf** (Stolzalpe, Austria), developed in the 1980s, is the standard ultrasound classification of the infant hip and is universally used in centers performing screening ultrasound. Graf's principle: "The better the diagnosis (typing), the more selective and effective the treatment." The triumph of hip sonography is the detection of clinically silent dysplasias that, untreated, would progress to dislocation or persistent dysplasia (Graf IIc).

**Technique.** Standardized coronal-section ultrasound image of the hip with the infant in lateral decubitus. Three anatomical landmarks must be identified (lower limb of the *os ilium*, the central acetabular fossa, and the transition from concavity to convexity at the bony rim). Two angles are measured:

- **α-angle** — between the iliac line (*base line*) and the bony acetabular roof (*bony roof line*); a measure of acetabular depth.
- **β-angle** — between the iliac line and the cartilaginous roof line referenced to the labrum; a measure of cartilaginous coverage.

**Classification.** The systematic types per Graf (per Sonometer Table 6.1) are:

- **Type I — mature hip.** α ≥60°; bony roof good; bony rim angular or slightly rounded ("blunt"); cartilaginous roof covers the femoral head. Subdivided by β: **Ia** (β <55°, longer cartilaginous coverage), **Ib** (β >55°, shorter cartilaginous coverage). Any age.
- **Type IIa(+) — physiologically immature, appropriate for age.** α = 50–59°; bony rim rounded; cartilaginous roof covers the head. Up to 12 weeks of life. Expected minimum maturation: α 50° at birth → 53° at 4 weeks → 57° at 8 weeks → 60° at 12 weeks.
- **Type IIa(−) — physiologically immature with maturation deficit.** α = 50–59° after 6 weeks not meeting the minimum linear maturation curve. Treatment indicated by today's standard.
- **Type IIb — delayed ossification.** α = 50–59° beyond 3 months. True dysplasia. Treatment.
- **Type IIc — critical zone.** α = 43–49°, β <77°. Severely deficient; close to decentering. "IIc stable" versus "IIc unstable." Treatment unconditional.
- **Type D — decentering hip.** α = 43–49°, β >77°. Bony rim rounded to flattened; cartilaginous roof displaced. Treatment.
- **Type III — dislocated with everted labrum.** α <43°; bony roof poor; bony rim flattened; proximal perichondrium directed cranially. Subtypes: **IIIa** — no structural disturbance of the hyaline cartilage (hypoechoic), **IIIb** — structural disturbance ("degeneration" — echogenic hyaline cartilage; a rarity in the era of early screening).
- **Type IV — dislocated with inverted, deformed labrum.** α <43°; cartilaginous roof pressed caudally; perichondrium runs horizontal or dips toward the original acetabulum; fibro-fatty tissue fills the empty socket.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <ul>
    <li>The α-angle determines the type; the β-angle adds individual differentiation (especially Ia vs Ib and Type D).</li>
    <li>Graf's rule of thumb: α (US) + AC (X-ray) ≈ 90°.</li>
    <li>By the end of week 12 a Type I hip (α ≥60°) must be attained; further delay is no longer physiologic and indicates dysplasia.</li>
    <li>Distinguishing Type III from Type IV is anatomic (course of the perichondrium), not measurement-based — both have α <43°.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Type IIa(+) versus IIa(−) should not be distinguished before 6 weeks — measurement imprecision precludes reliable differentiation.</li>
    <li>α <42° means the socket is so flat that the cartilaginous roof cannot retain the head — dislocation is an anatomic matter (Type III or IV), not a measurement decision.</li>
    <li>The sonometer must be read in the standard plane at magnification ≥1:1.7 — otherwise the measurements lack accuracy.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Tönnis Classification and AC Index
<details id="tonnis">
<summary>Tönnis Classification and AC Index</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Tönnis classification (CSHD, 1978/1982)** — based on the position of the femoral head ossific nucleus relative to Hilgenreiner's and Perkin's lines on an AP pelvic radiograph:

- **Grade 1.** Ossific nucleus medial to the vertical line through the superior acetabular rim (Perkin/Ombrédanne line).
- **Grade 2.** Nucleus lateral to Perkin's line but below the superior acetabular rim.
- **Grade 3.** Nucleus level with the superior acetabular rim.
- **Grade 4.** Nucleus above the superior acetabular rim.

**Howorth/Dunn classification (arthrography-based)** — four grades:

- **Grade 1** — unstable, subluxatable hip in the newborn (by definition, birth to 2–3 weeks); Ortolani-positive; articular surfaces still congruent.
- **Grade 2** — subluxated hip; labrum and superior acetabular rim deformed; femoral head rides on the rim.
- **Grade 3** — complete dislocation; head outside the acetabulum, has crossed the labrum.
- **Grade 4** — long-standing dislocation with a false acetabulum (Tönnis incorporates this in late follow-up).

**Acetabular index (AC angle of Hilgenreiner)** — angle between Hilgenreiner's line (horizontal through the triradiate cartilages) and the acetabular roof. Normal values per Tönnis-Brünken (1968) show a clear age-dependence: newborn ≤30°, 4 months ≤25°, 12 months ≤22°, 24 months ≤20°, 6 years ≤15°. Values exceeding 2 standard deviations are categorically pathologic on long-term follow-up.

</div>
</details>

Plain radiographs become useful as the ossific nucleus of the femoral head appears, typically at 3–6 months of age. The standard AP view of the pelvis is interpreted using:

- **Hilgenreiner's line** — horizontal through the triradiate cartilages; basis for further measurement.
- **Perkin's (Ombrédanne) line** — vertical at the lateral edge of the acetabulum.
- **Shenton's line** — a smooth curve along the inferior border of the superior pubic ramus and the medial border of the femoral neck; broken in dislocation.
- **Position of the ossific nucleus** — should lie in the inferomedial quadrant defined by Hilgenreiner and Perkin.
- **Acetabular index** — <30° in infants.

  <span class="callout-label">NB</span>

    <li>Arthrography is required in Graf Type III/IV and prior to reduction — it determines whether the head can be reduced without endangering vascular supply.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Pavlik Harness (0–6 months)
<details id="pavlik">
<summary>Pavlik Harness (0–6 months)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The harness of **Arnold Pavlik (1944, 1957)** is the standard treatment for the neonatal dislocated, dislocatable, or dysplastic hip up to approximately 6 months of age. The upper part consists of two shoulder straps that cross posteriorly and attach to a wide chest band fastening anteriorly. From the chest band, two anterior straps extend to the knees and two posterior straps to the calves. The anterior strap maintains hip flexion at **100–110°** (Tönnis: at least 110° to prevent redislocation); the posterior strap prevents adduction.

**Rationale** (per Tönnis): strong flexion directs the femoral head from its posterosuperior position toward the acetabulum, allowing deep reduction over time; the limbs are allowed to fall into abduction under their own weight and through gradual stretching of the adductors; the pull of the adductors guides the head into the socket. The hip is held in the "**human position**" (flexion 90–110° with gentle abduction), which encourages spontaneous reduction (the Ortolani maneuver is, in effect, performed continuously by the harness) and provides the optimal environment for acetabular development.

**Results.** Successful Pavlik harness treatment achieves reduction in 80–95% of Ortolani-positive hips treated before 3 months of age, with success rates falling for older infants and for hips with more secondary changes.

**Limits and contraindications.** Failure of the harness — failure to achieve reduction within **3–4 weeks**, persistent dislocation on ultrasound after 6–12 weeks of treatment, or the development of complications — is an indication to abandon the harness and convert to closed or open reduction. Persistence with an unsuccessful harness produces "**Pavlik harness disease**":

- Avascular necrosis of the femoral head (compression of the *medial femoral circumflex artery* against the *iliopsoas* tendon under extreme abduction).
- Posterior dislocation from prolonged stretch on the posterior structures.
- *Femoral nerve* palsy with excessive flexion.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Pavlik limits: flexion 90–110° (>120° → femoral nerve palsy); abduction not beyond 60° (extreme abduction → compression of the medial femoral circumflex artery → AVN).</li>
    <li>If the harness has not reduced the hip in 3–4 weeks, abandon it; persistence produces Pavlik harness disease.</li>
    <li>Tönnis: the Pavlik harness carries a high risk of ischemic necrosis in high dislocations and should be used only after confirming the joint is free of significant obstructive factors.</li>
    <li>For newborns with mild to moderate instability, a softer device — an abduction pillow (Frejka/Becker/Mittelmeier) — is often sufficient.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Closed Reduction and Hip Spica (6–18 months)
<details id="closed-reduction">
<summary>Closed Reduction and Spica (6–18 months)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For infants over 6 months in whom the Pavlik harness has failed or who present late, closed reduction under general anesthesia followed by hip spica casting is the next step. The procedure involves:

1. Examination of the hip under anesthesia.
2. **Adductor tenotomy** if hip abduction is restricted (typically required if abduction is less than 60–70°).
3. Gentle reduction of the femoral head into the acetabulum under image intensification — Ortolani maneuver with longitudinal traction and abduction with the hip flexed approximately **120°**.
4. **Arthrography** to confirm a concentric reduction and to identify any obstacles to reduction.
5. Immobilization in a hip spica cast in the "**human position**" (flexion 90–100°, abduction 40–50°, neutral or slight internal rotation); per Tönnis, 110–120° flexion with 50–60° abduction is the fastest and most reliable mode of fixation. The "frog-leg" (Lorenz) position of extreme abduction has been abandoned because of the risk of AVN.
6. The cast is typically maintained for 3–6 months, with a change to a less restrictive abduction brace as healing progresses.

**Arthrographic criteria.** A medial dye pool **<5 mm** at the inferomedial acetabulum is acceptable; >5 mm suggests inadequate reduction and indicates conversion to open reduction. The **"safe zone of Ramsey"** defines the range of abduction within which the hip remains reduced; a hip that dislocates with less than 25° from full abduction has an inadequate safe zone and is at risk of redislocation.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <ul>
    <li>After closed reduction confirm concentricity by arthrography: a medial dye pool <5 mm is acceptable, >5 mm calls for open reduction.</li>
    <li>The Ramsey safe zone is the arc of abduction in which the hip stays reduced; less than 25° from full abduction is inadequate and predicts redislocation.</li>
    <li>Alternative to the "frog-leg" position — the squatting position of Fettweis or the "human position" of Salter; both reduce pressure on the medial femoral circumflex artery and optimize remodeling.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Open Reduction
<details id="open-reduction">
<summary>Open Reduction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Open reduction is indicated when closed reduction fails or when secondary changes preclude closed reduction. Standard approaches:

- **Anterior (Smith-Petersen) approach** — the most common in the older infant and toddler. Bikini incision, splitting of the iliac apophysis, exposure of the capsule from the iliac lamina and from *iliopsoas*. Allows concurrent acetabular reconstruction (Salter, Pemberton).
- **Medial (Ludloff) approach** — useful in the younger infant (6–12 months): a transverse or oblique incision below the inguinal crease, splitting the interval between *pectineus* and *adductor longus*, exposing the *iliopsoas* tendon and adductors at the lesser trochanter. Direct access to the obstacles in the medial aspect of the joint without the morbidity of the anterior approach, but without access for acetabular reconstruction.
- **Combined approaches** — when concurrent acetabular reconstruction is planned.

**Principles of open reduction.** Capsulotomy to enter the joint → identification and excision of the five classical obstacles (*limbus*, *pulvinar*, hypertrophied transverse ligament) → lengthening or release of the contracted *iliopsoas* tendon and adductor tendon → capsulorraphy to tighten the redundant joint capsule → reduction of the femoral head into the deepened acetabulum. Hip spica casting follows for 6–12 weeks, with continued part-time bracing for further months.

### Femoral Varus-Derotation Osteotomy
<details id="femoral-osteotomy">
<summary>Femoral Varus-Derotation Osteotomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The femoral varus-derotation osteotomy (VDRO) addresses the typical proximal femoral anatomy of DDH: excessive anteversion and valgus. The osteotomy is typically performed in the **subtrochanteric region** with a blade plate or angled-blade plate fixation. Indications:

- Excessive anteversion (>50–60°).
- Excessive valgus (CCD angle >150°).
- Femoral head deformity with incongruency.

Per Pauwels' principles: the procedure improves congruency, reduces shear forces on the dysplastic acetabulum, and supports acetabular remodeling in the growing child. In the older child VDRO may be combined with a **femoral shortening osteotomy** to permit reduction without excessive tension on the neurovascular structures.

</div>
</details>


### Pelvic Osteotomies
<details id="pelvic-osteotomies">
<summary>Pelvic Osteotomies</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The choice depends on the age of the child, the morphology of the dysplasia, and the state of the triradiate cartilage.

**Salter innominate osteotomy** (Robert Salter, Toronto, 1961) — a single-cut osteotomy of the innominate bone extending from the *greater sciatic notch* to the *anterior inferior iliac spine*; rotation of the distal segment around the *symphysis pubis* redirects the acetabulum anteriorly and laterally; a triangular bone graft from the iliac crest is wedged into the open osteotomy and fixed with two threaded Kirschner wires. Depends on the elasticity of the symphysis pubis — indicated for children between **18 months and 6 years**.

**Pemberton osteotomy** — an incomplete supra-acetabular osteotomy with the inferior cortex left intact; pivots on the triradiate cartilage and achieves steeper redirection than Salter. Can be performed up to skeletal maturity provided the triradiate cartilage is open. Often combined with open reduction in the older child.

**Dega osteotomy** — incomplete supra-acetabular osteotomy with the posterior cortex left intact; redirects the acetabulum anteriorly. Preferred in neuromuscular dysplasia (cerebral palsy), where the deficit is principally posterior.

**Triple pelvic osteotomy (Steel, Tönnis)** — separate osteotomies of the *ilium*, *ischium*, and *pubis*; allows rotation of the entire acetabular fragment. Used in older children and adolescents with a **closed triradiate cartilage**, in whom Salter and Pemberton are no longer possible.

**Periacetabular osteotomy (Ganz PAO)** — the modern standard for the dysplastic adolescent and adult hip with a **closed triradiate cartilage**. Four osteotomies around the acetabulum (ischial, supra-acetabular, partial pubic, retroacetabular) free the entire acetabulum as a single fragment while preserving the posterior column of the pelvis intact; the fragment is rotated to provide optimal coverage and fixed with multiple screws. Transformed the management of adult hip dysplasia — joint preservation and avoidance of arthroplasty.

**Chiari osteotomy** — a salvage procedure for the older child or adolescent with severe dysplasia and a non-congruent joint, in whom redirectional osteotomies are not feasible. A cut through the iliac wing immediately superior to the acetabular rim, with medial displacement of the inferior fragment; the joint is not redirected — the femoral head is provided with additional weight-bearing surface (metaplastic articular cartilage).

**Shelf acetabuloplasty** — cortico-cancellous bone graft fixed to the lateral acetabular rim to extend coverage. Used as an adjunct to reduction in younger children (>3–4 years) and as a salvage procedure for non-congruent joints.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Pelvic osteotomy choice depends on the triradiate cartilage and morphology: Salter (single cut, redirecting) at 18 months to 6 years; Pemberton (incomplete, pivots on the triradiate) for steeper coverage; Dega for posterior deficit in neuromuscular hips; triple (Steel/Tönnis) or Ganz PAO once the triradiate is closed.</li>
    <li>PAO is the modern adolescent and adult standard for the concentric, congruent dysplastic hip.</li>
    <li>Salvage procedures (Chiari, shelf) — for non-congruent hips where redirection is not feasible.</li>
  </ul>

## Avascular Necrosis of the Femoral Head
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="avn">
<details>
<summary>Avascular Necrosis of the Femoral Head</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The principal iatrogenic complication of DDH treatment is **avascular necrosis** of the femoral head, produced by compression of the *medial femoral circumflex artery* against the *iliopsoas* tendon during excessive abduction. Tönnis reported 14.8% AVN after traction-based treatment and 8.4% after primary open reduction (CSHD collective statistics).

**Kalamchi-MacEwen classification (1980)** — four grades:

- **Grade I** — transient changes confined to the ossific nucleus. The nucleus may be mottled, delayed, or fragmented; the *physis* is uninvolved; the nucleus usually regains its spherical shape with minimal residual height loss or *coxa magna*.
- **Grade II** — lateral physeal injury; early signs are ossification irregularity or bridging of the growth plate laterally; secondary valgus deformity ("head-in-neck deformity") from premature lateral fusion with continued medial growth. Subluxation often develops due to inadequate coverage.
- **Grade III** — central physeal injury; shortened femoral neck with relative trochanteric overgrowth; the CCD angle is preserved but the neck is short. The head is rounded but progressively broad, with incongruency.
- **Grade IV** — complete physeal arrest; delayed ossification, early fragmentation, flattening, *coxa magna*; the neck is structurally irregular, broadened, shortened, and in *coxa vara*. Pronounced trochanteric overgrowth. Acetabular development is retarded; late progressive subluxation and limb shortening.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <ul>
    <li>Iatrogenic AVN is the principal complication of DDH treatment; Kalamchi-MacEwen I–IV (transient changes; lateral physeal injury with valgus; central arrest with short neck and trochanteric overgrowth; complete physeal arrest).</li>
    <li>Avoid the "frog-leg" (Lorenz) position and any extreme abduction in cast or brace.</li>
    <li>Tönnis: evaluate AVN on two planes (AP + axial) — anterolateral and posteromedial involvement is often missed on the standard AP film.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## The Neglected and Adolescent Hip
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="neglected">
<details>
<summary>The Neglected and Adolescent Hip</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Neglected DDH presents as a unilateral or bilateral dislocation in an older child or adolescent who has not received treatment. The anatomic challenges are formidable: a false acetabulum has developed on the lateral iliac wall, the true acetabulum is small and dysplastic, the femoral head is deformed, the neck is short and anteverted, the soft tissues are severely contracted, and the limb is shortened.

Treatment options:

- **Open reduction with femoral shortening osteotomy** (to allow reduction without excessive tension on the neurovascular structures) combined with a pelvic osteotomy.
- **Salvage procedures** (Chiari, shelf) in severe incongruency.
- **Acceptance of the dislocated position** with later total hip arthroplasty in adulthood.
- For residual deformity in late reconstruction — **Ilizarov external fixation for distraction osteogenesis** in complex multiplanar corrections of the proximal femur and pelvic ring.

The choice depends on the age at presentation, the unilateral or bilateral nature of the dislocation, and the severity of the secondary changes.

</div>
</details>

## Outcomes and Adolescent/Adult Dysplasia
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Adolescent/Adult Dysplasia</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The long-term outcome of DDH depends on the age at diagnosis, the success of initial treatment, and the development of residual dysplasia. Early diagnosis and successful Pavlik harness treatment produces near-normal hips in 90–95% of cases. Late diagnosis or failed initial treatment with subsequent open reduction and osteotomy produces less favorable outcomes — residual dysplasia, AVN, and early osteoarthritis as the principal long-term sequelae.

Adolescent and adult hip dysplasia presents with **groin pain**, *femoroacetabular impingement* symptoms, and progressive arthritis. Management:

- **Periacetabular osteotomy (Ganz PAO)** in the appropriate candidate with a concentric, congruent joint.
- **Arthroscopic management** of concomitant labral pathology in well-centered, mildly dysplastic hips.
- **Total hip arthroplasty** in advanced disease with destroyed articular cartilage.

## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopedic school; the National Specialised Hospital for Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya bears his name. His three-volume *Surgical Orthopaedics* (Sofia) contains detailed accounts of the classical procedures for congenital hip dislocation, including indications for closed and open reduction and postoperative spica immobilization in the Bulgarian tradition — the foundation of curricular training for generations of Bulgarian orthopaedists.

**Reinhard Graf <sup class="natflag">AT</sup> (Stolzalpe).** Hip sonography in the newborn and infant: standardized coronal plane with three landmarks, α- and β-angle measurement, systematic four-type classification with subtypes (Sonometer Table 6.1), and the universally adopted national neonatal screening programs in Austria, Germany, and Switzerland. "Better ultrasound today than a limp tomorrow!" — Graf's motto captures the concept: early detection of clinically silent dysplasia before dislocation develops.

**Dietrich Tönnis <sup class="natflag">DE</sup> (Dortmund).** Age-stratified acetabular index norms (Tönnis-Brünken 1968, Tönnis-Trede 1970), the four-grade CSHD classification of femoral head position (1978/1982), and the triple pelvic osteotomy for the closed triradiate cartilage. His textbook *Die angeborene Hüftdysplasie und Hüftluxation im Kindes- und Erwachsenenalter* (Springer 1984; English edition 1987, Telger) remains the reference for any operative approach to severe DDH in the older child.

**Arnold Pavlik <sup class="natflag">CZ</sup> (1944, 1957).** The Pavlik harness — the standard for neonatal treatment from 0 to 6 months. The concept of "spontaneous reduction through positioning" (flexion 100–110°, gravity-assisted abduction) transformed congenital hip dislocation from a late-discovered deformity to an early-preventable condition where adequate screening exists.

**Robert Salter <sup class="natflag">CA</sup> (Toronto, 1961).** Innominate osteotomy — the concept of **redirecting** (rather than deepening) the acetabulum around the elastic symphysis pubis and triradiate cartilage. The classical procedure for persistent dysplasia after open reduction in the child between 18 months and 6 years. Salter also introduced the term "**human position**" (flexion 95–100°, abduction 45°) as a biologically safe alternative to the Lorenz "frog-leg" position.

**Reinhold Ganz <sup class="natflag">CH</sup> (Bern, 1988).** Periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) — four osteotomies around the acetabulum freeing a single fragment while preserving the posterior column. Transformed the management of adolescent and adult acetabular dysplasia in the concentric, congruent joint; the standard for avoiding arthroplasty in the appropriate candidate.

**Gavriil A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (Kurgan).** Transpositional osteogenesis with external fixation — applicable to complex late reconstructions of the proximal femur and pelvic ring in neglected and unsuccessfully treated DDH. Distraction osteogenesis for correction of shortening, deformity, and rotational malalignment in multiplanar late reconstructions.

</div>
</details>

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical line — operative surgery of DDH</strong>
  <p>The operative era of DDH begins in the late 19th century with two German-language schools whose debate — bloody versus bloodless reduction — shaped hip surgery for the following century.
