## Overview

<section class="topic-section" id="overview">

Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease (LCPD) is an idiopathic avascular necrosis of the developing femoral head in childhood. The entity was codified by three simultaneous independent reports in 1909–1910 by Arthur Legg in Boston, Jacques Calvé in Berck-sur-Mer, and Georg Perthes in Tübingen, although Henning Waldenström of Stockholm preceded them with the radiographic description of "coxa plana" and remains the author of the staging that bears his name. The disease still occupies a peculiar place in pediatric orthopaedics: the cause is unresolved, the natural history is highly variable, and the treatment remains one of the most-debated topics in pediatric hip surgery. The pathological sequence — necrosis, fragmentation, reossification, and remodeling — unfolds over two to four years and may end in a near-normal spherical femoral head or in a severely deformed, prematurely arthritic joint. Outcome depends on three principal factors: age at onset, extent of head involvement, and whether the softened head is contained within the acetabulum during the biological plasticity window. This chapter follows the classical Catterall monograph (1982), the modern lateral-pillar synthesis of Herring and colleagues (1992; multicenter study 2004), and the Paley/Feldman reconstructive approach (2020), together with the Bulgarian pediatric-orthopaedic tradition.

## Epidemiology and Pathogenesis
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="epidemiology">
<details>
<summary>Epidemiology and Pathogenesis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Risk Factors and Demographics
<details id="risk-factors">
<summary>Risk Factors and Demographics</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Incidence varies markedly with geography and ethnicity: approximately 1 in 1,200 in populations of European descent in the United Kingdom, 1 in 9,000 in African-American populations, and intermediate rates in others. Boys are affected four to five times more often than girls; girls present at a slightly younger age and have a worse prognosis, attributed to earlier skeletal maturity and a shorter remodeling window. The peak age of onset is 4–8 years (range 2–12). Bilateral disease occurs in 10–20% of cases, but synchronous, stage-identical bilateral involvement is rare — when both hips show identical Waldenström stages simultaneously, multiple epiphyseal dysplasia or spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia must be excluded before committing to a Perthes diagnosis.

Etiological associations include low birth weight and small stature; delayed skeletal maturation (typical Perthes children have a bone age one to two years behind chronological age); lower socioeconomic status; second-hand cigarette smoke exposure; and prothrombotic states (factor V Leiden, protein C and protein S deficiencies, antithrombin deficiency, hypofibrinolysis) in a subset of cases — though the role of thrombophilia in the average sporadic case is uncertain. A history of mild trauma is common but is not thought to be causative. The disease is not familial in the great majority of cases; modest familial clustering observed in some series is attributed to shared environmental factors rather than direct heredity.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Bilateral disease occurs in 10–20% of cases, but synchronous, stage-identical bilateral involvement is rare. If both hips show the same stage simultaneously, suspect multiple epiphyseal dysplasia or spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia before committing to a Perthes diagnosis.</p>
</aside>

### Vascular Anatomy of the Developing Femoral Head
<details id="vascular">
<summary>Vascular Anatomy of the Developing Femoral Head</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The fundamental insult in LCPD is a vascular event interrupting the blood supply to the femoral head; the resulting infarction, with subsequent attempts at revascularization, drives the clinical course. The vascular anatomy of the developing hip is critical: between 4 and 9 years of age, the lateral epiphyseal vessels — branches of the medial femoral circumflex artery — are the dominant supply to the femoral head. The contribution of the artery of the ligamentum teres is minimal in this age group, and the metaphyseal vessels are blocked from reaching the epiphysis by the physeal cartilage. This narrow vascular window (when the lateral epiphyseal vessels are essentially the sole supply) corresponds precisely to the age at which LCPD has its peak incidence. Disruption of these vessels — whether by repeated micro-thrombotic events, mechanical compression from an effusion or transient synovitis, or some idiopathic event — produces the avascular insult that initiates the disease.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Between 4 and 9 years the lateral epiphyseal vessels are the sole supply to the femoral head — the ligamentum teres contribution is minimal and the metaphyseal vessels are blocked by the physis. This narrow vascular window matches the LCPD peak incidence exactly.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Waldenström Stages

<summary>Waldenström Stages</summary>

  </figcaption>

The pathological sequence proceeds through four classical stages described by Waldenström (1922, 1934). The initial avascular stage: bone of the femoral head dies but the radiographic appearance is initially normal, followed by apparent increased radiodensity as the surrounding live bone becomes osteopenic by disuse. The fragmentation stage: necrotic bone is gradually resorbed and replaced by ingrowing vascular fibrous tissue, producing the characteristic patchy lytic appearance and the period during which the femoral head is mechanically softest and most vulnerable to deformation. The reossification (repair) stage: new woven bone is laid down on the dead trabecular bone scaffold. The residual (healed) stage: the femoral head reaches its final shape. The entire sequence takes 2–4 years from onset to healing, with the variability depending on age at onset (older children have longer disease courses), extent of head involvement, and effectiveness of treatment. The Elizabethtown classification (Joseph et al., 2003) subdivides the Waldenström sequence further (Ia/b initial, IIa/b fragmentation, IIIa/b reossification, IV residual) to identify the optimal moment for containment surgery — chiefly, in the necrotic stage or the early fragmentation stage, before deformation has progressed.

    <li>The Joseph (Elizabethtown) modification subdivides Waldenström into six stages and identifies the necrotic-to-early-fragmentation window as the optimal moment for containment surgery.</li>

## Clinical Features
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="clinical">
<details>
<summary>Clinical Features</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The presentation is typically of a young child (4–8 years) with intermittent hip, groin, or referred knee pain, an antalgic gait, and a limp that worsens with activity. The pain may be exquisitely mild and easily attributed to growing pains or minor injury; many children present after weeks or months of symptoms when the limp becomes obvious to the family or a teacher. Examination reveals reduced hip abduction and internal rotation — the most consistent finding, with the contralateral hip serving as a normal control — a Trendelenburg gait pattern, mild hip joint tenderness, and slight apparent shortening of the affected limb in some cases. The classical position of the hip in LCPD at rest is flexion, abduction, and external rotation. Nakamura and colleagues (cited in Paley/Feldman 2020) showed that a decrease in abduction range at presentation independently worsens prognosis, even in the youngest patients.

The differential diagnosis includes transient synovitis (resolves within days, in contrast to weeks of symptoms in LCPD), septic arthritis (acute presentation with systemic illness), juvenile inflammatory arthritis, leukemia and lymphoma, sickle cell disease (in appropriate populations), other causes of avascular necrosis (sickle cell crisis, corticosteroid therapy, post-traumatic), and slipped capital femoral epiphysis (in older children).

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>Transient synovitis is self-limiting within days. A limp persisting more than two weeks demands radiographs and an LCPD work-up — do not anchor on the more common benign diagnosis.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Imaging
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="imaging">
<details>
<summary>Imaging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Plain radiographs — AP pelvis and frog-lateral views of both hips — are the standard initial imaging study and demonstrate the characteristic stages of disease. The earliest radiographic finding is a small ossific nucleus (compared with the unaffected side), followed by a thin crescent-shaped subchondral lucency representing subchondral fracture through the necrotic bone (the Caffey sign or crescent sign, best seen on frog-lateral views). As the disease progresses, the epiphysis becomes increasingly dense (the "white femoral head" of the avascular stage), then fragments with patchy lucencies, then progressively reossifies. The metaphysis may show changes (a small lateral metaphyseal cyst is a recognized sign of poor prognosis) and the femoral neck may become wide and short (coxa magna, coxa breva) in the residual stage. Joseph and others have shown that the subchondral fracture is visible in approximately one quarter of cases at presentation.

MRI — particularly perfusion-weighted MRI with gadolinium — has become important for early diagnosis in the pre-radiographic stage and for assessment of the extent of femoral head involvement. The classical MRI signs are: marrow oedema on T2-weighted images in the affected region; loss of normal enhancement on gadolinium-enhanced sequences corresponding to the avascular area; the "double-line" sign on T2-weighted images (a low-signal inner line of necrotic bone with a high-signal outer line of granulation tissue at the reactive interface); and the eventual appearance of the same morphological changes seen on plain radiographs as the disease progresses. Kim and colleagues (2014) showed that perfusion MRI in the necrotic stage can predict the eventual lateral-pillar classification — a tool of growing importance for stratifying children before treatment decisions are required.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>The subchondral crescent (Caffey) sign is best seen on the frog-lateral view, not the AP. AP-only imaging misses early disease.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>
</section>

The classification systems address three distinct questions: extent of femoral head involvement (Catterall, Salter-Thompson), pillar height at the maximum-fragmentation stage (Herring), and morphological outcome at skeletal maturity (Stulberg). They are not interchangeable — they apply to different time-points in the disease and answer different prognostic questions.


### Catterall Groups and Head-at-Risk Signs
<details id="catterall">
<summary>Catterall Groups and Head-at-Risk Signs</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Catterall, working at the Institute of Orthopaedics in London, published the four-group system in 1971 and consolidated it in the 1982 monograph from his prospective London series. The classification is applied during the fragmentation stage, when the necrotic segment is clearly demarcated from the surviving head. Group one: involvement of the anterior head only (no more than 25%), without sequestrum or epiphyseal collapse — universally favourable outcome. Group two: anterior involvement up to 50% with a clear sequestrum and a preserved medial and lateral column. Group three: up to 75% of the head involved, with only small medial and lateral preserved segments — a poor-prognosis group. Group four: 100% of the head involved, with the entire epiphysis sequestered. In the 1970 survey of 388 cases Catterall reported the distribution as approximately 8% group one, 37% group two, 28% group three, and 11% group four (with the remainder bilateral).

The Catterall classification is the source of the "head-at-risk" concept — five radiographic features identified during the active phase that predict poor outcome and provide the operative indication. The Gage sign — described by Courtnay Gage in 1932 — is a curved (V-shaped) radiolucent defect in the lateral epiphysis and adjacent metaphysis, representing involvement of the lateral pillar. Calcification lateral to the epiphysis represents ossification of an enlarged cartilaginous head model and indicates that the head is no longer plastic enough to remodel. Lateral subluxation — Waldenström's original sign of impending deterioration — appears as a break in Shenton's line and widening of the inferomedial joint space and indicates a fixed deformation. A horizontal physis on the AP view, present when the femoral head lies in adduction and external rotation, indicates fixed subluxation. A diffuse metaphyseal cyst (as distinct from a small localised anterior cyst) indicates extensive cartilage extension into the metaphysis with imminent growth disturbance of the femoral neck.

Reliability of the Catterall classification has been criticised: Hardcastle and colleagues (1980) showed only modest inter-observer agreement, improved when groups two and three are pooled. Catterall is now used less frequently as a treatment trigger than Herring, but the head-at-risk signs remain in clinical use as warning signals during the active phase.

  <strong>NB — Five head-at-risk signs (Catterall 1971)</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Gage sign — V-shaped radiolucent defect in the lateral epiphysis and adjacent metaphysis.</li>
    <li>Calcification lateral to the epiphysis — the head is no longer plastic.</li>
    <li>Lateral subluxation — Waldenström's original sign; break in Shenton's line, widened inferomedial joint space.</li>
    <li>Horizontal physis on AP — fixed adduction-external-rotation subluxation.</li>
    <li>Diffuse metaphyseal cyst (not localised anterior cyst) — predicts neck growth disturbance.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Salter-Thompson
<details id="salter-thompson">
<summary>Salter-Thompson</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Salter-Thompson classification (1984), derived in part from Salter's experimental pig model, is a two-group system based on the extent of the subchondral fracture line (the Caffey crescent sign). Group A: the fracture involves less than half of the femoral head — favourable prognosis. Group B: the fracture involves more than half — unfavourable. Of 1,264 hips in the original series, 376 hips were identifiable by the crescent sign, and in all of them the eventual extent of head resorption matched the original crescent extent. The system has the advantage of early predictive value (the crescent appears before the fragmentation stage) and the disadvantage that the sign is identifiable in only a minority of patients at presentation — many do not have a visible crescent, and the sign disappears at follow-up.

</div>
</details>


### Herring Lateral Pillar
<details id="herring">
<summary>Herring Lateral Pillar</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Herring classification (Herring, Neustadt, Williams, Early, Browne — *J Pediatr Orthop* 1992) is the most widely used modern classification in active-phase decision-making. It divides cases according to the height of the lateral pillar of the femoral head — the lateral 15–30% of the head as seen on the AP radiograph — at the early-to-maximum fragmentation stage. Group A: full lateral pillar height, no involvement. Group B: the lateral pillar maintains more than 50% of its original height. Group B/C border (added later): lateral pillar at exactly 50% on a poorly ossified or very narrow base. Group C: less than 50% of original lateral pillar height. The lateral pillar is, in effect, a structural surrogate for the load-bearing capacity of the head during fragmentation; once the lateral pillar collapses, containment is no longer effective.

The Herring multicenter prospective study (2004), enrolling 345 patients across 39 pediatric orthopaedic surgeons, established the prognostic value of the lateral pillar and the modern treatment guidelines. The principal findings, in compressed form: lateral pillar A — uniformly good outcomes regardless of treatment; lateral pillar B with onset younger than 8 years — uniformly good outcomes; lateral pillar B (and B/C border) with onset 8 years or older — significantly better outcomes with operative containment (femoral or pelvic osteotomy) than with nonoperative treatment; lateral pillar C — poor outcomes regardless of treatment, with no improvement from any intervention tested. Reliability is substantially better than Catterall; Herring himself demonstrated near-perfect agreement among six pediatric orthopaedic raters on 20 sets of radiographs.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Herring lateral pillar outperforms Catterall in both inter-observer reliability and clinical decision-making. Group A → universally good outcome; Group B → operative containment beats observation when age &gt;8 years; Group C → poor outcome regardless of treatment.</p>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>Up to a third of children change Herring category over time. Classify in the early-to-maximum fragmentation stage on stabilised radiographs; a single early film can mis-classify a B that will collapse to C. The Herring system also does not apply reliably above the age of 12, when remodeling capacity is exhausted.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Stulberg Outcome
<details id="stulberg">
<summary>Stulberg Outcome</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Stulberg classification (Stulberg, Cooperman, Wallenstein — *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 1981) describes the morphological outcome at skeletal maturity and is the principal predictor of long-term arthritis risk. Class one: completely normal hip. Class two: spherical femoral head fitting within 2 mm of a circle on AP and frog-lateral views, with abnormalities of the femoral neck (coxa magna, coxa breva) or acetabulum (spherical congruency). Class three: ovoid (out of round by more than 2 mm) femoral head with a congruent acetabulum (aspherical congruency). Class four: flat femoral head (at least 1 cm of flattening over the weight-bearing surface) with a congruent flat acetabulum (aspherical congruency). Class five: flat femoral head with an incongruent, normal acetabulum (aspherical incongruency). The clinical correlations established in the original cohort and confirmed in many subsequent series: classes one and two — normal hip into late adulthood; class three — osteoarthritis in middle age; classes four and five — early and progressive arthritis, with class five typically requiring arthroplasty by the fourth decade and class four by the fifth. The Stulberg classification is the principal outcome measure of every modern Perthes treatment trial.

<aside class="callout callout-classical">
  <strong>Classical lineage — classification</strong>
  <p>Waldenström 1922/1934 (staging) → Catterall 1971/1982 (four-group system, head-at-risk) → Salter-Thompson 1984 (subchondral fracture) → Stulberg 1981 (morphological outcome) → Herring 1992 / multicenter study 2004 (lateral pillar). Each generation refined the previous: Catterall established that extent matters, Salter that timing matters, Stulberg that final shape predicts arthritis, Herring that a single load-bearing structural index outperforms global classification.</p>
</aside>

## Treatment
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="treatment">
<details>
<summary>Treatment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Principles and Containment
<details id="principles">
<summary>Principles and Containment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Treatment is guided by two principles: containment of the femoral head within the acetabulum during the period of biological plasticity (so that the head heals in a spherical shape moulded by the round acetabulum), and minimisation of deforming forces on the softened head during the avascular and fragmentation stages. The concept of containment — formulated in the British school by Lloyd-Roberts, Catterall, and Salter in the 1960s–1970s — rests on the observation that the lateral and anterolateral femoral head, the portion most prone to collapse, is best protected when held under the acetabular roof. Containment can be achieved on the femoral side (varus-derotation osteotomy), on the acetabular side (Salter, Pemberton, triple osteotomy, shelf), or by unloading the joint entirely (Paley articulated hip distraction). Modern practice selects among these approaches according to age, lateral pillar classification, and centre experience.

Three principles are universally agreed: (1) hip range of motion — especially abduction and internal rotation — must be preserved at all times, by activity modification, physiotherapy, and where necessary by adductor tenotomy or Petrie casts; (2) the deformation of the femoral head occurs in the fragmentation stage, so any containment must be in place before or at fragmentation onset; (3) once the head has healed in an aspherical shape, containment is no longer useful and the operative question becomes one of impingement, congruency, and salvage.

</div>
</details>


### Observation and Range of Motion
<details id="observation">
<summary>Observation and Range of Motion</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For younger children (less than 6 years) and for older children with Herring group A disease, observation with symptom control is appropriate. Standards: activity modification, restricted weight-bearing as needed, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for pain, physiotherapy to maintain hip range of motion (particularly internal rotation and abduction), and serial radiographic follow-up at 3–6 month intervals through the Waldenström stages. Petrie casts (bilateral long-leg casts with a connecting bar holding the hips in abduction) and adductor tenotomy are used in the more painful or stiffer child to restore motion before any containment decision is made. The Catterall principle — that motion must be regained before containment — remains valid: a stiff, adducted hip cannot be operatively contained without producing iatrogenic hinge abduction.

</div>
</details>


### Nonoperative Containment
<details id="nonop-containment">
<summary>Nonoperative Containment</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

For the older child with Herring group B disease and substantial head involvement, nonoperative containment with an abduction brace — the Atlanta brace or Scottish-Rite brace are the principal historical examples — holds the femoral head in abduction so that the spherical portion is contained within the acetabulum. The brace was worn continuously, the child's activity was restricted, and treatment lasted months to years. The Herring multicenter study (2004) found no benefit of bracing over the no-treatment or range-of-motion groups, and modern practice has largely abandoned prolonged bracing. The role for nonoperative containment is now restricted to (1) the child whose family declines surgery, (2) the period of pre-containment range-of-motion recovery, and (3) the postoperative period after Paley distraction, where a bilateral abduction brace is used at night for six months.

</div>
</details>


### Femoral Varus-Derotation Osteotomy
<details id="femoral-osteotomy">
<summary>Femoral Varus-Derotation Osteotomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Femoral varus-derotation osteotomy — the operation popularised by Axer (1965, with updates 1980), Lloyd-Roberts and Catterall (1976), and Joseph (2003) — is the standard femoral-side containment procedure. The operation is performed in the proximal subtrochanteric region by removing a lateral-based wedge, producing 15–25° of varus and 15–30° of derotation, so that the previously uncovered anterolateral portion of the femoral head is brought under the acetabular roof. Fixation is with a paediatric blade plate or paediatric DCP. The cost is permanent shortening of the affected limb (typically 1–2 cm) and a slight Trendelenburg gait from greater-trochanter overgrowth, both generally well tolerated.

Joseph and colleagues (2003) studied 97 patients to identify the optimal timing for containment surgery: the chance of retaining a spherical femoral head was much higher in children operated during the avascular stage or the early part of the fragmentation stage than in those operated later. The interpretation — that surgery should "bypass" the deformation of the fragmentation stage — is the conceptual basis for modern timing recommendations. Joseph also introduced the combination of femoral varus-derotation osteotomy with greater-trochanter epiphyseodesis to reduce trochanteric overgrowth: in his series 62.5% of these patients reached Stulberg I or II compared to 20% in controls.

Friedlander's retrospective review of 116 patients (varus osteotomy, mean follow-up 6 years 9 months) reported Stulberg I or II outcomes in 86% of patients younger than 9 with Herring B disease, in 67% of patients aged 9 or older with Herring B, in 43% of patients younger than 9 with Herring C, and in only 30% of those aged 9 or older with Herring C — confirming both the age cut-off and the lateral pillar prognosis.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Timing matters: containment in the necrotic or early fragmentation stage produces dramatically better outcomes than containment after head deformation has begun.</li>
    <li>Greater-trochanter epiphyseodesis combined with varus osteotomy reduces postoperative Trendelenburg gait from compensatory trochanteric overgrowth.</li>
    <li>Over- or under-varusation does not improve outcome; Kim's multicenter prospective work shows that 10–15° of varus is sufficient in lateral pillar B.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Pelvic Osteotomy and Shelf
<details id="pelvic-osteotomy">
<summary>Pelvic Osteotomy and Shelf</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The Salter innominate osteotomy — developed by Robert Salter in Toronto in the 1960s for developmental dysplasia and adapted to LCPD by Lloyd-Roberts and others — redirects the acetabulum to cover the anterolateral femoral head, achieving containment from the acetabular side rather than the femoral side. The acetabulum is rotated forward, downward, and laterally on the symphyseal hinge, increasing anterolateral coverage without changing femoral mechanics. The procedure can be combined with femoral osteotomy or used alone, particularly when the acetabulum is dysplastic. The Herring multicenter study showed no difference in Stulberg outcomes between Salter and femoral varus-derotation osteotomy — the surgeon may choose either, and the choice is now usually driven by limb-length and Trendelenburg considerations (Salter avoids both at the cost of a more technically demanding pelvic osteotomy).

The Pemberton osteotomy (an incomplete acetabuloplasty hinging at the triradiate cartilage) and the triple innominate (Steel, 1973) extend the technique to more severe acetabular dysplasia. The shelf acetabuloplasty — Spitzy's original procedure adapted by Staheli and others — provides a buttress of bone over the laterally uncovered femoral head; in current practice it is used as a salvage procedure when the head is too deformed for redirection or when standard containment is not feasible. Comparisons by Moberg (1997) and Kitakoji (2005) of femoral versus pelvic osteotomy show similar Stulberg outcomes with a more anatomic proximal femur and better cosmesis on the pelvic side, leading both authors to recommend innominate osteotomy where the deformity allows it.

</div>
</details>


### Paley Articulated Hip Distraction
<details id="paley-distraction">
<summary>Paley Articulated Hip Distraction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In 1989 Dror Paley introduced hip-joint distraction with an articulated external fixator as a treatment for Perthes disease — the conceptual basis being that the cartilaginous epiphysis actively proliferates into the unloaded, uncovered regions of the extruded head, and that mechanical unloading combined with maintained motion can restore both sphericity and range. Bed rest and non-weight-bearing fail to unload the hip because muscular forces apply one to two times body weight even without ground reaction; only joint distraction with an articulated frame neutralises these forces while preserving motion. Arthrodiastasis had been used previously for chondrolysis, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, and post-traumatic stiffness; Paley extended the technique to LCPD in the resorption phase.

The operative protocol (Feldman and Paley, 2020): supine positioning; arthrogram of the hip joint; a centre-of-rotation Steinmann pin placed superior to the centre of the head so that after distraction it ends in the centre of the acetabulum. The cannulated hinge of a unilateral rail (Orthofix, Biomet, modular rail system, or Devise Drive Rail) is mounted on this pin. Pelvic pins are inserted at an oblique angle to abduct the hip; femoral pins fix the rail parallel to the femoral shaft. The hip is acutely distracted, the centre-of-rotation pin removed and reinserted to confirm position in the acetabulum. Distraction proceeds at 0.5 mm/day until Shenton's line is over-reduced; the fixator remains in place for 4 months in children younger than 12 years and 5 months in older children. Physiotherapy is restricted to hip flexion-extension only (abduction-adduction and rotation are forbidden, to spare the pin-bone interface). After fixator removal a bilateral abduction brace at 30° is applied for six weeks full-time and then six months at night.

Outcomes in Paley and Segev's first 16 consecutive patients (18 hips, 1989–1999, mean age 10.2 years, mean follow-up 6.7 years) — patients selected for whole-head involvement (Catterall IV), marked proximal migration, and subluxation, i.e. patients who would be expected to do poorly with conventional containment: Stulberg I in 1, Stulberg II in 5, Stulberg III in 8, Stulberg IV in 4 hips. The radiographic sphericity index improved from 1.29 to 1.17 (p = 0.001); 15 of 16 patients were pain-free at follow-up; 16 of 18 hips regained at least two-thirds of normal range of motion. Pin-tract infections were common but minor; one femoral neck fracture occurred on the day of fixator removal.

The conceptual position of distraction in the Paley algorithm: it is the preferred treatment for whole-head involvement (Catterall IV, Herring C) during the resorption phase with subluxation and collapse, especially in older children (over 8 years) for whom conventional containment performs poorly. It is not the definitive treatment to prevent late osteoarthritis; once the head has healed in an aspherical shape, FAI develops and must be addressed by hip arthroscopy, surgical dislocation with osteochondroplasty, or femoral head reduction osteotomy.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Bed rest does not unload the hip — muscular force alone exceeds body weight. Only articulated distraction neutralises joint compression while preserving motion. This is the conceptual basis of arthrodiastasis as the operative answer to the late-presenting, whole-head Perthes hip.</p>
</aside>

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Distraction applied immediately after subchondral fracture (before resorption is established) tends to fail with re-collapse after fixator removal; the optimal timing is during the resorption phase, ideally just before head collapse.</li>
    <li>Forbid abduction-adduction and rotation exercises during fixator wear — these stress the pin-bone interface and risk pin loosening or femoral neck fracture.</li>
    <li>Manipulation of the hip after fixator removal is forbidden — the osteoporotic femoral head and neck can fracture; full weight-bearing is resumed gradually over 4 weeks.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Salvage and Late Reconstruction
<details id="salvage">
<summary>Salvage and Late Reconstruction</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Once the femoral head has healed in a deformed shape (Stulberg III, IV, or V) and symptoms develop in the older child or adolescent, salvage procedures address the resulting impingement, instability, or arthritis. Valgus-extension proximal femoral osteotomy redirects the deformed head out of the impingement zone, presenting a more spherical portion of the head to the acetabulum; the technique is particularly suited to the "hinge abduction" deformity. Femoral head-neck offset correction by surgical dislocation (Ganz technique) or by hip arthroscopy addresses the cam impingement that develops from the aspherical head. Periacetabular osteotomy (Ganz, 1988) addresses residual acetabular dysplasia that develops because the deformed femoral head fails to mould a normal acetabulum.

The Ganz femoral head reduction osteotomy — described by Reinhold Ganz and refined by Paley and others for late-presenting Perthes deformity — addresses the extreme case of coxa magna with non-spherical head: through a surgical dislocation approach, a central or anterolateral segment of the cartilaginous, partially dead head is resected and the remaining viable portions are reapproximated to produce a smaller, more spherical head. The technique requires extensive preoperative imaging (CT, MRI) to delineate the viable and necrotic zones, and meticulous preservation of the medial femoral circumflex artery. Paley's reconstructive series in the 2020 monograph extend the technique to combined head reduction with simultaneous relative neck lengthening, greater-trochanter transfer, and articulated distraction. Hip arthroscopy plays an increasing role for impingement and labral pathology in the residual Perthes hip. Total hip arthroplasty is the definitive late treatment for the patient with established secondary arthritis, but it should be deferred as long as possible because of the typically young patient age.

</div>
</details>

## Differential Diagnosis
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="differential">
<details>
<summary>Differential Diagnosis</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Although LCPD is by far the commonest cause of avascular necrosis of the femoral head in childhood, several other conditions produce similar pathological and radiographic appearances. Sickle cell disease is the commonest cause of multifocal avascular necrosis in children of African or Mediterranean ancestry; sickling-induced microvascular occlusion precipitates avascular events, often during sickle cell crisis. Multiple joints can be affected, with femoral and humeral heads most commonly involved. Treatment combines optimization of the underlying disease (hydroxyurea, chronic transfusion in selected cases), pain management, and joint-preserving surgery (core decompression, vascularised bone grafting) or arthroplasty in advanced disease.

Corticosteroid-induced avascular necrosis occurs in children receiving high-dose corticosteroids for inflammatory disease, transplant immunosuppression, or chemotherapy. The mechanism involves direct cytotoxicity to osteocytes, fat-cell hypertrophy with increased intramedullary pressure, and disturbances in clotting predisposing to microvascular thrombosis. The pattern of multifocal disease, the corticosteroid history, and typical involvement in adolescence or young adulthood distinguish this entity from LCPD. Post-traumatic AVN follows hip dislocation, femoral neck fracture, or septic arthritis; the antecedent traumatic or septic event and progressive hip symptoms identify the diagnosis.

Meyer's dysplasia is a benign condition of delayed and disorganised ossification of the femoral head in young children, often confused with early LCPD. The ossific nucleus is small and irregular but the head is not avascular and the condition resolves spontaneously without treatment. Bilateral synchronous involvement in a very young child without symptoms is the typical presentation, and the radiographic appearance can be reassuringly different from LCPD on careful inspection. Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia are inherited skeletal dysplasias producing bilateral synchronous abnormalities of the femoral head and other epiphyses — short stature, family history, and involvement of other epiphyses distinguish them. Hypothyroidism produces a similar bilateral epiphyseal dysgenesis with delayed bone age. Gaucher disease produces avascular necrosis of the femoral head in adolescence and young adulthood through marrow infiltration by glucocerebroside-laden macrophages.

<aside class="callout callout-pitfall">
  <span class="callout-label">Pitfall</span>
  <p>Bilateral, synchronous, stage-identical disease is the pattern of skeletal dysplasia (MED, SED) or hypothyroidism — not classical Perthes. A skeletal survey, bone age, and thyroid function studies before treatment.</p>
</aside>

</div>
</details>
</section>



## Outcomes and Long-Term Considerations
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="outcomes">
<details>
<summary>Outcomes and Long-Term Considerations</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Long-term outcome depends primarily on age at onset (younger is better), extent of femoral head involvement (Herring), and achievement of containment during the biological plasticity period. Stulberg I and II outcomes are achievable in the majority of children under 8 years and in those with Herring group A disease; these patients have hips that function well into late adulthood without premature arthritis. Stulberg III (aspherical, congruent) predicts arthritis in middle age and is common in children with later onset or more extensive head involvement. Stulberg IV and V (aspherical, incongruent) are associated with early arthritis, often requiring arthroplasty by the fifth decade for class IV and by the fourth for class V.

The classical sequelae of inadequately treated LCPD include: coxa magna (enlarged femoral head), coxa breva (short femoral neck with relative trochanteric overgrowth), coxa plana (flattened head), femoroacetabular impingement (cam-type, from the aspherical head), secondary acetabular dysplasia (from the deformed head's effect on acetabular development), and limb-length discrepancy. Each may be addressed by appropriate adolescent or adult reconstruction. The patient's age at presentation remains the single most reliable predictor of outcome — Wiig and colleagues showed in a Norwegian register-based study that children younger than 6 years have favourable outcomes regardless of treatment, while Herring and colleagues showed the cut-off to be 8 years for the modern lateral pillar B group.

<aside class="callout callout-pearl">
  <span class="callout-label">Pearl</span>
  <p>Stulberg at skeletal maturity predicts the timeline: I–II → normal hip into late adulthood; III → arthritis in middle age; IV → arthroplasty by the fifth decade; V → arthroplasty by the fourth.</p>
</aside>

</div>

## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Anthony Catterall <sup class="natflag">UK</sup>.** Consultant at the Royal National Orthopaedic Hospital and Charing Cross Hospital, London; described the four-group radiographic classification (1971) and the five head-at-risk signs that defined the active-phase prognostic model for two generations. His 1982 monograph *Legg-Calvé-Perthes' Disease* (Churchill Livingstone, Current Problems in Orthopaedics series) remains the principal English-language reference on natural history and morphological pathology.

**John A. Herring <sup class="natflag">US</sup>.** Texas Scottish Rite Hospital for Children, Dallas; described the lateral pillar classification (1992) and led the multicenter prospective Legg-Perthes study (2004) — the data on which modern treatment recommendations rest. Editor of *Tachdjian's Pediatric Orthopaedics*.

**Dror Paley <sup class="natflag">US</sup>.** Paley Orthopedic and Spine Institute, West Palm Beach; introduced articulated hip distraction (arthrodiastasis) for Perthes in 1989 and developed the reconstructive approach to late Perthes deformity — femoral head reduction osteotomy with relative neck lengthening and greater-trochanter transfer. Co-editor with David Feldman of *Legg-Calvé-Perthes Disease — A Comprehensive Clinical Guide* (Springer, 2020), the standard modern operative reference.

**Reinhold Ganz <sup class="natflag">CH</sup>.** Bern; developed the surgical hip dislocation approach with preservation of the medial femoral circumflex artery and the periacetabular osteotomy (1988) — both central techniques in modern reconstruction of the residual Perthes deformity. The Ganz approach is the foundation of the femoral head reduction osteotomy.

**Robert Salter <sup class="natflag">CA</sup>.** Toronto; described the innominate osteotomy (1961) for developmental dysplasia of the hip, later adapted to LCPD as the acetabular-side containment procedure. The pig experimental model of LCPD with Thompson produced the Salter-Thompson subchondral-fracture classification (1984).

**Henning Waldenström <sup class="natflag">SE</sup> (1877–1972).** Stockholm; described "coxa plana" (1909–1922) and the four-stage radiographic evolution of the disease that bears his name and remains in use a century later. The original observation of lateral subluxation as a sign of impending head deformation is also his (1934).

**Gavriil A. Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1921–1992).** Kurgan; transosseous osteosynthesis with the circular external fixator. Although the Ilizarov apparatus is not the direct ancestor of the Paley articulated hip distractor, the principle — that gradual, controlled mechanical unloading and distraction at a joint surface can drive biological regeneration — is the Ilizarov insight.

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the Bulgarian orthopaedic school; the *Operative Orthopaedics and Traumatology* (Sofia) remains a curricular reference; the chapters on the pediatric hip cover LCPD in the classical East-European framework. The eponymous University Hospital of Orthopaedics "Prof. Boycho Boychev" in Gorna Banya continues the Bulgarian pediatric-hip programme.
