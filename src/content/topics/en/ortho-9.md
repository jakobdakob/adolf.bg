## Overview
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="overview">
<details open>
<summary>Overview</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Avascular necrosis (AVN), also called osteonecrosis, is the death of bone tissue following interruption of its blood supply, followed by an attempt at revascularization, subchondral fracture, and ultimately articular collapse. In adults it is a major cause of progressive joint destruction and one of the leading indications for early arthroplasty: roughly 20,000–30,000 new cases of femoral head osteonecrosis are diagnosed annually in the United States, and the condition accounts for about 10% of all primary total hip arthroplasties (Mont et al., in *The Adult Hip*, ch. 37). The disease is bilateral in 50–80% of cases, often with markedly asynchronous involvement of the two joints.

The topic treats adult AVN as a pathophysiologically unified entity with different clinical manifestations by anatomical site. The dominant manifestation is AVN of the femoral head — in the older English literature this is sometimes called *Chandler's disease* for the idiopathic forms (F. A. Chandler, *AAOS Instructional Course Lectures*, 1948). The second major site is AVN of the lunate — *Kienböck's disease*, described by the Viennese radiologist Robert Kienböck in 1910 as "traumatic malacia of the lunate." Additional sites of practical importance include AVN of the humeral head, the medial femoral condyle (spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee, SONK or Ahlbäck's disease), the talus (with Hawkins' prognostic sign), the scaphoid (Preiser's disease), and the metatarsal heads (Freiberg's).

The logic of treatment is almost identical across sites: early MRI diagnosis in the pre-collapse phase, an attempt at joint preservation through unloading, revascularization (vascularized bone graft) and/or load redistribution (osteotomy, joint leveling), and, once collapse has occurred, arthrodesis or joint replacement. The classification systems (Steinberg, Ficat–Arlet, ARCO for the femoral head; Lichtman, Schmitt, Bain for Kienböck) serve to stratify risk and to standardize comparisons across series.

The topic synthesizes content from *Osteonecrosis* (Koo, Mont, Jones, eds., Springer 2014), *Kienböck's Disease: Advances in Diagnosis and Treatment* (Lichtman, Bain, eds., Springer 2016), and *The Adult Hip*, 3rd edition, ch. 37 (Mont et al., Callaghan, Rosenberg, Rubash, eds., Lippincott 2015), supplemented by classical Bulgarian and Russian contributions.

## Pathophysiology and Etiology
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="pathophysiology">
<details>
<summary>Pathophysiology and Etiology</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Vascular mechanisms
<details id="vascular-mechanisms">
<summary>Vascular mechanisms</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The fundamental mechanism in AVN is disruption of blood supply to a region of bone, followed by ischemic death of osteocytes and marrow elements. Mont and colleagues (*The Adult Hip*, ch. 37) distinguish direct from indirect risk factors: direct factors injure vascular channels or cells outright (trauma, hemoglobinopathies, radiation, chemotherapy, dysbarism), while indirect factors act through more complex pathophysiologic pathways (corticosteroids, alcohol, tobacco, lupus, myeloproliferative disorders, renal failure, coagulopathies, organ transplantation, pregnancy, genetic factors).

In synthesis, five general mechanisms can produce the initial vascular insult: mechanical interruption of vascular supply by trauma, dislocation, or surgical injury (femoral neck fracture, posterior hip dislocation, iatrogenic disruption of the retinacular branches of the medial femoral circumflex artery); intravascular occlusion by emboli, fat droplets, sickled erythrocytes, thrombi, or gas bubbles in decompression sickness; extraluminal compression by raised intramedullary pressure from marrow infiltration (Gaucher's disease, leukemia), fat-cell hypertrophy (steroid- or alcohol-induced disease), or hemorrhage; immunologic and inflammatory injury to vessel walls (systemic *lupus erythematosus*, vasculitis, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome); and idiopathic or multifactorial events with suspected involvement of thrombophilia (factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, protein C and S deficiencies), hypofibrinolysis, and hypercholesterolemia.

</div>
</details>


### Risk factors
<details id="risk-factors">
<summary>Risk factors</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Corticosteroid therapy.** The most common non-traumatic cause. The threshold of risk is widely quoted as a cumulative prednisone equivalent of approximately 2 g or a daily dose greater than 30 mg of prednisone for more than 30 days (Mont et al.). The presumed mechanism is fat-cell hypertrophy raising intramedullary pressure, suppression of osteogenesis, and reduced angiogenesis through downregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Steroid use in solid-organ transplantation, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and autoimmune disease (especially systemic lupus) makes steroid-associated AVN one of the major iatrogenic diseases of twentieth-century medicine.

**Alcohol abuse.** The second most common cause. Risk rises with cumulative intake — over about 300 g of alcohol per day (about a liter of wine or four large beers daily) is associated with substantially elevated risk. The mechanism is fat-cell hypertrophy and alterations in lipid metabolism.

**Trauma.** Displaced femoral neck fracture carries a 10–15% risk of AVN with anatomic reduction and prompt fixation, rising to 30% or more with delayed fixation or inadequate reduction. Posterior hip dislocation carries a 5–15% risk with reduction within 6 hours and a substantially higher risk with later reduction.

**Hemoglobinopathies.** Sickle cell disease produces multifocal AVN through sickling-induced microvascular occlusion, particularly in patients of African or Mediterranean descent. Thalassemia is also associated with increased risk.

**Dysbarism.** Decompression sickness ("caisson disease") in deep-sea divers and tunnel workers — nitrogen bubbles producing an intraosseous compartment syndrome.

**Radiation therapy.** Pelvic radiotherapy can produce delayed AVN through direct endothelial and microcirculatory injury.

**Pregnancy.** Third trimester and early postpartum; resolves spontaneously in many cases.

**HIV infection.** Including through antiretroviral therapy — a risk factor for AVN and for poor joint outcomes (29% periprosthetic infection at 10 years in HIV-positive arthroplasty patients, Mont et al.).

**Idiopathic ("Chandler") form.** Roughly 10–20% of cases; modern molecular studies have implicated thrombophilia and hypofibrinolysis in many of these.

    <li>Femoral head AVN is bilateral in 50–100% of series, more than 80% in the larger ones — mandatory MRI evaluation of the contralateral hip.</li>
    <li>In roughly 10% of patients, three or more joints are affected (multifocal AVN) — targeted history of other joint symptoms is essential.</li>
    <li>Displaced femoral neck fracture: 10–15% AVN risk with anatomic prompt fixation, 30%+ with delay; posterior hip dislocation: 5–15% if reduced within 6 hours.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

### Pathologic evolution and the crescent sign
<details id="pathologic-evolution">
<summary>Pathologic evolution and the crescent sign</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The pathologic evolution follows a stereotyped sequence regardless of anatomical site: an immediate ischemic phase with autolysis of osteocytes within 14–21 days of the vascular event; an inflammatory phase with invasion of vascular granulation tissue and primitive mesenchymal cells from surrounding viable bone; a reparative phase with deposition of new woven bone on the dead trabecular scaffold (Phemister's "creeping substitution," 1930); and finally either healing or subchondral fracture of the weakened bone with secondary collapse of the articular surface. This last event — articular collapse — marks the transition from a potentially reversible pre-collapse disease to an established, progressive arthritis.

Histologically, Arlet and Durroux (1973) distinguish four lesion types: type 1 (disappearance of the hematopoietic marrow, separation of lipocytes by edema or hemorrhage, foam cells — not diagnostic by itself); type 2 (necrosis of the fatty marrow with an eosinophilic reticular background, oil cysts, and necrosis of the hematopoietic marrow of granular type); type 3 (complete medullary and trabecular necrosis); type 4 (complete necrosis with dense medullary fibrosis and new bone laid down in apposition to dead trabeculae). Progression is interpreted as successive circulatory (type 1), medullary (type 2), and osseous (types 3–4) phases; correlation with radiographic stage is weak.

**The crescent sign.** A thin subchondral radiolucent line representing a subchondral fracture through the necrotic bone, best seen on the frog-lateral view. It marks the moment of irreversible transition — pre-collapse disease with potential for joint preservation versus post-collapse disease with a progressive mechanical deficit. This anatomical phenomenon is the dividing line in every modern classification system of femoral head AVN (Ficat–Arlet III; Steinberg III; ARCO 3).

## Imaging and Classification
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="imaging-staging">
<details>
<summary>Imaging and Classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### MRI protocol and the double-line sign
<details id="mri-protocol">
<summary>MRI protocol and the double-line sign</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

MRI is the imaging modality of choice for early diagnosis and staging of AVN. Sensitivity and specificity are on the order of 99% (Mont et al.). Plain radiographs are normal in early disease and become abnormal only after several months of pathological evolution; relying on them to rule out AVN leads to delayed diagnosis.

**Characteristic MRI findings of femoral head AVN.** Focal subchondral marrow edema on T2-weighted and STIR images in early disease. The pathognomonic *double-line sign* on T2-weighted images — a low-signal inner line of necrotic bone with a high-signal outer line of granulation tissue at the reactive interface. Loss of normal enhancement on gadolinium-enhanced sequences corresponds to the avascular zone; persistent enhancement may indicate partially preserved viability and predict a better response to revascularization. The subchondral fracture line and articular collapse are later findings.

CT is a supplementary modality for assessment of subchondral collapse (detecting depression below 2 mm, inaccessible to plain radiography) and for surgical planning; bone scintigraphy is less sensitive (misses more than 20% of lesions) and is not first-line. Invasive methods (intraosseous pressure measurement, Ficat's core biopsy) have largely been abandoned since MRI became available.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>MRI is the only modality that can establish the diagnosis before radiographic changes appear; a symptomatic patient with a normal radiograph must have MRI.</li>
    <li>The T2 *double-line sign* is highly specific for AVN; complete absence of gadolinium enhancement in the zone correlates with a poor response to revascularization.</li>
    <li>Scintigraphy and CT are not first-line for early diagnosis.</li>
    <li>The contralateral hip must be imaged even when asymptomatic (bilaterality 50–100%).</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### Ficat and Arlet staging
<details id="ficat">
<summary>Ficat and Arlet staging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The classical system, introduced by Robert P. Ficat and Jean Arlet in Toulouse in the late 1970s and formally published in *J Bone Joint Surg Br* in 1985 (Ficat RP, *Idiopathic bone necrosis of the femoral head. Early diagnosis and treatment*, 1985;67:3–9). Based on two conceptual principles: (1) every AVN passes through a pre-radiographic stage that can be diagnosed only by functional bone investigation (now replaced by MRI); (2) osteonecrosis is the end result of severe and prolonged ischemia, undetectable by routine radiography.

The system divides the disease into five stages:

Stage 0 — clinically silent, radiograph normal; Ficat described this stage as a suspicion in the contralateral hip when AVN is established on the other side (Hungerford's "silent hip").

Stage I — clinically symptomatic with pain, radiograph normal; diagnosis only through functional bone exploration (historically) or MRI (now).

Stage II — radiographic changes without collapse: curvilinear sclerosis around the necrotic zone, small cystic decalcifications within the necrotic portion, the head still spherical.

Transition II–III — the *crescent sign* (subchondral fracture line) and segmental flattening of the femoral head producing an "out-of-round" appearance.

Stage III — the pathognomonic sequestrum on the radiograph; collapse of the sequestered region into the femoral head; joint space preserved.

Stage IV — terminal phase with progressive loss of articular cartilage and development of acetabular osteophytes; the radiographic picture is osteoarthritis superimposed on a deformed femoral head.

The strength of the system is its simplicity and wide clinical use; the limitation is that it lacks a quantitative assessment of lesion size — a critical prognostic factor that the newer systems (Steinberg, ARCO) incorporate.

</div>
</details>


### Steinberg (University of Pennsylvania) staging
<details id="steinberg">
<summary>Steinberg (University of Pennsylvania) staging</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Developed by David R. Steinberg and Marvin E. Steinberg in Philadelphia, presented at the Third International Symposium on Circulation in Bone in Toulouse in 1982 and published in 1984 (Steinberg ME, Hayken GD, Steinberg DR, *A quantitative system for staging avascular necrosis*, *J Bone Joint Surg Br* 1995;77:34–41). The system was the first to incorporate MRI and to quantify lesion size — two parameters the modern consensus regards as essential for any classification. Endorsed by ARCO in 1991 (with later modifications).

Seven stages by pathologic and radiographic findings, each subdivided as A (mild, less than 15% of the head), B (moderate, 15–30%), and C (severe, more than 30%), except for stages 0 and VI:

Stage 0 — normal or non-diagnostic imaging; AVN suspected (typical example: a symptomatic hip contralateral to a confirmed AVN).

Stage I — normal radiograph; abnormal bone scan and/or MRI. Subclassified A/B/C by lesion volume measured on MRI slices.

Stage II — radiographic lucencies and/or sclerosis, sometimes demarcated by a sclerotic rim; rarely, generalized osteopenia of the head. Subclassified A/B/C.

Stage III — subchondral collapse (*crescent sign*) without flattening of the head; collapse of cancellous trabeculae beneath an intact articular surface. Subclassified A/B/C by the length of the crescent line as a percentage of the articular surface.

Stage IV — flattening of the femoral head. Subclassified A/B/C by area and depth of depression: A — less than 15% of the surface and less than 2 mm depression; B — 15–30% of the surface or 2–4 mm depression; C — more than 30% or more than 4 mm. The acetabulum is radiographically normal at this stage, but early acetabular cartilage changes are present in most patients reaching arthroplasty.

Stage V — flattening plus joint-space narrowing and sclerotic-cystic changes in the acetabulum. Subclassified A/B/C by the average of femoral head and estimated acetabular involvement.

Stage VI — advanced degenerative changes with a virtually obliterated joint line and marked deformity.

Steinberg and Steinberg emphasize that most lesions involve the anterolateral aspect of the head, and that attempts to classify by location (medial, central, lateral, as in the Japanese JIC) do not accurately reflect the pathophysiology.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Steinberg quantitative size: A < 15%, B 15–30%, C > 30% of the head; prognostically, lesion size is an independent factor more powerful than stage.</li>
    <li>Stage III (before flattening) is the critical threshold for joint-preserving surgery — potentially reversible while the head is still spherical.</li>
    <li>Stage IV is subdivided A/B/C by combined area and depth of depression — the granularity is absent from older systems.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

</div>
</details>


### ARCO consensus and the JIC classification
<details id="arco-jic">
<summary>ARCO consensus and the JIC classification</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**ARCO (Association Research Circulation Osseous).** An international consensus system formalized in 1991, modified in 1992 and 1993, and adapted again in 2019. ARCO integrates Ficat–Arlet and Steinberg elements: stages 0 (histologic confirmation without imaging findings), 1 (normal radiograph, abnormal MRI), 2 (radiographic changes without collapse), 3 (subchondral fracture — crescent sign), 4 (head flattening), and 5–6 (arthritic and late stages in the older version; consolidated or modified in the newer ones). For each stage, location (medial, central, lateral) and quantitative size in the Steinberg style are recorded.

**JIC (Japanese Investigation Committee).** A classification that divides lesions into type A (medial location, less than 1/3 of the weight-bearing surface), type B (central), type C1 (lateral with the lesion confined within the acetabular rim), and type C2 (lateral with the lesion extending past the acetabular rim) — the last carrying the highest risk of collapse. JIC is popular in Japan and Asian literature, less used in Western centers.

**Kerboul (combined necrotic angle).** Mont and colleagues (*The Adult Hip*) emphasize the practical value of the Kerboul angle as a prognostic measure: the angle of femoral head involvement on the AP radiograph plus that on the lateral, summed. Small lesions — combined angle below 160°; medium — 160–199°; large — 200° or above. A size above 250° is associated with 23% survivorship after core decompression at 12 years versus 57% below 250° (Mont et al.) — lesion size is a more powerful prognostic factor than stage. The MRI modification by Ha and colleagues (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2006;88:35–40) is more sensitive for predicting collapse.

</div>
</details>


### Kienböck staging: Lichtman, Schmitt, Bain
<details id="lichtman">
<summary>Kienböck staging: Lichtman, Schmitt, Bain</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Lichtman classification (radiographic).** Introduced by David Lichtman and colleagues in 1977 (Lichtman DM, Mack GR, MacDonald RI, Gunther SF, Wilson JN, *Kienböck's disease: the role of silicone replacement arthroplasty*, *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 1977;59:899–908), the system has been extended and modified repeatedly and remains the most widely used today:

Stage I — normal radiograph; abnormal MRI (decreased T1 signal) — early ischemic disease.

Stage II — sclerosis of the lunate on plain radiographs with preserved shape and architecture.

Stage IIIA — collapse of the lunate without carpal collapse: normal carpal height, no proximal migration of the capitate, no fixed scaphoid hyperflexion (Goldfarb CA et al., *J Hand Surg Am* 2003 — the IIIA/IIIB subdivision).

Stage IIIB — lunate collapse with carpal collapse: proximal migration of the capitate, decreased carpal height, fixed scaphoid hyperflexion (positive scaphoid ring sign with an increased radioscaphoid angle), and ulnar translation of the carpus.

Stage IIIC — coronal fracture or fragmentation of the lunate, added as a separate stage by Lichtman in 2010 (Lichtman DM et al., *J Hand Surg Eur* 2010 — also adding a hypothetical stage 0 by analogy with ARCO's "silent hip"). With coronal fracture, the prognosis for revascularization is poor.

Stage IV — pancarpal arthritis involving both the radiocarpal and midcarpal joints.

Lichtman and Bain (*Kienböck's Disease*, 2016, ch. 30) present a modern (twenty-first-century) treatment algorithm combining the Lichtman radiographic stage, the Bain arthroscopic chondral assessment, and vascular information from gadolinium-enhanced MRI.

**Schmitt MRI staging.** Schmitt and colleagues (*Eur J Radiol* 1997;25:92–103) describe lunate osteonecrosis staging based on MRI signal and contrast enhancement: A — edema without structural injury (preserved gadolinium-enhanced T1 signal); B — necrosis with partial viability (mosaic pattern of enhancement); C — complete necrosis with complete absence of post-gadolinium enhancement. Schmitt staging adds information not reflected in the purely radiographic Lichtman system and is particularly important when selecting patients for revascularization.

**Bain arthroscopic chondral classification (2006).** Bain and Begg (*Tech Hand Up Extrem Surg* 2006;10:8–13) developed a system based on the number of non-functional articular surfaces around the lunate (by analogy with "knee joint compartment"):

Stage 0 — all four articular surfaces functional.

Stage 1 — one non-functional surface (usually the proximal — between lunate and radius).

Stage 2A — two adjacent non-functional surfaces (proximal + one other).

Stage 2B — two non-adjacent non-functional surfaces.

Stage 3 — three non-functional surfaces.

Stage 4 — four non-functional surfaces (including the capitolunate).

Arthroscopic staging is particularly useful in patients with radiographically advanced disease, where the choice between joint-preserving (revascularization, joint leveling) and salvage procedures (proximal row carpectomy, partial fusion, total arthrodesis) depends on cartilage integrity, which plain radiography cannot reliably show.

<aside class="callout callout-nb">
  <strong>NB</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>The Lichtman IIIA/IIIB subdivision is the work of Goldfarb et al. — the critical difference is fixed scaphoid hyperflexion (IIIB), which renders isolated joint leveling ineffective.</li>
    <li>Lichtman IIIC (coronal lunate fracture) was added in 2010; the prognosis is poor and revascularization rarely succeeds.</li>
    <li>The Bain arthroscopic classification (2006) complements Lichtman in radiographically advanced disease — the choice between joint preservation and salvage depends on cartilage status.</li>
    <li>Schmitt staging with gadolinium is critical before revascularization — complete absence of enhancement (Schmitt C) predicts a poor response.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Chandler's Disease (Femoral Head AVN)
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="chandler">
<details>
<summary>Chandler's Disease (Femoral Head AVN)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Clinical features
<details id="chandler-clinical">
<summary>Clinical features</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Presentation is with insidious onset of groin pain, often referred to the anterior thigh or medial knee (referred dermatome L2–L3). Pain is worsened by activity and by weight-bearing; about 10–15% of patients have atypical trochanteric or buttock pain that can mimic lumbar radiculopathy, and in them a diagnostic intra-articular injection of local anesthetic helps distinguish hip from spine.

Examination in early disease may show only mild restriction of internal rotation. As subchondral collapse and secondary arthritis develop, the typical findings of advanced hip osteoarthritis emerge: marked restriction of internal rotation and abduction, antalgic gait, fixed flexion deformity (positive Thomas test), and shortening of the limb.

</div>
</details>


### Conservative management
<details id="chandler-conservative">
<summary>Conservative management</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In established symptomatic AVN, conservative management produces poor outcomes. A meta-analysis of more than 20 studies (Koo, Mont, Jones) reports 22% success with observation and restricted weight-bearing at a mean follow-up of 34 months, with no significant difference by degree of restriction. In asymptomatic small lesions (especially contralateral ones discovered on screening), observation is acceptable — Jergesen and Khan report onset of pain at less than 5 years in fewer than half of asymptomatic hips. Bisphosphonates (alendronate, zoledronate) reduce bone turnover; early small studies (Nishii) were encouraging, but a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial with 2-year follow-up showed no significant difference in joint survivorship. Statins, anticoagulants, and prostacyclin analogs (iloprost) have been studied with variable results.

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy was examined by Camporesi and colleagues — 20 patients with unilateral Ficat II AVN randomized to 30 consecutive sessions of hyperbaric oxygen versus compressed air; the oxygen arm showed significant pain improvement and lesion healing in 7 of 9 cases. Evidence remains limited and the technique is experimental.

</div>
</details>


### Core decompression
<details id="chandler-core">
<summary>Core decompression</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The standard joint-preserving procedure for pre-collapse disease (Ficat I–II, Steinberg I–II). The procedure involves drilling a single large-diameter (8–10 mm) channel from the lateral cortex of the femur into the necrotic region of the femoral head, or, in the more recent "multiple decompression" technique of Kim and Mont, multiple small-diameter (3–5 mm) channels using the same principle but with less biomechanical weakening of the cortex.

**Mechanism.** Multifactorial: decompression of raised intramedullary pressure; promotion of revascularization through the drilled tract; stimulation of bone formation through the trauma of drilling. Adjunctive biologic agents delivered through the channel include autogenous bone marrow concentrate (BMAC, concentrated mesenchymal stem cells), bone morphogenetic protein (BMP-2, BMP-7), and demineralized bone matrix.

**Results.** Core decompression gives 60–80% success in pre-collapse disease, dropping precipitously once collapse has occurred. Mont and colleagues report 57% survivorship at 12 years in patients with a combined necrotic angle below 250°, versus 23% above 250° — lesion size is a more powerful prognostic factor than stage. Core decompression with adjunctive bone marrow concentrate shows an advantage in small randomized trials, but the definitive evidence is still accumulating.

</div>
</details>


### Vascularized fibular grafting
<details id="chandler-vfg">
<summary>Vascularized fibular grafting</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Free vascularized fibular grafting (FVFG; Bishop, Urbaniak): the contralateral fibula is harvested with its peroneal artery and vein, transferred to the femoral head through a decompression tract, and anastomosed microvascularly to recipient vessels in the thigh (usually a branch of the lateral femoral circumflex artery). The procedure has the dual advantage of providing structural support to the weakened subchondral bone and of bringing in fresh blood supply for revascularization.

Reported success in pre-collapse disease is 70–90%, with substantially better results than core decompression alone in selected series. Berend and colleagues (224 hips with FVFG) report 64.5% overall survivorship at a minimum of 5 years in post-collapse disease, stratified by depth of depression: 69.9% with collapse below 1 mm, 66% at 2–3 mm, 56.6% above 3 mm. Depth of depression remains a predictive factor for the FVFG outcome.

**Disadvantages.** Donor-site morbidity — peroneal nerve injury, dorsiflexor weakness, ankle pain; the technical demands of microvascular surgery (skill, time, resources). Anastomotic thrombosis (5–10%) is a critical complication requiring prompt re-operation.

Vascularized iliac bone grafting (Meyers, Hori, and Japanese modifications) uses a branch of the deep circumflex iliac artery or the superior gluteal artery and is an alternative, particularly in Asian literature. Free vascularized grafting from the medial femoral condyle (Higgins et al.) is used in North America and Europe for smaller lesions.

</div>
</details>


### Transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy
<details id="chandler-osteotomy">
<summary>Transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

The principle of osteotomy in AVN is to redirect the necrotic segment out of the principal weight-bearing region of the acetabulum. Sugioka (1973, 1978) described the transtrochanteric anterior rotational osteotomy — the head is rotated 90° anteriorly about the axis of the femoral neck, so that the necrotic anterosuperior segment swings to a posterior position. The procedure requires preservation of the medial and lateral retinacular branches, otherwise it produces iatrogenic infarction. Sugioka reports 70–80% long-term survivorship in selected Japanese series; Western series report substantially lower results (around 30–50% at 5 years).

Varus-valgus-flexion osteotomy (Wagner; Saito): a simpler intertrochanteric osteotomy that also redirects the necrotic segment. Suitable for smaller lesions. Atsumi's posterior rotational osteotomy (1999) — an alternative posterior rotation for the uncommon cases with posterosuperior involvement.

Mont and colleagues in *The Adult Hip* emphasize that osteotomies remain more popular in Asia than in Western countries, with reported 50–80% success in selected pre-collapse and early post-collapse cases, but with significant technical demands and difficult future conversion to THA owing to the altered anatomy.

</div>
</details>


### Total hip arthroplasty
<details id="chandler-tha">
<summary>Total hip arthroplasty</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

THA is the treatment of choice for post-collapse disease (Steinberg IV and above; Ficat III and IV). The technical considerations are similar to those of THA for primary osteoarthritis, with additional considerations in this typically younger population:

**Bearing choice.** A more durable bearing with lower linear wear is required for long-term survivorship: ceramic-on-ceramic (Al2O3 or ZTA composites) or ceramic-on-highly-cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE). Metal-on-metal bearings (formerly a standard option for young patients) have largely been abandoned because of metal ion problems and adverse local tissue reactions (ALTR, ALVAL). Mont and colleagues recommend avoiding conventional UHMWPE in patients under 60.

**Bone quality.** Long-term corticosteroid therapy produces systemic osteopenia that affects primary fixation — cemented or hybrid strategies are mandatory in selected cases. Alcoholic etiology is often associated with poor general health and elevated risk of postoperative complications.

**Contralateral hip.** Because of 50–80% bilaterality, the contralateral hip must be evaluated with MRI before unilateral THA. With bilateral symptoms, staged or simultaneous bilateral arthroplasty is considered.

**Prior surgery.** About 15% of AVN patients reaching THA have had previous core decompression, FVFG, or osteotomy — the last two distort the anatomy (trochanteric osteotomy, fibular graft in the neck) and can complicate component implantation. Hardware removal (screws from prior neck fracture fixation) may need specific equipment.

**Results.** Modern THA in this population produces excellent functional outcomes with 10–15 year survivorship above 95% in many series. Younger patients (under 50) have a higher long-term revision risk owing to wear and activity, reinforcing the argument for harder bearings.

**Bipolar hemiarthroplasty and resurfacing arthroplasty.** Bipolar hemiarthroplasty was used in selected younger patients with a still-normal acetabulum, but it has largely been supplanted by THA. Resurfacing arthroplasty (Birmingham, Conserve Plus) has appropriate biomechanical logic for young AVN patients with a preserved femoral neck, but results are variable and the metal-on-metal bearing carries known risks.

<aside class="callout callout-cave">
  <strong>Cave</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>HIV-positive patients and those on antiretroviral therapy have a substantially elevated risk of periprosthetic infection (29% at 10 years, Mont et al.) — mandatory perioperative optimization and protective protocols.</li>
    <li>Previous vascularized fibular grafting — the graft may remain in the neck and complicate stem broaching; preoperative CT is required when planning THA.</li>
    <li>Young patient with bilateral AVN — staged THA is the standard; simultaneous bilateral only with specific indication and adequate general health.</li>
    <li>Results in young patients with systemic disease (sickle cell disease, lupus, transplantation) are worse than in idiopathic Chandler-type AVN — realistic informed consent is essential.</li>
  </ul>
</aside>

## Kienböck's Disease (Lunatomalacia)
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="kienbock">
<details>
<summary>Kienböck's Disease (Lunatomalacia)</summary>
<div class="ts-body">


### Pathogenesis and ulnar variance
<details id="kienbock-pathogenesis">
<summary>Pathogenesis and ulnar variance</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

Kienböck's disease is avascular necrosis of the carpal lunate, described by the Viennese radiologist Robert Kienböck in 1910 as "traumatic malacia of the lunate" — initially understood as a posttraumatic injury. In 1920 Müller extended the concept to a combination of softening and densification (*Erweichung und Verdichtung*) and described the spontaneous form as a distinct entity. The disease predominantly affects men aged 20–40 in manual occupations, with the dominant hand involved more often; bilateral disease in 5–10%.

The etiology remains incompletely settled. Bain and Irisarri (*Kienböck's Disease*, 2016, ch. 7) emphasize that multiple biological and mechanical factors contribute, and that no single explanation covers all cases. Bain favors the mechanical-structural theory (AVN as an intraosseous compartment syndrome of the lunate), and Irisarri the biologic (thrombogenic and microvascular). The current synthesis treats the disease as the result of simultaneously acting factors:

**Anatomical vascular vulnerability.** The lunate is supplied by 1, 2, or 3 nutrient vessels entering through palmar and dorsal foramina (Gelberman); with a single nutrient vessel (around 7–8% of the population) the perfusion reserve is minimal. The proximal articular convex surface has a subchondral cortical plate only 0.1 mm thick — Bain (2016) — making it extremely vulnerable to fracture.

**Ulnar variance.** Hultén (1928) described the statistical association between ulnar-negative variance (a relatively shorter ulna compared with the radius) and Kienböck's disease. Mechanism: with negative variance more axial load is transmitted directly through the lunate onto the radius rather than being shared with the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) onto the ulna. Biomechanical studies confirm the association, but not all Kienböck patients have negative variance, and only a minority of negative-variance patients develop the disease — ulnar variance is a risk factor, not a necessary condition.

**Trapezoidal lunate shape.** A type II lunate (with an additional medial articular facet for the hamate) is associated with lower risk — lunate shape correlates with load distribution.

**Microtrauma and single trauma.** Repetitive axial compression in manual labor (hammers, jackhammers) and single dorsiflexion injuries are associated with the disease, but neither is a necessary condition.

**Thrombogenic factors.** In sporadic patients, hypercoagulopathy (factor V Leiden), sickle cell disease, and lupus have been identified — particularly in pediatric and adolescent cases ("teenböck's").

</div>
</details>


### Conservative management
<details id="kienbock-conservative">
<summary>Conservative management</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In Lichtman stage I (MRI-positive, radiograph normal), conservative management with short-term cast immobilization (4–6 weeks), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories, and an occupational review is standard. Some authors recommend continued immobilization for 2–3 months with MRI reassessment.

Stahl (2010) and Keith et al. describe progression of untreated disease: most patients left untreated progress to stage IIIA within 6 months to 2 years; in long-term follow-up (Kristensen 1986, 8–33 years) 33% develop a lunate fracture and 66% develop radiocarpal arthritis. Viljakka and colleagues (*J Hand Surg Eur* 2014; PMID 24300510) followed 9 wrists over a mean of 27.3 years — 5 stage IIIA patients did not progress, but 2 stage IIIA and both stage IIIB patients progressed to stage IV. The conclusion is that observation alone is not an acceptable recommendation for stage III.

In older patients with low functional demands and stage IV pancarpal arthritis, conservative management with NSAIDs, casting during flares, and activity modification may remain the only strategy short of total arthrodesis.

</div>
</details>


### Joint-leveling procedures
<details id="kienbock-leveling">
<summary>Joint-leveling procedures</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In Lichtman stages II and IIIA with ulnar-negative variance, joint-leveling procedures redistribute axial load from the lunate to the ulna and TFCC. This is the working first line in modern Kienböck surgery for the appropriately selected patient.

**Radial shortening osteotomy (RSO).** Preferred in most centers. A transverse osteotomy of the distal radius 5–6 cm proximal to the articular surface, removing a 2–4 mm wedge (depending on the amount of ulnar-negative variance), with rigid plate fixation. Results are durable — Viljakka (2014) with 20–33-year follow-up shows significant pain improvement and stable functional results, despite radiographic stage progression in a minority. Restoration of carpal height and reversal of collapse are not expected; the goal is to halt progression and relieve pain.

**Ulnar lengthening.** An alternative for the same biomechanical effect: a step-cut ulnar osteotomy with bone graft interposition and plate fixation. Less popular than RSO because of greater technical complexity and more uncertain union.

**Radial closing wedge osteotomy.** A closing wedge in the radial metaphysis, without shortening — the goal is reduction of the radial inclination and unloading of the lunate. Described by the Japanese school (Watanabe and colleagues); limited adoption in Western centers.

**Ulnar-positive or ulnar-neutral variance.** In such patients RSO is contraindicated (it would damage the TFCC and produce ulnocarpal abutment). Alternatives:

— *Almquist's capitate shortening (1986).* A transverse osteotomy of the capitate waist removing a 2–4 mm bone wedge, which unloads the lunate from above. Almquist originally described combining capitate shortening with STT arthrodesis for stabilization; newer modifications (Citron, Watson) show that isolated capitate shortening without scaphocapitate arthrodesis is effective and produces less wrist stiffness.

— *Scaphocapitate (SC) fusion.* Transfers load through the midcarpal joint, unloading the lunate. An alternative to capitate shortening at the same indication.

</div>
</details>


### Vascularized bone grafts
<details id="kienbock-vbg">
<summary>Vascularized bone grafts</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In Lichtman stages II and IIIA, revascularization of the lunate with a vascularized bone graft is an alternative or adjunct to joint leveling. The principle is to introduce living bone with intact blood supply into the necrotic lunate to stimulate revascularization and replace the dead trabecular scaffold.

**Fifth-plus-fourth ECA pedicled flap (Mayo Clinic; Bishop and Moran).** The standard dorsal pedicled flap for lunate revascularization. *Green's Operative Hand Surgery*, 6th ed., ch. 53 (Bishop, Shin, Berger, in Wolfe, Hotchkiss, Pederson, Kozin, eds., Elsevier/Churchill 2010) describes the technique precisely: the source of retrograde flow is the **fifth extensor compartmental artery (5th ECA)**, traced distally to its confluence with the 4th ECA at the intercarpal arch; antegrade flow through the confluence supplies the 4th ECA nutrient branches that perforate the dorsal cortex of the distal radius a mean of 11 mm proximal to the joint line. Pedicle length is roughly twice that of either vessel alone — it reaches anywhere in the carpus and is protected from injury during capsulotomy by its ulnar location. Surgical sequence (Mayo): dorsal Lister approach; elevation of a radially based retinacular flap over the fifth compartment; identification of the 5th ECA and tracing proximally to its origin from the posterior division of the anterior interosseous artery; preservation of the distal anastomoses; elevation of a cancellous block over the 4th ECA; ligament-sparing dorsal capsulotomy; curettage of necrotic lunate with preservation of the cartilage shell; gentle expansion of the collapsed lunate to its normal dimensions with a small elevator; vertical insertion of the graft as a proximal-distal strut. No internal fixation when the lunate is intact.

**Mayo series results.** Moran and colleagues (Green's, ch. 53) report 26 Kienböck patients treated with a dorsal radius pedicle graft at a mean 31-month follow-up: 92% pain improvement, 85% satisfactory overall results, grip strength from 50% to 89% of the opposite side, and progressive MRI signs of revascularization over time. A second multi-center series of 26 patients with 28 procedures, mean 6.7 years: MRI-confirmed lunate reconstruction in 37%, disease stabilization in an additional 23%; overall 43% good and excellent, 43% fair, 14% poor. Bishop and Berger (*Kienböck's Disease*, ch. 18) emphasize that the procedure is most effective in stages II and IIIA without advanced chondral defects and in ulnar-positive or -neutral variance (where joint leveling is contraindicated).

**Lunate unloading after revascularization.** The revascularized lunate is mechanically vulnerable in the first weeks — *Green's* ch. 53 recommends mandatory postoperative unloading with an external fixator (6–8 weeks), capitate shortening, limited intercarpal arthrodesis, or temporary STT pinning (K-wires maintained for 3 months with motion initiated at 3 weeks — matching Yajima's and Yasuda's technique reported in Lichtman & Bain). Without unloading, late collapse after revascularization is a real risk.

**Mathoulin's pedicled flap (1+2 ICSRA).** An alternative pedicled flap from the distal radius based on the first and second intercompartmental supraretinacular artery. Suitable for dorsoradial transport.

**Free vascularized graft from the medial femoral condyle (Higgins).** A free microvascular graft — the lunate core is replaced by a vascularized bone block from the medial femoral condyle with the described descending genicular artery pedicle. Higgins and colleagues have published series of complex cases with advanced fragmentation in which local pedicled flaps were not applicable; results are encouraging, but technical complexity and donor-site morbidity (thigh pain, risk of saphenous nerve injury) limit broader adoption.

**Osteochondral free flap.** For advanced disease with lunate fragmentation — reconstruction of the proximal articular surface with an osteochondral graft from the medial femoral condyle hemicortex or a metatarsal base. Ch. 19 of *Kienböck's Disease* describes the Higgins technique; the indication remains experimental.

</div>
</details>


### Limited carpal arthrodeses
<details id="kienbock-limited-fusion">
<summary>Limited carpal arthrodeses</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In Lichtman stage IIIB with fixed scaphoid hyperflexion and carpal collapse, joint-leveling alone is ineffective — a method that either corrects the carpal axis or accepts the permanently altered geometry is needed.

**Scaphotrapeziotrapezoidal (STT) arthrodesis.** Three-bone fusion of the scaphoid, trapezium, and trapezoid, aimed at stabilizing the distal pole of the scaphoid in a position opposite to its hyperflexion — the carpal axis is straightened, the lunate is mechanically unloaded. Described by Watson, later standardized with screw or K-wire fixation. Loss of 30–50% of wrist range of motion; good pain relief in 70–80% of cases.

**Scaphocapitate (SC) arthrodesis.** An alternative to STT — only two bones, less invasive, comparable biomechanics. *Green's* ch. 14 (Surgical Techniques, Bishop, Berger and colleagues) emphasizes one clinically important point in Kienböck: when SC arthrodesis is used to unload the lunate, scaphoid reduction is usually NOT necessary (unlike its use for rotatory scaphoid subluxation), but a dorsally tilted lunate must be corrected to ensure normal alignment of the fused capitate. Decortication of the dorsal 80% of the SC joint with preservation of the palmar 25% maintains normal intercarpal spacing. Fixation: headless compression screws or two 15-mm staples. Results: mean 50–60% of the opposite ROM; flexion-extension arc 62° (Sennwald and Ufenast); grip strength 74–83% of the contralateral hand (Pisano; Sennwald); nonunion rate 13–18% in pooled series (meta-analysis 6–28% at 95% CI). Temporary STT pinning (Yajima, later Yasuda 1998 and Kazuki 2005) is a minimally invasive variant for early disease — only K-wires across the scapho-trapezial joints for temporary stabilization of the scaphoid axis, without formal arthrodesis.

**Radioscapholunate (RSL) arthrodesis.** Indicated for combined radiolunate and radioscaphoid arthrosis with a still-functional midcarpal joint. The lunate is placed in anatomical position and arthrodesed to the radius with a compression screw; with additional distal scaphoid excision (or additional triquetrum resection) residual midcarpal motion is preserved.

**Reverse Graner–Wilhelm (modified Graner) — lunatectomy with progressive capitate lengthening.** A historical alternative for stage IIIB: excision of the lunate and gradual proximal pull of the capitate in a decoupled position under a midcarpal distractor. Rarely used in modern practice, supplanted by PRC or partial arthrodesis.

</div>
</details>


### Salvage procedures
<details id="kienbock-salvage">
<summary>Salvage procedures</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

In Lichtman stage IIIC (coronal lunate fracture), stage IV (pancarpal arthritis), or when less aggressive procedures fail, salvage procedures provide durable pain relief at the cost of functional motion.

**Proximal row carpectomy (PRC).** Excision of the scaphoid, lunate, and triquetrum so that the capitate articulates with the lunate fossa of the radius. Technique — a short dorsal incision, transverse capsulotomy over the proximal row, sequential removal of the three bones with preservation of the palmar radiocarpal ligaments. Preserves about 50–70% of normal motion and 60–80% of grip strength. Long-term follow-up (10–15 years) shows acceptable functional results with proper selection; critical is the state of the proximal articular surface of the capitate — if arthritic, PRC fails.

**Total wrist arthrodesis.** For pancarpal arthritis with a destroyed capitate, after failed PRC, or in patients with high functional demands unsuited to arthroplasty. Capsulotomy, cartilage preparation in the radiocarpal and midcarpal joints, and fixation with a dorsal plate (standard), K-wires, or Steinmann pins. Mild 10–20° dorsiflexion with neutral rotation — the optimal functional position. Results: complete pain relief, strong grip, no wrist motion (but compensatory radioulnar and metacarpal-radiocarpal pronation/supination).

**Total wrist arthroplasty.** For selected patients with low functional demands and a desire to preserve some motion. Newer implants (Universal 2, Maestro, Re-Motion) — 70–90% survivorship at 10 years. Contraindications: young active patient, poor bone quality, severe adhesive capsulitis.

</div>
</details>


### Wrist denervation
<details id="kienbock-denervation">
<summary>Wrist denervation</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

An alternative or adjunctive procedure for pain relief in patients in whom arthrodesis is undesirable or contraindicated. Wilhelm (Heidelberg, 1958, 1966) described complete wrist denervation by sectioning sensory branches of the radial, median, and ulnar nerves around the carpus. Buck-Gramcko (Hamburg, 1977) simplified the procedure to sectioning the interosseous branches of the anterior and posterior interosseous nerves (AIN + PIN) in the distal pre-radiocarpal region — usually enough for substantial pain relief with minimal sensory loss and no motor deficit. Berger (Mayo Clinic) popularized AIN + PIN denervation in the modern American school and advocates it as a first step before arthrodesis in selected patients.

Denervation does not change the natural course of the disease — radiographic progression continues — but it can defer salvage surgery by years in favorable cases. Diagnostic blockade of the two nerves before permanent section is recommended to predict response. *Green's* ch. 14 describes PIN excision (resection of 1–2 cm of the *posterior interosseous nerve* proximal to the distal radiocarpal joint) as a routine adjunct to total wrist arthrodesis and to joint-preserving procedures — preventing neuromatous pain from an inadvertently injured nerve in the field and relieving dorsal joint pain. Selective blockade of the dorsal and palmar carpal branches preoperatively identifies good candidates for denervation.

  <strong>NB</strong>

    <li>Lichtman and Bain (2016, ch. 30) present a twenty-first-century algorithm combining radiographic Lichtman stage, arthroscopic Bain chondral assessment, and gadolinium-enhanced MRI — procedure choice requires all three.</li>
    <li>Buck-Gramcko or Berger denervation (AIN + PIN) — an alternative when arthrodesis is undesirable; diagnostic anesthetic blockade is mandatory.</li>
    <li>Temporary STT pinning (Yajima, Yasuda) — a minimally invasive option in early disease before formal arthrodesis or osteotomy.</li>
  </ul>

## Other Adult AVN Sites
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="other-sites">
<details>
<summary>Other Adult AVN Sites</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Humeral head AVN.** The second most common site after the femoral head. Cruess classification (5 stages) analogous to Ficat. The vascular supply of the proximal humerus is predominantly through the arcuate artery (the terminal ascending branch of the anterior humeral circumflex artery), entering the head through the lateral aspect of the bicipital groove; disruption by displacement in proximal humerus fractures accounts for 20–50% AVN in four-part fractures. Early-stage treatment — core decompression with or without bone graft; advanced disease — anatomical or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty depending on rotator cuff status.

**Spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee (SONK; Ahlbäck's disease, 1968).** A focal lesion of the medial femoral condyle (less commonly the medial tibial plateau or lateral condyle) in women over 60 without trauma. Now understood as a subchondral insufficiency fracture in osteoporotic bone with secondary necrosis — not as a primary vascular event. MRI is diagnostic with a characteristic subchondral fracture line and surrounding marrow edema. Treatment: restricted weight-bearing, anti-inflammatories, bisphosphonates; for persistent pain — high tibial osteotomy or unicompartmental arthroplasty; for tricompartmental progression — total knee arthroplasty.

**Talar AVN.** Most commonly after a talar neck fracture, with risk rising with displacement per Hawkins: type I 0–10%, type II 20–50%, type III 80–100%, type IV near 100%. *Hawkins' sign* — a subchondral radiolucency in the talar dome on the AP view at 6–8 weeks after injury — is the classical favorable prognostic sign (preserved vascularity, expected viability). Non-traumatic talar AVN is uncommon (systemic risk factors). Treatment: early core decompression in selected cases; advanced collapse — ankle arthrodesis, in selected cases total ankle arthroplasty.

**Scaphoid AVN (Preiser's disease).** Idiopathic AVN of the proximal pole of the scaphoid, rare, in women aged 30–50. Risk factors: corticosteroids, alcohol, prior scaphoid fracture with microtrauma. Treatment: vascularized bone graft in early disease; with arthritis — scaphoidectomy with four-corner fusion, or PRC.

**Navicular AVN (Mueller–Weiss disease).** Idiopathic AVN of the tarsal navicular in adults — distinct from pediatric Köhler's disease. Middle-aged women with midfoot pain; conservative management early, selective tarsal arthrodesis when advanced.

**Freiberg's infraction.** AVN of the head of the second (rarely third) metatarsal, typically in adolescents and young women; adult manifestation is continuation of untreated juvenile disease. Late treatment — dorsiflexion osteotomy of the metatarsal head or excisional arthroplasty.

<aside class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>Note</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>SONK is a subchondral insufficiency fracture — not a vascular event. Expect it in women > 60 with sudden medial knee pain and no trauma; MRI shows the subchondral fracture line with marrow edema.</li>
    <li>Hawkins' sign (subchondral radiolucency in the talar dome at 6–8 weeks) is favorable — it indicates preserved vascularity.</li>
    <li>Humeral head AVN after four-part fracture: 20–50% — mandatory preoperative assessment of the medial periosteal ties and a decision between osteosynthesis and arthroplasty.</li>
  </ul>

## Local Contributions
<!-- toc-h2 -->
<section class="topic-section" id="bulgarian">
<details>
<summary>Local Contributions</summary>
<div class="ts-body">

**Boycho Boychev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup> (1908–1992).** Founder of the orthopedic school in Bulgaria; in the three-volume *Surgical Orthopaedics* (Sofia) Boychev presents the classical treatment of adult osteonecrosis, including femoral head AVN as a sequela of femoral neck fracture and hip dislocation, lunatomalacia as an industrial and occupational disease of manual labor, and the surgical tactics of the era (arthrodesis for pancarpal involvement, partial arthrodesis for selective involvement). The lunatomalacia chapter remains a reference for generations of Bulgarian orthopedic surgeons and preserves the lexical distinction between "softening" (*Erweichung*) and "osteomalacia" in Bulgarian terminology.

**Ilizarov <sup class="natflag">RU</sup> (1921–1992).** G. A. Ilizarov and his Kurgan school developed the methodology of transosseous distraction osteosynthesis, applied in selected complex cases of hip AVN as a salvage option — particularly in young patients in whom arthroplasty is to be deferred. Ilizarov-style distraction arthroplasty (external fixator with gradual joint distraction) preserves the joint space and stimulates cartilage regeneration; the foundational monograph *Transosseous Osteosynthesis: Theoretical and Clinical Aspects* (Springer, 1992) describes the principles and technique. The Ilizarov approach to knee and ankle AVN with a fixed distractor remains in the Bulgarian curriculum at the postgraduate level.

**Tanchev and Kaymakchiev <sup class="natflag">BG</sup>.** The Bulgarian orthopedic curriculum cites the works of P. Tanchev and G. Kaymakchiev in percutaneous diagnostics and operative technique for spinal and limb pathologies (see the spine surgical anatomy topic for their core techniques); in femoral head AVN their tactic follows the modern international standard (core decompression in pre-collapse, THA in post-collapse), with emphasis on radiographic planning and technical preparation.

**Boychev — lunatomalacia in workers.** In the Bulgarian school, lunatomalacia is also studied in the context of occupational disease in miners, drillers, and stonemasons — where repetitive axial loading and single dorsiflexion impacts are routine. Classification of work positions and industrial contraindications (avoidance of jackhammer and compressor work in early symptomatic disease) are part of the occupational-medicine tradition of Bulgarian orthopaedics.

From the classical Russian and Ukrainian schools of joint surgery, directly reflected in the Bulgarian curriculum:

**N. I. Khvisyuk, A. A. Korzh <sup class="natflag">UA</sup>.** The Kharkov school (the Tsivian Institute of Traumatology and Orthopaedics) developed the techniques of salvage in severe hip AVN, including arthrodesis and early arthroplasty, in the era before modern tribological bearings became widespread. The principles of patient selection and perioperative preparation remain didactically relevant.

**Ya. L. Tsivian <sup class="natflag">RU</sup>.** The Novosibirsk school of spinal and joint surgery — applied to complex post-arthrodesis correction after sequelae of hip AVN, especially in young patients with compensatory lumbar lordosis.

  <p>The terminology of "aseptic bone necrosis" became established in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Lexer (1904) systematizes the concept as a separate category, distinguishable from infectious osteomyelitides. Phemister (1930) describes the process of "creeping substitution" — revascularization of necrotic bone by gradual replacement. Chandler (1948) establishes "idiopathic" femoral head AVN as a distinct entity, later carrying his name in the older English literature.</p>

    <li><strong>Lexer 1904</strong> — *Lehrbuch der allgemeinen Chirurgie*, vol. 2 — aseptic bone necrosis (<a href="https://archive.org/details/lehrbuchderallg01lexegoog">Facsimile</a>).</li>
    <li><strong>Phemister 1930</strong> — *Repair of bone in the presence of aseptic necrosis*, *Clin Orthop* (reprinted 1992;277:4–11) — creeping substitution and cortical strut grafting.</li>

      if (!id) return;
      var el = document.getElementById(id);

      if (el.tagName === "DETAILS") el.open = true;

      if (inner) inner.open = true;
      var node = el.parentElement;

        if (node.tagName === "DETAILS") node.open = true;
        node = node.parentElement;
      }
      requestAnimationFrame(function () {

      });
    }
    function wire() {

        while (t && t !== document) {

            openByHash(t.getAttribute("href"));
            break;
          }
          t = t.parentNode;
        }
      });

    }
